Developer financing in The Gambia — instalment plans explained

MK MyKunda Updated 31 August 2026 22 min read

Paying a developer in instalments is a real route into new-build Gambian property, and for a diaspora buyer who cannot get a mortgage it is often the only route. It is also, as at 31 August 2026, an unregulated one: no Gambian statute governs off-plan sale, no body licenses developers, no rule requires your money to be held in escrow, and nothing protects your deposit if the developer fails. What protects you is the contract you sign — and one cheap step almost no buyer takes, which is registering that contract at the Deeds Registry. This guide sets out both, and is honest about which of the market's familiar percentages are published and which are not.

In short

  • The Gambia has no statutory regulation of off-plan sales, no developer licensing, no mandatory escrow and no deposit protection. A Real Estate Agents Bill was validated at a stakeholder workshop on 29 April 2026 but is not law, and it is a bill about agents, not about off-plan deposits.
  • The only published Gambian down-payment figure is CAHF's: developers and mortgage providers normally require 20% to 35% of the cost of the house. The reservation percentages, instalment periods and "0% interest" claims the market repeats are not published by anyone, and we no longer repeat them as typical.
  • The one real protection is statutory and cheap: a sale contract is an "instrument" registrable at the Deeds Registry under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, and priority runs from registration — with a twelve-month relation-back window for a contract executed outside The Gambia.
  • On a state lease, the Minister's written consent is needed for any assignment, and it "shall not be granted" where the land has not been developed. A clause in your contract cannot override a refusal.
  • An instalment plan trades interest-rate risk for counterparty risk. Building and construction carries an 18.7% non-performing loan ratio in Gambian banks, and Gambian property disputes have run over three years in court.

What developer financing is

Developer financing means the seller of the property, rather than a bank, gives you time to pay. You sign a contract, pay a deposit and then pay the balance in instalments, often tied to construction milestones. There is no bank, so there is no credit assessment and no mortgage to register.

That last point is usually presented as a benefit. It is worth pausing on, because it cuts the other way too. A mortgage creates a registered charge over the property: the lender's interest is on the record at the Deeds Registry and binds anyone who deals with the land afterwards. An unregistered instalment contract creates nothing on the record at all. For however long you are paying — and that may be years — the land stays in the developer's name, and to anyone searching the register you do not exist. Fixing that is the single most useful thing in this guide.

How common the arrangement is, we cannot tell you. No Gambian regulator, central bank release, industry body or research body publishes a breakdown of how diaspora or foreign buyers finance new-build property. What is fair to say is that instalment plans are widely advertised by Gambian developers to overseas buyers, and that bank finance is scarce enough to leave most buyers little else.

The payment structure — what is published and what is not

An earlier version of this guide set out a "typical" schedule: 5–10% to reserve, 20–30% on signing, the balance over three to five years. We have withdrawn it. We could not find a Gambian regulator, industry body, lender or legal source that publishes any typical off-plan payment structure, and a made-up range dressed up as market research is worse than an admission.

What we could not verify

No Gambian body publishes a typical off-plan payment structure, a typical instalment period, or what developers charge for credit. CAHF's 20% to 35% down payment is the only published figure we could find, and it covers mortgage providers and developers together rather than instalment plans specifically. Any percentage you are quoted is that developer's commercial offer, not a market standard — and there is no published standard to measure it against.

What the law does and does not protect

This is the part that decides whether the rest of the guide matters, so it comes before the comparisons.

There is no Gambian statute that regulates the off-plan sale of residential property, licenses developers, requires escrow or a trust account for buyers' instalments, or protects a buyer's deposit against developer default. Four independent sources support that, and none contradicts it:

  • CAHF's Gambia profile records that "the real estate sector is largely unregulated", and its 2024 yearbook chapter that "estate agents are not adequately regulated, thus exposing consumers to manipulation and price gouging by some";
  • Foroyaa, 28 December 2023: "The country lacks legislation to protect customers from real estate scamming" and "the land laws of the country do not cater for the real estate sector". The same report quotes Musa Janko Ceesay, president of the Association of Real Estate Companies: "Other countries regulate their real estate sector. We don't have anything like that";
  • The Point, 9 April 2021, reporting a study by the Gambia Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (GCCPC) which recommended that the sector "be assigned a regulator" — that is, one did not exist;
  • The Point, 4 May 2026, reporting the validation of a Real Estate Agents Bill at a national stakeholder workshop on 29 April 2026. Validation is not enactment. As at 31 August 2026 the Bill does not appear among the current bills on the National Assembly's tracker, its text is unpublished, and it is a bill about agents — nothing in the reporting suggests it would create off-plan escrow or deposit protection.

"No regulator at all" would nonetheless be too strong. Estate agents are reporting entities under section 41(b) of the AML/CFT Act 2012, supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit, which is why a developer or agent taking large diaspora payments should be asking you identity and source-of-funds questions. What does not exist is a licensing regulator, a professional register and any sector-specific consumer rulebook.

Do not plan on the Consumer Protection Act 2014

The obvious place to look for protection is consumer law. The position there is genuinely unresolved, and leaning the wrong way.

The Consumer Protection Act 2014 defines a "consumer" as a person supplied with goods or services in the course of a business, and defines "services" and "supply" — but it contains no definition of "goods" at all, and no reference anywhere to land, immovable property, real property or premises. It neither clearly includes nor clearly excludes the sale of a house.

The evidence on how it works in practice points both ways. The GCCPC has asserted jurisdiction: its 2021 sector study found violations under section 8(3) of the Act and recorded 13 real-estate complaints worth up to D3 million in the first half of 2020, the recurring complaint being buyers "unable to obtain their transfer documents or … not shown their properties". Against that, Foroyaa reported in December 2023 that the Act "does not cover real estate victims", and in May 2025 a consolidating Competition and Consumer Protection Bill was validated precisely to "address key existing gaps that has limited the powers and impact of the commission". That Bill is also still a bill.

The practical reading: it is worth complaining to the GCCPC (info@gcc.gm, +220 446 6940), and if the Act does apply it is useful — section 9 lets a tribunal vary, sever or strike unfair terms in a standard-form agreement and order restitution, and section 12(9) puts the burden of proving an advertising claim on the advertiser. But you should not sign an instalment contract on the assumption that consumer law will rescue it. Assume the contract, and the register, are all you have.

What we could not verify

Whether the Consumer Protection Act 2014 applies to a sale of land or a house is contested in Gambian reporting and unresolved on the text of the Act. We could also find no commencement notice for the Act published in the Gazette. Treat GCCPC complaint as a route worth trying, not as a right you can rely on.

Register the contract — the protection almost nobody uses

The Gambia has a deeds register, not a title register. It sits in the Deeds Registry of the Registrar General's Department, under the Attorney General's Chambers and Ministry of Justice, and it operates under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01. You will see the phrase "land registry" used loosely for it, but no office of that name exists under the Ministry of Lands: the Ministry of Lands issues leases and consents, and the Registrar General registers deeds. Our title verification guide explains what a search there does and does not tell you.

Three provisions matter to an instalment buyer:

  • Section 2 defines an "instrument" to extend to "any State grant, deed, contract, or power of attorney", and "land" to extend to "any estate or interest whatever in real property". Your sale contract is, on the face of the Act, a registrable instrument.
  • Section 7 provides that an instrument "shall take effect as against other instruments affecting the same land from the date of its registration" — with relation back to the date it was signed if it is registered within 7 days where it was executed in Banjul, 60 days where executed elsewhere in The Gambia, and 12 months where executed outside The Gambia.
  • Section 5(2): the register is indexed by the names of the parties, not by parcel. A search is a name search, which is why the developer's exact registered company name matters.

The consequence is worth stating baldly. If your contract is registered, a later deed or mortgage over the same land ranks behind it. If it is not, the developer can sell or mortgage the same land to someone else and that person's registered instrument comes first. That is not a theoretical risk in The Gambia. In August 2026 the Brusubi Magistrates' Court heard evidence from a buyer who said she had paid D650,000, D500,000 and D360,000 — D1.51 million — for three 20 m × 25 m plots at Salagi, and that "the three plots had been resold by the accused to different individuals", on documents alleged to be false (Foroyaa, 19 August 2026; charge under section 265 of the Criminal Offences Act 2025). Brikama magistrate Kanjura B. Sambou observed on 27 August 2026 that it is "increasingly common for a single plot of land to be sold to several individuals".

The twelve-month window is a gift to diaspora buyers specifically. If you sign the contract abroad, you have a full year in which registration relates back to signature. Ask your Gambian lawyer to do it as a matter of course, and get the receipt.

Two limits to be straight about:

  • Stamp duty comes first. Section 29 of the Stamp Act, Cap. 82:01 provides that no instrument may be registered unless duly stamped, and section 17 makes an unstamped instrument inadmissible in evidence. Budget for it and pay it.
  • Not all land can be registered. Only surveyed, mapped land with a unique identity can go on the deeds register; customary land and land held on deemed leasehold cannot be registered at all. That covers a large share of the plots on sale in the Kombos. If your developer's land is in that category, ask your lawyer what is actually possible, and treat the answer as part of the price.

Where registration of the contract is not possible, ask your lawyer about lodging a caveat: caveats and mortgages are among the things the register does record, so a caveat at least surfaces in a search. Stamp duty on a caveat is D500 under the 2019 stamp schedule.

Ministerial consent and the development permit

"Title transfers on final payment" is a sentence buyers ask for and developers happily sign. On a state lease — which is what most developed land in Greater Banjul and the Kombos is held on — it is not a promise the developer can unilaterally keep.

The State Lands Regulations 1995 (Legal Notice No. 13 of 1995) imply covenants into every state lease. Three of them decide instalment purchases:

  • Regulation 22(l): the lessee "shall not subdivide, convey, assign or otherwise alienate the premises or any part thereof by sale, transfer of possession, lease or sublease without the consent of the Minister in writing first obtained and such consent shall not be granted if the lessee has not developed the demised premises in accordance with the covenants in that behalf".
  • Regulation 22(k): no mortgage, charge or encumbrance without the Minister's written consent.
  • Regulation 22(g): no development without a development permit issued under the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990.

Two things follow. First, a contract clause cannot override a refusal. The Minister is not a party to your agreement. If consent is refused or delayed, your contractual right to a transfer converts into a claim for damages against the developer — which is worth exactly what the developer is worth. Any instalment contract over state land should therefore say who applies for consent, who pays the fee, by when the application must be made, what evidence of the application you are entitled to see, and precisely what happens to your money if consent is refused. The World Bank's Doing Business 2020 study (data collected May 2019) measured "application for ministerial consent to transfer" at 60 days and GMD 750 — the longest single step in a Gambian conveyance, and a 2019 measurement rather than a current fee.

Second, an undeveloped plot is the weakest thing you can buy on instalments. Consent to assign is expressly withheld where the build covenants have not been met, which is also why lenders are cautious about undeveloped land: their security cannot easily be realised. If you are buying a bare plot on a payment plan, understand that the ability to put it in your name may depend on building on it first.

Ask to see the development permit. It is a covenant in the lease, so its absence is a breach as well as a risk to your delivery date. Under section 30(2) of the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990 a development permit is valid for two years unless renewed — on a plan that runs longer than that, ask what the renewal position is. A permit does not confer ownership; the Minister for Lands said so publicly in November 2025. Enforcement is real: the Act allows enforcement notices, stop notices and, under section 38, removal of the development itself, and demolitions have been carried out. Our building guide covers the permit process in detail.

If the estate is on the coast, two further checks are worth making. A development involving "major structures within 150 metres of the high water mark" falls into the National Environment Agency's Class A and needs an environmental impact assessment. And all new land applications in the West Coast Tourism Development Area — the strip from Kotu to Kartong, 800 m inland from the high water mark — have been suspended since 23 January 2025 pending a Gambia Tourism Board audit, so a developer telling you it is "in the process of acquiring" TDA land is describing something currently on hold.

How it compares with a bank mortgage

The earlier version of this guide said developer financing "almost always" beats a mortgage, and quoted bank rates of 15–22%. Both need correcting: the low end of that range is unsupported, and the comparison is not one-directional.

No licensed Gambian bank publishes a mortgage rate sheet, tenor or loan-to-value, and the Central Bank publishes no average lending rate, so "roughly 19% to 22%" is the most that can be said with a source behind it. Note also that CAHF reports labour rules preventing a bank deducting more than a third of an employee's salary in mortgage repayment, and that the Social Security and Housing Finance Corporation's own mortgage page states that "non-Gambian nationals can purchase complete housing units only with an additional levy of 20% on the price". Islamic products exist too: AGIB Bank publishes real estate financing, Murabaha, Ijarah, Salam and Istisna. Our mortgage guide goes through the lenders in detail.

Set against that, the fair statement of the trade-off is this. An instalment plan removes interest-rate risk and adds counterparty risk. With a mortgage the property is yours from the start and the lender's interest is registered; if the builder fails, you still own the asset and you argue with the builder. With an instalment plan the developer is simultaneously building your property and financing it, so a single failure takes both. There is no regulator behind that risk, no deposit protection, and no charge over the asset unless you create one by registering your contract.

It is worth understanding why developers can offer what looks like free credit. With the policy rate at 14% and lending rates around 22%, your instalments are cheaper working capital than a bank loan would be. That alignment is genuine — and it is also the reason your money is funding construction rather than sitting anywhere safe.

The credit market's own view of that construction risk is published. In its February 2026 Monetary Policy Report the Central Bank put the banking system's non-performing loan ratio at 8.1%, with the concentration "highest in Distributive Trade (25.1%), Building & Construction (18.7 percent)". You are taking, unsecured and unregulated, a risk that Gambian banks price as among the highest in the economy.

Developer financing is not a cheaper mortgage. It is a different bargain: no interest, no credit check and no bank, in exchange for being an unsecured creditor of a construction company for several years.

Which developers offer plans, and what we can confirm about them

We name companies here only to be useful, and we separate what is independently confirmed from what a company says about itself. Nothing below is a recommendation, and MyKunda has no commercial relationship with any of them.

  • TAF Africa Global. Scale confirmed: the African Union for Housing Finance describes TAF as a pan-African real estate development company "with presence in 8 African countries and dominance in two — Nigeria and The Gambia", targeting one million homes between 2018 and 2038, and Foroyaa has reported contracts signed worth D570 million. Its financing terms are not independently sourced — the "up to five years, 20–30% down" that this guide previously printed had no source, and TAF's own marketing has advertised ownership "with just 25% down payment". Treat advertised terms as an offer to be negotiated and documented, not a published standard.
  • Blue Ocean. We described this company as a beachfront and resort developer with milestone-based, interest-free schedules. That was wrong. Blue Ocean Properties presents itself principally as an estate agency, describing itself as "a prime real estate company in Gambia, offering high-quality home ownership of properties". Its development arm lists one project — Heritage Community at Brufut, completed in 2023, from US$135,000, marketed with a "5 Year Payment Plan Available". The five-year plan is corroborated by the company's own site; "milestone-based" and "interest-free" are not stated anywhere we could find.
  • Global Properties. A genuine Gambian developer and agency. Its Gambian development list is quoted in euros, dollars and dalasi — Kololi Gardens from €88,750, Lake View Residence from €64,300, Aquaview from US$130,000 — which supports the hard-currency point. No instalment terms are published on its site.

The Dalaba Estate dispute, and the lesson in it

A guide that names TAF as an example and stops there would be leaving out the most instructive documented episode in the Gambian off-plan market.

In March 2025 The Fatu Network reported that residents of TAF's Dalaba Estate accused the company of unfulfilled promises on the perimeter fence, solar street lighting, bituminised roads (described as "less than 50% completed", replaced with paving slabs), security gates, a playground, 1,500 fruit trees and street signage. Buyers said they had spent D2–3 million each; one, Babucarr Kujabi, was quoted as saying: "I will be pleased with other homeowners to sue Taf. We will definitely sue him." In June 2025 The Standard reported that residents had formally petitioned the company, saying they bought "at a significantly higher market value" because of the promised amenities and "have been left with incomplete or non-existent infrastructure" — roads around 20% surfaced, street lighting on under 25% of the estate, no playground and no shopping centre.

TAF's chief executive, Mustapha Njie, replied that "purchasing involves contractual agreements, not promises."

Whatever one makes of that as customer service, as legal advice to buyers it is accurate, and it is the lesson: anything you were shown in a brochure, a video, a scale model or a site visit is worth nothing unless it is a term of the written contract, with a delivery date and a remedy attached. Most off-plan contract checklists cover the unit — price, schedule, specification, completion date. Almost none cover the estate. On the documented Gambian evidence, the estate is where buyers get hurt.

So schedule the estate. In the contract, list the roads, the surfacing standard and the length; the street lighting and how many units; the perimeter fence, gates and security arrangements; drainage; water and electricity connection to the plot boundary; the playground, the shopping centre, the landscaping, the tree planting. Against each: a completion date, and what happens if it is missed — a stated sum per month of delay, a right to withhold a defined slice of the final instalment, or a retention held by your lawyer until delivery. A promise with no date and no consequence is a promise, and TAF's chief executive is right that it is not a contractual agreement.

What to check, and what to put in the contract

Before you sign

  • Verify the land, not the brochure. Get sight of the actual document the developer holds — a lease, a deed of conveyance, a certificate of occupancy, an alkalo transfer — and have your own lawyer search the Deeds Registry against the developer's exact registered name. Remember the register is a name index, and that customary and deemed-leasehold land is not on it at all. Our title guide and tenure guide cover what each document does and does not prove.
  • Be suspicious of a new "freehold". New freeholds have not been creatable in The Gambia since 1945 without the consent of the legislature, and surviving grants are essentially Banjul-only. A coastal plot marketed as freehold is a warning, not a bonus.
  • Ask for the development permit, and check it has not expired (two years, unless renewed).
  • Ask where ministerial consent stands for the subdivision and for the assignment to you.
  • Check the company. Gambian companies are registered under the Companies Act 2013 and the Single Window Business Registry Act 2013 at the Registrar General's office in the Ministry of Justice. Get the exact registered name and confirm it matches the name on the land documents and on the contract.
  • Visit completed work and find owners yourself. Not only the ones the developer introduces you to. Ask them specifically about the estate amenities and about how long transfer took after final payment.

In the contract

  • Your own lawyer drafts or reviews it — not the developer's, and not one the developer recommends. This is the least negotiable item on the page.
  • Total price, in a stated currency, with the exchange rate mechanism spelled out if payments are made in another currency.
  • The full payment schedule, with dates and amounts, and what each payment is for.
  • The specification of the unit — and, separately, the estate schedule described above.
  • Registration. An express term that the contract is to be registered at the Deeds Registry, who pays the stamp duty, and by when — inside the 12-month window if you sign abroad.
  • Ministerial consent. Who applies, who pays, by when, what you get to see, and what happens to your money if consent is refused.
  • Delay. What your rights are at six months, a year, two years. Liquidated sums are easier to enforce than an argument about loss.
  • Your own default — see the section below. Negotiate this while you are the one with money the developer wants.
  • Where the money goes. Payment into your lawyer's client account, released against verified milestones, is the arrangement to insist on.

On escrow, plainly: no Gambian statute requires escrow for an off-plan or instalment sale, no rule creates a developer trust account, and no client-account rule for agents exists. Where you see a general statement on this site or elsewhere that purchase money "is paid into the escrow account of your lawyer or notary", read that as what you should insist on and pay for, not as a protection the law gives you. If a developer will not accept staged release through a law firm, that is information about the developer.

While you are paying

  • Visit, or have someone you trust visit, and photograph progress against the schedule.
  • Keep every receipt, transfer confirmation and message. Pay by bank transfer, traceably, into a company account in the company's registered name — never into a personal account.
  • Watch the ground rent and the local rate. Annual ground rent on a state lease is payable to the Department of Lands before 31 January, with re-entry for non-payment, and Area Councils levy an annual rate on developed property under the General Rate Act 1992. Ask, in writing, who is paying these while the land is still in the developer's name.
  • Silence is the warning sign. So is a request to move a payment forward.

If you cannot keep paying

This is the question buyers ask least and need most, because an instalment plan is the one property arrangement where your own circumstances, not the seller's, can cost you everything.

There is no Gambian statutory answer. No statute regulates forfeiture of instalments paid on an off-plan sale, no rule requires a refund of any part of them, and we could find no reported Gambian decision setting a limit on what a developer may keep. Whatever your contract says is, in practice, the rule — and if it says nothing, you are arguing from scratch, in a system where property disputes have run over three years.

So negotiate it before you sign, and get it in writing:

  • a grace period for a late instalment, and a cure period after formal written notice before any default is declared;
  • notice in writing to a named address, not a phone call or a message;
  • what is refunded if the contract ends — as a formula, for example instalments paid less a stated percentage of the price and less documented costs, payable within a stated number of days;
  • a right to sell your position to another buyer, with consent not to be unreasonably withheld, which is often worth more than a refund;
  • whether arrears carry interest, and at what rate;
  • what happens to a registered contract: if you have registered, your interest has to be discharged, and that gives you real negotiating leverage at exactly the moment you need it.

Where the failure is the developer's rather than yours, be equally clear-eyed. Your remedies are ordinary contract remedies in the ordinary courts. Foroyaa reported in December 2023 that some Gambian real estate cases exceed three years without resolution, and that "the overwhelming majority of the real estate companies in the country are facing legal disputes with their customers in court". Congestion is part of the picture: the Minister for Lands said on 21 May 2026 that 67% of court cases are land-related, while a validation workshop report in May 2026 put it at around 75%.

What we could not verify

We could find no Gambian authority on whether a forfeiture clause in an instalment contract can be reduced or set aside, and none on how a buyer's part-paid instalments rank if the developer becomes insolvent. Absent a registered interest or money held by a law firm, a buyer part-way through a plan appears to be simply someone the company owes money to — but that is our reading, not a published Gambian rule. Ask a Gambian lawyer to advise on both points before you sign.

Private seller and small-builder plans

Outside the estates, individual owners and small builders also sell on terms. This guide previously said those plans "typically" involve a 40–60% deposit with the balance over 12 to 36 months. That figure had no source at all and has been removed; there is no published Gambian evidence of what private instalment terms look like.

What can be said is that the risk profile is different and, in one respect, worse. There is no company to sue, no registry filing to check, no completed estate to visit — and the most-documented Gambian land fraud pattern, the same plot sold to several buyers, is a private-seller pattern. The Salagi prosecution described above involved exactly that. So:

  • the agreement must be drawn by your own lawyer, and it must be registered — the Cap. 57:01 priority point matters more here than anywhere;
  • confirm what the seller actually holds and whether it can be transferred at all: a deemed leaseholder or a customary occupier may need to convert to a formal lease first, and land in the Regions is subject to District Authority consent, with alienation without the required consent void under section 18(1) of the Lands (Regions) Act;
  • money should move through a law firm's client account against defined steps, not hand to hand;
  • check for outstanding ground rent and Area Council rates before you take anything on;
  • agree the schedule, any escalation and any penalty in the document, and never on the basis that you will "sort it out later".

What the plan does not include

An instalment schedule covers the price. It does not cover the transaction, and the transaction costs are not trivial.

Currency risk, quantified

A long plan denominated in one currency while you earn another is a multi-year currency position, whether or not you think of it that way. The Central Bank has published the size of the recent move: in its February 2026 Monetary Policy Report it recorded the dalasi depreciating over the preceding year by 11.53% against the euro, 4.36% against the US dollar, 6.33% against sterling and 11.93% against the CFA franc.

Applied to a plan, that cuts two ways. A balance fixed in dalasi would have cost a euro earner roughly 11.5% less in euros over that year. A balance fixed in euros would have cost a dalasi earner roughly 13% more in dalasi. Neither is a forecast — it is one year, in one direction, and the exchange regime has been free-floating since 1986. But it shows that on a four-year balance the currency clause is not a detail; it can be worth more than the entire difference between a good developer's terms and a poor one's.

Practical points: ask whether the developer will fix the total in hard currency in the contract; if the price is in dalasi, ask what rate and what source converts your payments, and pin it to a named published rate rather than "the prevailing rate"; and remember that money transfer operators may not buy and sell foreign exchange in the domestic market, so payments must move through a licensed commercial bank. Our money transfer guide covers the cost of getting funds there — the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide database put the UK-to-Gambia corridor at a mean of 12.06% at £120 and 10.90% at £300 in the third quarter of 2025, so on instalment-sized transfers the fee structure matters. The live rates in this site's header come from the Central Bank's own feed; use those rather than any figure typed into an article.

If you are not Gambian

Two points that a multi-year commitment makes more, not less, urgent.

The State Lands Regulations 1995, Schedule II, paragraph 2, says in terms: "A non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land." That schedule governs government allocation of state plots in approved layout areas. It does not, on its face, stop a non-Gambian taking an assignment of an existing lease from a private holder — but every such assignment needs the Minister's written consent under regulation 22(l), and consent is withheld on undeveloped land. So the anyone-can-buy framing you will meet in marketing is wrong, and the real constraint is the consent regime. Our guide for foreign buyers goes into it properly.

What we could not verify

Some sources, including the US State Department's 2018 Investment Climate Statement, report that non-Gambians may hold residential land only for up to 21 years, only outside 1.5 km of the high water mark, and only up to 2,500 m²; a Gambian law firm repeats it. We have searched the full text of the State Lands Regulations 1995 and found no such provision, and we could locate no Gambian statute or gazette carrying it. We therefore do not state it as law — but because on a coastal purchase it would matter enormously, and because an instalment plan locks you in for years before title moves, any non-Gambian buyer should have a Gambian lawyer confirm the position in writing before committing.

Separately, buying property gives you no right to live in The Gambia. There is no golden visa and no retirement visa; residence is a separate permit question covered in our residency guide.

If you are the developer or the seller offering the plan

The unregulated market cuts both ways: nothing licenses you, and equally nothing shields you.

  • You need no licence to sell off-plan or on instalments. As at 31 August 2026 no developer licensing regime exists and the Real Estate Agents Bill is unenacted. That is a fact about the law, not an endorsement — and buyers who have read this guide will be asking you for documents rather than reassurance.
  • Perfect the land documents first. Before you take a shilling: the lease or conveyance in the selling entity's exact registered name; the survey plan; the approved layout and subdivision; the development permit under the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990, in date; ground rent paid to 31 January; Area Council rates clear. A buyer's lawyer will find any gap, and a gap found after deposits are taken is a refund conversation.
  • You cannot deal with the land freely while buyers are paying. Regulations 22(k) and 22(l) require the Minister's written consent to mortgage or to assign, and a buyer who has registered their contract under Cap. 57:01 takes priority over any later instrument. Plan your development finance around that rather than discovering it at drawdown.
  • Start the consent application early. Ministerial consent was measured at 60 days in 2019, and consent to assign is refused where the land is undeveloped. If your contracts promise transfer on final payment, your build programme has to reach the covenanted standard before that promise can be kept.
  • Assume your standard form and your adverts can be tested. If the Consumer Protection Act 2014 applies to land — contested, see above — section 9 lets a tribunal vary, sever or strike unfair terms in a standard-form agreement and order restitution or reduce the sum payable, section 12(9) puts the burden of proving an advertising claim on the advertiser, and section 31 allows a refund of the contract price. Draft forfeiture clauses on the assumption they may one day be read by someone unsympathetic.
  • Put the estate schedule in the contract yourself. The Dalaba Estate petition is what happens when amenities live in the marketing and not in the agreement. Dated, costed obligations you can actually meet protect you as much as the buyer; open-ended promises in a brochure protect nobody.
  • Tax. On disposal, companies, partnerships and trustees pay capital gains at the higher of 25% of the gain or 10% of the consideration; individuals the higher of 15% or 5%. GRA sets a 15-day return deadline from the transaction. Corporation tax is the higher of 27% of net profit or 1% of turnover (2% where accounts are unaudited). VAT registration is compulsory at D2,000,000 of taxable supplies and the rate is 15%; whether the sale of a completed dwelling is a taxable supply is not something GRA publishes, so confirm it rather than assuming.
  • Withholding on your contractors. GRA's withholding brochure and the 2025 Budget Speech give 10% for non-resident contractors, 5% for resident contractors on government public works and 8% for other resident suppliers, consultants and contractors — while GRA's own domestic FAQ page still shows 10% resident and 15% non-resident. Confirm the applicable rate with GRA in writing before you contract.
  • Anti-money-laundering. Estate agents are reporting entities under section 41(b) of the AML/CFT Act 2012, supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit. Expect identification for occasional customers above D200,000, and purpose, origin and destination for cash transactions above US$10,000. Taking large diaspora payments into a personal account is the fastest way to make a legitimate sale look like something else.
  • You cannot register the buyer's deed until the duty is paid (Stamp Act s.29) and, on a state lease, until consent is obtained. Say so in the contract, with dates, rather than letting the buyer conclude you are stalling.
What we could not verify

We could find no source establishing whether offering vendor credit — letting a buyer pay over years — brings a Gambian developer within any Central Bank licensing or credit-regulation regime. We could also find no Gambian authority on the enforceability of a forfeiture clause against a defaulting buyer. Both should be put to Gambian counsel rather than assumed either way.

Questions people actually ask

Is developer financing regulated in The Gambia?

No. As at 31 August 2026 no Gambian statute regulates off-plan sales, licenses developers, requires escrow or protects buyer deposits. A Real Estate Agents Bill was validated at a stakeholder workshop on 29 April 2026 but has not been introduced or passed, and it concerns agents rather than off-plan deposits.

Is my deposit protected if the developer goes under?

Not by law. There is no deposit protection scheme, no mandatory escrow and no developer trust account. The only protections are ones you create: money held by a law firm and released against verified milestones, and a sale contract registered at the Deeds Registry.

Can I get anything on the register before I have title?

Yes, and this is the most useful thing in this guide. Under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01, an "instrument" extends to a contract, and section 7 gives priority from the date of registration, relating back to signature if registered within 7 days (executed in Banjul), 60 days (elsewhere in The Gambia) or 12 months (executed outside The Gambia). Stamp duty must be paid first. Note that customary land and land held on deemed leasehold cannot be registered at all.

Will title definitely transfer when I finish paying?

Not automatically on state land. Regulation 22(l) of the State Lands Regulations 1995 requires the Minister's written consent to any assignment, and provides that consent "shall not be granted if the lessee has not developed the demised premises". A clause in your contract cannot override a refusal, so the contract should say who applies for consent, by when, and what happens to your money if it is refused.

How do I check the development has planning permission?

Ask to see the development permit issued under the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990. Holding one is a covenant in every state lease under regulation 22(g). A permit is valid for two years unless renewed, so check the date, and remember a permit does not prove ownership.

Are the amenities in the brochure enforceable?

Only if they are terms of the contract. When Dalaba Estate residents petitioned TAF in 2025 over undelivered roads, lighting, a playground and a shopping centre, the company's chief executive replied that "purchasing involves contractual agreements, not promises". Schedule every estate-level item with a delivery date and a remedy — a sum per month of delay, or a retention held by your lawyer.

What happens if I miss instalments?

Whatever your contract says. No Gambian statute regulates forfeiture on an instalment sale and we found no reported decision limiting what a developer may keep. Negotiate a grace period, a written cure notice, a refund formula and a right to sell your position on, before you sign.

How long would a court case take?

Long. Foroyaa reported in December 2023 that some Gambian real estate cases exceed three years without resolution and that most real estate companies are in litigation with customers. The Minister for Lands put land-related matters at 67% of court cases in May 2026, and a validation workshop report at around 75%. Litigation is not a plan; the contract and the register are.

Should I agree the price in dalasi or in euros?

Whichever currency you earn in removes the risk for you, and the developer knows that too. The Central Bank recorded the dalasi falling 11.53% against the euro and 4.36% against the dollar in the year to February 2026, so on a four-year balance this is a material term, not a formality. If the price is in dalasi, fix the conversion to a named published rate.

Could I get a mortgage instead, or refinance later?

Rarely. CAHF records about two mortgage providers and roughly 7,000 residential mortgages nationally as at 2021, a typical rate of 19.5%, a maximum term of 20 years and deposits of 20% to 35%. The World Bank and IMF put the average lending rate at 22% for 2024 and 2025. No Gambian bank publishes a rate sheet.

What do I pay on top of the price?

Stamp duty at 2% of the amount on the assignment, primarily payable by the buyer, and it must be paid before the deed can be registered. Then legal fees (no reliable benchmark exists; VAT at 15% applies if your supplier is VAT-registered), survey and registration. The "5%" often quoted is usually the seller's capital gains tax floor, and because it must be paid before registration it becomes your completion risk too.

As a non-Gambian, can I hold this land at all?

Schedule II paragraph 2 of the State Lands Regulations 1995 says "A non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land", but that governs government allocation rather than a private assignment. Every assignment still needs the Minister's consent. Reports of a 21-year, 1.5 km, 2,500 m² restriction on foreigners could not be traced to any Gambian statute, so get written advice from a Gambian lawyer before committing to a multi-year plan.

Can I buy a bare plot on instalments and build later?

You can be sold one, but it is the weakest version of this arrangement. Consent to assign a state lease is expressly refused where the land has not been developed, so putting the plot in your own name may depend on building on it first — and a lender's security over undeveloped land is weak for the same reason.

I am the seller — do I need a licence to offer a payment plan?

No licensing regime exists for developers as at 31 August 2026. Whether offering vendor credit brings you within any Central Bank licensing regime is not something we could establish from any published source, so take Gambian legal advice rather than assuming either way.

Key conditions — no matter how you finance

  • Your own lawyer, always. Independent of the developer, the agent and anyone who introduced you.
  • Establish what the seller actually holds before money moves — see our title verification guide.
  • Register the contract, and pay the stamp duty that makes registration possible.
  • Deal with ministerial consent as a term, not an afterthought.
  • Schedule the estate, with dates and remedies, not only the unit.
  • Budget the transaction costs on top of the price — see our costs guide.
  • Fix the currency, or accept that you are running a multi-year position.
  • Agree what happens when things go wrong — their delay and your default — while you still have leverage.

MyKunda's position: a developer instalment plan can be a sound way to buy, and for many diaspora buyers it is the only practical one. But it is an unregulated private credit arrangement with a construction company, not a consumer product with a safety net. Everything that protects you is something you and your lawyer put in place before you pay: the contract, the registration, the consent route, and where the money sits in the meantime.
