Inheritance and wills for Gambian property

MK MyKunda Updated 31 August 2026 18 min read

The hardest property problems in The Gambia are not sales — they are estates. A house bought in one country and inherited under the law of another, with heirs on three continents and a deed in a drawer, is how a family asset becomes a family dispute. This guide sets out which law applies to a Gambian property when someone dies, who then has authority to sell it, what tax bites and when, and what a buyer should establish before going anywhere near inherited or family land.

In short

  • Three systems can govern a Gambian estate — statutory law through the High Court, Sharia through the Cadi Courts, and customary law through District Tribunals. The 1997 Constitution recognises all three.
  • The High Court administers an estate and issues the grant. A Cadi Court fixes the shares where the interested persons are Muslims. There is no separate "probate registry" in The Gambia.
  • If no grant of probate or letters of administration is obtained within six months of the death, the Curator of Intestate Estates may take the estate over under the Intestate Estates Act, Cap. 14:02, and administer it through the High Court.
  • Sharia shares are fixed, not discretionary: in reported Gambian cases the wife took one-eighth and a male heir took double the share of a female heir.
  • A property that cannot be divided without harm must be sold, and a dissenting heir can be compelled to accept that — the Cadi Appeals Panel has said so in terms.
  • Sharia inheritance rules reach only land that has been individualised. They do not reach family land — which is why some "inherited" plots cannot lawfully be sold to you at all.
  • There is no inheritance, estate or death duty in The Gambia. Capital gains tax applies when heirs sell.
  • There is no Gambian forced-heirship or family-provision statute. The Women's Act 2010 protections for a widow are expressly "subject to personal law".

Three systems, one country

The Gambian legal system is genuinely tripartite: English common law and equity together with statute, customary law applied by District Tribunals, and Sharia administered by the Cadi Courts. Section 7 of the Constitution of the Republic of The Gambia, 1997 is explicit that the laws of The Gambia include customary law and "the Sharia as regards matters of marriage, divorce and inheritance among members of the communities to which it applies".

SystemApplies toForum, and where an appeal goes
Statutory lawAdministration of the estate — collecting in, paying debts, transferring or selling assets, under a will or on intestacyHigh Court. Grants of probate and letters of administration, and administration orders in favour of the Curator of Intestate Estates, issue from the High Court
ShariaEntitlement — who inherits and in what shares — where the parties or other persons interested are MuslimsCadi Court, appeal to the Cadi Appeals Panel (Constitution s.137A)
Customary lawMembers of the communities to which it applies, particularly land held under customary tenure in the RegionsDistrict Tribunal — presided over by the Seyfo with five members — with appeal to the High Court, and to the Cadi Appeals Panel where Sharia law is involved (s.137A(6))

The two upper rows are complementary rather than rival. In a Muslim estate the Cadi Court decides what each heir is entitled to; the High Court is where the estate is actually administered and where the piece of paper that lets anyone deal with the house comes from.

Appeals do not go to a governor. Under the Lands (Regions) Act, Cap. 57:03, a Governor's role in land matters is confined to disputes over the value of fixtures and economic trees, with a right of appeal to the Minister whose decision is final. That is compensation valuation. It is not review of a tribunal's decision on who inherits, and no Gambian source supports sending an inheritance appeal to a regional governor.

Which system governs your estate is not something you pick casually at the end. It follows from who you and your heirs are, from what the property legally is, and — crucially — from what documents exist.

The Cadi Court route

Section 137 of the Constitution establishes Cadi Courts, composed at first instance of a panel of the Cadi and two other scholars of the Sharia qualified to be a Cadi or Ulama. The jurisdiction is drawn narrowly: the Cadi Court "shall only have jurisdiction to apply the Sharia in matters of marriage, divorce and inheritance where the parties or other persons interested are Muslims". Both conditions have to hold — the subject matter must be one of those three, and the interested persons must be Muslims. Appeals go to the Cadi Appeals Panel, which also hears appeals from District Tribunals where Sharia law is involved.

The Mohammedan Law Recognition Act, Cap. 6:04, has never been expressly repealed. Section 7 of it provides that the Mohammedan Courts — now the Cadi Courts and the Cadi Appeals Panel — may entertain causes and matters relating to civil status, marriage, succession, donations, testaments and guardianship. That is a grant of jurisdiction to the Cadi side of the system, not to the general courts. It matters for two reasons: it puts succession squarely within the Cadi Courts' remit, and by naming "testaments" it confirms that a Muslim testamentary instrument is contemplated by Gambian law.

The Muslim court itself is old. A Muslim court was constituted in Bathurst in 1905, under the Mohammedan Law Recognition Ordinance No. 10 of that year, with jurisdiction over civil status, marriage, succession, donation, testament and guardianship where both parties were Muslims. The Cadi Court you would go to today is a creature of the 1997 Constitution; the 1905 body is its ancestor, not the same institution.

What the reported cases actually decide

Under Sharia the shares are fixed rather than discretionary, and there is real Gambian authority for that — the Judiciary's own Gambia Sharia Law Report. In Musa Sabily v Hassan Baye (AP/14/2005, decided 2007) the Cadi Appeals Panel upheld a distribution in which the wife took one-eighth and a son and daughter shared the residue, "a male carries the double share of a female". The same pattern appears in Aji Tide Ceesay v Modu Njie (AP/31/2005, decided 2010).

Two further holdings from those reports have direct commercial consequences, and both are dealt with below: property that can be partitioned without harm must be partitioned rather than settled in money, and an heir has no right to dispose of estate property by sale, gift or mortgage without the Panel's approval.

What we could not verify

Whether a Muslim family may choose the general courts instead of the Cadi Court. An earlier version of this guide said that using a Sharia court is a matter of choice, not compulsion. We cannot source that. A University of Pennsylvania Law School access-to-justice report describes the Cadi Courts as exercising personal jurisdiction over Muslims in inheritance, divorce and marriage, and says their decisions "are not reviewed by any other courts"; section 33(5)(c) of the Constitution removes devolution of property on death and other matters of personal law from the non-discrimination guarantee. Treat the point as contested and ask a Gambian lawyer, not as a free election. We also could not find any Gambian statute, rule or reported decision imposing the classical one-third limit on a bequest (wasiyya) out of a Muslim's estate, so we do not state it as Gambian law — but a Muslim owner should assume a will's reach over the estate may be much narrower than expected and take advice before relying on it. Nor could we source the claim, made in an earlier version of this guide, that the Gambian Cadi system is one of only a handful of its kind in the world.

Wills, grants and the High Court

Under the general law a person may make a will directing how their property is to be distributed. What The Gambia does not have is a forced-heirship or family-provision statute of the kind found in many civil-law countries and in England's Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act. We could locate no Gambian equivalent. An earlier version of this guide said forced-heirship principles protect spouses and children; that was wrong, and the real position is weaker.

What exists instead is the Women's Act 2010. Section 44 gives a widow the right to an equitable share in the inheritance of her husband's property and the right to continue to live in the matrimonial home; section 45 gives a right to inherit in equitable shares. Both are expressly stated to be "subject to personal law". Section 33(5)(c) of the Constitution then exempts devolution of property on death and other matters of personal law from the constitutional guarantee against discrimination. The practical effect is that the personal law — for most Gambian families, Sharia — determines the shares, and the Women's Act does not override it. The World Bank's Women, Business and the Law assessment records The Gambia as "No" both on whether sons and daughters have equal rights to inherit and on whether surviving male and female spouses do.

The grant is the thing that unlocks the house

Where there is a will, the executor applies for a grant of probate. Where there is no will, or no executor able to act, the court appoints an administrator and issues letters of administration. Either way the grant issues from the High Court. The Gambia has no separate "Probate Registry" — an earlier version of this guide named one, and no reliable Gambian source supports it.

Until that grant issues, nobody can safely sell, transfer or mortgage the house, and a buyer has no one with proven authority to sign. That is why estates freeze. Gambian property litigation runs long — Foroyaa has reported property cases taking over three years — so a frozen estate can stay frozen for a very long time.

One cheap protection that is almost never used: a will is a registrable instrument. The Registrar General's Department registers deeds, conveyances, wills, contracts and other instruments affecting land under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01. Registering the will alongside the deed puts it in legal custody and produces an extract admissible in court. See how to verify land title in The Gambia for what the Deeds Registry does and does not tell you.

What we could not verify

Two things about wills. First, what makes a will formally valid in The Gambia: we found no Gambian Wills Act. The Law of England (Application) Act carries the common law, the doctrines of equity and English statutes of general application in force on 1 November 1888 into Gambian law, which would bring in the English Wills Act 1837 — but that is our inference from the reception statute, not a sourced statement of Gambian law, so have a Gambian lawyer settle the execution formalities. Second, whether a foreign will is recognised: an earlier version of this guide said a foreign will "can often be recognised". We could find no Gambian resealing statute, no Gambian conflict-of-laws authority on succession to immovable property and no official Judiciary or Ministry of Justice page on foreign grants. It may well work in practice. We cannot tell you it is the law, which is itself the argument for making a separate Gambian will for the Gambian property.

When nobody applies: the Curator of Intestate Estates

This is the most useful thing in Gambian estate practice and it is almost never explained. Where a person dies intestate, the distribution of the estate is governed by the Intestate Estates Act, Cap. 14:02, and the Curator of Intestate Estates, a department of the Ministry of Justice, is responsible for the collection, management and administration of intestate estates.

The Curator's mandate arises in four situations: the deceased died intestate; a testator appointed no executor; the named executor is unable or unwilling to act; or probate or letters of administration were not obtained within six months of the death. That six-month trigger is the only hard, checkable deadline in the whole subject.

StepWhat happens
1. DeclarationBeneficiaries file a Declaration Form (Form D) with the Curator, supported by the death certificate, the children's birth certificates and the marriage certificate or an affidavit
2. Gazette noticeThe Curator publishes notice of the estate in the Gazette for a period of fourteen days — a public record anyone, including a prospective buyer, can look for
3. Court orderThe Curator applies to the High Court for an order authorising her or him to administer the estate
4. AdministrationAssets are collected in, funeral expenses and debts are paid, and the balance is distributed to those entitled

Two consequences follow. For a family: if you do nothing for six months, control of your relative's house may pass to a government department, and you will be dealing with the Curator rather than with each other. For a buyer: if more than six months have passed since the death and no grant exists, the Curator may be the proper party to deal with, and no heir's signature will do instead.

There is a narrow but relevant sub-rule for expatriate families: where a non-ECOWAS national dies in Gambian public service, the Curator's role is limited to realising the assets, paying funeral costs and debts, and remitting the balance to the legal representatives abroad.

Family land and individualised land — the distinction that decides everything

Before any question of shares arises, one prior question decides whether there is an estate asset at all. CIFOR-ICRAF's guide to land rights in The Gambia puts it plainly: "the Sharia rules of inheritance only apply to land that has been individualized and not to family land."

Family land held by a lineage is not owned by the deceased in the sense that lets it be divided among heirs. No share calculation, however carefully done, will produce a seller with clean title to it. A person offering you "my father's land" may in truth be offering you a lineage holding that he has no power to sell, and no Cadi Court distribution order will cure that. This is the difference between a plot that is buyable and one that is not.

Customary tenure compounds it. Devolution in rural areas is generally through the male lineage for most productive land, and a widow may find herself under pressure to remarry within the late husband's family. Disputes go first to the District Tribunal and on appeal to the High Court. Meanwhile, land under customary tenure and land held on deemed leasehold cannot be registered at the Deeds Registry at all, so the "central check" a diaspora buyer imagines returns nothing for a large share of the plots on offer in Kombo.

The single most effective protection is to have converted the holding into a properly documented, individualised title in the owner's own name long before it is ever an estate. See freehold, leasehold and customary land explained.

What actually proves the heirs may sell

There is no single universal document, and this is where most inherited-property deals go wrong. The honest answer is layered, and a buyer needs the whole stack, not one item from it.

DocumentWhat it provesWhere it comes from

Read the stack in that order. The grant proves authority to transact. The Cadi order proves who owns what. The valuation and the heirs' consents are what stop the deal being reopened. The Minister's consent is what makes an assignment of a State lease effective at all — and on an undeveloped plot it may simply not be available, which is a problem the estate cannot solve for you.

One house, several heirs

This is the commonest real situation: a single villa, five heirs, no agreement. Gambian authority answers it, and the answer is more decisive than most families expect.

Any property subject to inheritance which could be partitioned without any harm for purposes of distribution to the inheritors must be partitioned for that purpose. — Aji Tide Ceesay v Modu Njie, Cadi Appeals Panel, AP/31/2005 (2010)

So monetary compensation does not apply where the property can in fact be partitioned and distributed among the heirs. And where it cannot:

What is divisible, land or estate should be divided if no harm ensues but what cannot be divided without causing harm, then it must be sold and the opposing party must be forced to accept it. — Aji Hauwa Sillah v Aji Fanta Drammeh, Cadi Appeals Panel, AP/06/2006 (2009)

In plain terms: a house that cannot be split without damaging it must be sold and the proceeds divided, and an heir who refuses can be compelled to accept the sale. A single heir cannot hold the whole family hostage indefinitely — but reaching that outcome means litigation, valuation and time, which is why deciding it in advance, in writing, is worth far more than winning it later.

Tax: nothing on inheriting, something on selling

There is no inheritance tax, estate duty, death duty or gift tax in The Gambia. That is consistent negative evidence across five independent listings — the Gambia Revenue Authority's own domestic taxes FAQ, which enumerates eleven tax types and includes no death or gift tax; the GRA capital gains tax brochure, whose exemption categories include none for death or inheritance; PwC's Gambia guide; BRITACOM; and Andersen's country tear sheet. It is stated as an absence, not as a statutory exemption. Inheriting is not a taxable event.

Selling is. Capital gains tax applies to the disposal of a capital asset, expressly including land, at GRA's published rates:

SellerCapital gains tax
IndividualsThe higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration
Companies, partnerships and trustees — including the trustee of a deceased estateThe higher of 25% of the gain or 10% of the consideration

The seller pays it, and it is payable before the deed can be registered, with a GRA return deadline of 15 days from the transaction. That makes it a completion risk for the buyer as well as a cost for the estate: if the estate cannot fund the tax, the transfer does not complete. A D24,000 exemption and private-residence and agricultural reinvestment reliefs exist; whether any of them reaches an estate sale depends on facts we cannot generalise about.

On the instruments themselves, under the Stamp Act (Amendment of Schedule) Order, 2019 (Legal Notice No. 18 of 2019, Gazette No. 34 of 4 October 2019): a Deed of Assent — the instrument by which a personal representative vests property in an heir — carries a flat D500, as does a Vesting Deed and a Deed of Gift; an assignment or conveyance on a sale carries 2% of the amount, payable by the buyer. Section 29 of the Stamp Act means no deed may be registered unless duly stamped, so this is not optional. The 5% figure people are quoted as "transfer tax" is almost always the seller's capital gains tax floor, not duty. There is more on this in the real cost of buying property in The Gambia.

Running costs do not stop because the owner died. An annual ground rent is payable on a State lease to the Department of Lands before 31 January, with forfeiture by re-entry for non-payment, and Area Councils levy an annual rate on developed property under the General Rate Act 1992.

What we could not verify

Three tax points. We could not find any published Gambian rule on the base cost of an inherited asset for capital gains tax — that is, whether the heir's gain is measured from the deceased's original cost or from the value at death. It can change the bill materially, so get it in writing from GRA before pricing a sale. We could not confirm whether an assent or transmission to an heir, as opposed to a sale, is treated as a chargeable disposal for CGT. And on the rate for a deceased estate, GRA publishes trustees at the company rate of 25% / 10%, while PwC's older Gambia guide groups a "trustee of a deceased estate" with individuals at 15% / 5% — confirm which applies to your estate with GRA rather than assuming. Separately, the frequently repeated claim that unpaid Area Council rates follow the property to a new owner is something we could not trace to any Gambian statute or Council source; treat it as a question to put to the Council in writing, not as a settled rule.

Widows, minor heirs and heirs abroad

A widow in occupation

Section 44 of the Women's Act 2010 gives a widow the right to continue to live in the matrimonial home, alongside an equitable share in her husband's property — both "subject to personal law". For a family, that is the reason a widow cannot simply be told to leave while the estate is sorted out. For a buyer, it is a reason to establish who is living in the house and on what basis before agreeing anything: a sale that requires a widow to vacate is not a paperwork problem, it is a dispute waiting to happen.

Minor heirs

A minor heir stops a sale in its tracks. Musa Sabily v Hassan Baye turned on exactly this point: estate property could not be disposed of by sale, gift or mortgage without the approval of the Cadi Appeals Panel, or of the heir herself once she had reached the legal age of puberty. If any heir is a child, expect the Panel's approval to be a precondition of any transaction, and expect that to take time.

Heirs abroad

Diaspora families ask this constantly and the honest answer is that it is unresolved. Inheritance is not allocation: Schedule II, paragraph 2 of the State Lands Regulations 1995 provides that "A non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land", but that governs government allocation of state plots, not succession. Separately, the Lands (Regions) Act caps a non-indigene at 50 years — a rule of descent, not of nationality, so it catches many Gambian citizens too — and any assignment of a State lease needs the Minister's written consent under regulation 22(l).

What we could not verify

Whether a non-Gambian heir may hold Gambian land inherited from a Gambian relative, and on what terms. We found no Gambian statute, regulation or official page addressing succession by a non-Gambian, and no reported decision on it. The interaction between the succession rules and the foreign-ownership rules is genuinely unsettled, and it is exactly the kind of question a Gambian lawyer should answer in writing for your family before, not after, a death. See buying property in The Gambia as a foreigner for what is and is not established about non-Gambian ownership generally.

If you are offered inherited or family land

Inherited land is not automatically a bad buy. It is a buy where the questions are different, and where the seller often does not know the answers. Ask these before money moves, and put the answers in writing.

  1. Is this individualised land or family land? If it is lineage land, no share calculation makes it saleable. This is the first question, not the last.
  2. Is the owner dead, and when? Get the death certificate and the date. Six months from that date is when the Curator's mandate can arise.
  3. Where is the grant? Ask for the grant of probate, the letters of administration, or the High Court order in favour of the Curator. No grant, no authority — an heir's signature alone is not enough.
  4. Has a Cadi Court distributed the estate? In a Muslim estate, ask for the distribution order and read who is named. Anyone entitled and not named is a future claimant.
  5. Has the Curator taken it over? Look for a Gazette notice — the Curator publishes for fourteen days. If the Curator is administering, deal with the Curator.
  6. Has the estate been valued by a qualified estate valuer? Distribution is not supposed to proceed without it.
  7. Is every heir consenting, and is any of them a minor? A minor heir means the Cadi Appeals Panel's approval, and delay.
  8. Is anyone living there? A widow has a statutory right to remain in the matrimonial home. Establish occupation before agreeing a completion date.
  9. What is the underlying tenure? A State lease needs the Minister's written consent to assign; customary and deemed-leasehold land cannot be registered at the Deeds Registry at all. See how to verify land title.
  10. Who is paying the capital gains tax, and can the estate fund it? It has to be paid before the deed is registered. If the estate is illiquid, agree in the contract how it is funded.
  11. Are the ground rent and Area Council rates up to date? Ask for receipts, and ask the Council in writing what is outstanding.
  12. Is there a dispute already running? Pending litigation does not appear on the Deeds Registry search — that is a separate enquiry, and Gambian property cases have been reported to run over three years.

If the answers are unclear, the right response is not a bigger deposit and a faster contract. It is a slower one — and money held in your lawyer's client account rather than paid over. No Gambian rule compels an agent or a seller to use a client account, so you have to insist on it.

If you own Gambian property: what to do now

  1. Register the property in your own name. An estate cannot pass what the documents do not say you own. A deed in a cousin's name "for convenience" is the most common and most expensive mistake in Gambian diaspora property.
  2. Individualise the holding. If what you have is family or customary land, converting it into a documented, individualised title is the single change that turns an unbuyable, undividable holding into an estate asset your heirs can actually deal with.
  3. Make a Gambian will for your Gambian property, drafted by a Gambian lawyer, even if you already have a will at home. We cannot verify how a foreign will is treated here, which is precisely the reason not to rely on one.
  4. If you are Muslim, take advice on how far a will can go. The Cadi Courts have jurisdiction over succession and testaments, and the fixed shares are the starting point. Do not assume your will overrides them.
  5. Register the will. Wills are registrable instruments at the Deeds Registry under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01. It is a small fee against a large risk.
  6. Name an executor who can actually act in The Gambia. An absent executor is exactly how an estate drifts past six months and into the Curator's hands.
  7. Decide the partition question in advance. If the estate is one house and there are several heirs, write down now whether it is to be sold and divided, held jointly under a written agreement, or left to one heir with others compensated elsewhere. The law will otherwise decide it for you: what cannot be divided without harm must be sold.
  8. Keep the file complete and known. Deed or lease, survey plan, development permit, ground rent and rates receipts, stamp duty evidence, the death certificates of anyone earlier in the chain, and your lawyer's name — in one place your family can find. Half of all estate disputes are really document disputes.
  9. Review after every life event — marriage, divorce, a child, a new property, a move between countries.

If you are the one inheriting

  • Watch the six months. Diarise the date of death. If no grant is obtained within six months, the Curator of Intestate Estates may take the estate over.
  • Do not pay anyone, agree anything or accept a share until you have seen the deed or lease and know which system governs the estate.
  • Instruct one Gambian lawyer for the heirs jointly if you possibly can. Separate lawyers for each heir is how estates become litigation.
  • Get the grant, or the Cadi Court distribution order, or both. Nothing else is possible without them, and in a Muslim estate the two do different jobs.
  • Get a valuation from a qualified estate valuer. It is a precondition of distribution, and it protects you against a sibling's number.
  • Establish the running costs. Ground rent to the Department of Lands, Area Council rates, utility accounts. Ask the Council in writing what is outstanding.
  • Price the capital gains tax into any sale before you agree a figure with a buyer. It must be paid before the deed is registered.
  • Expect it to take longer than you are told. The only fixed timings anyone can hold you to are the six-month Curator trigger and the Curator's fourteen-day Gazette notice. Everything else is unpublished.

Questions people actually ask

Which law governs inheritance of property in The Gambia?

One of three, and often two at once. The High Court administers the estate and issues the grant under the general law. A Cadi Court applies the Sharia to fix entitlement where the parties or other persons interested are Muslims, with appeal to the Cadi Appeals Panel. Customary law applies through District Tribunals, particularly to land held under customary tenure, with appeal to the High Court. The 1997 Constitution recognises all three.

Is there a probate registry in The Gambia?

No. Grants of probate, letters of administration and administration orders issue from the High Court. We could find no Gambian source naming a separate probate registry, and an earlier version of this guide was wrong to do so.

What happens if nobody applies for a grant?

If probate or letters of administration are not obtained within six months of the death, the Curator of Intestate Estates, a department of the Ministry of Justice, may take the estate over under the Intestate Estates Act, Cap. 14:02. Beneficiaries file a Declaration Form with the death certificate and family documents, the Curator publishes notice in the Gazette for fourteen days, and then applies to the High Court for an order authorising administration.

Do I need a Gambian will for my Gambian property?

It is the safer course. We could not find any Gambian resealing statute or conflict-of-laws authority telling us how a foreign will is treated, so we cannot say a Dutch or British will is recognised here. A Gambian will drafted by a Gambian lawyer, naming an executor who can act locally, removes that uncertainty for your heirs.

How are shares divided under Sharia inheritance in The Gambia?

They are fixed, not discretionary. In reported Cadi Appeals Panel cases the wife took one-eighth and a son and daughter shared the residue, with a male taking double the share of a female. Property capable of partition without harm must be partitioned among the heirs rather than settled in money.

The estate is one house and there are five heirs. What happens?

The Cadi Appeals Panel has held that what cannot be divided without causing harm must be sold, and the opposing party must be forced to accept it. So the house is sold and the proceeds divided in the fixed shares, even over one heir's objection — but getting there means litigation, a valuation and time, so agreeing it in advance is far cheaper.

Can I buy a plot from one heir of a family compound?

Usually not safely. Sharia inheritance rules apply only to land that has been individualised, not to family land. Lineage land is not an estate asset to be divided, so no heir has a share of it to sell you, and no distribution order will fix that.

Is there inheritance tax in The Gambia?

No inheritance tax, estate duty, death duty or gift tax appears in any of five independent listings of Gambian taxes, including the Gambia Revenue Authority's own. Inheriting is not taxed. Selling is: capital gains tax applies at the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration for individuals, and the higher of 25% or 10% for companies, partnerships and trustees, and it must be paid before the deed can be registered.

What documents prove that heirs are entitled to sell?

A grant of probate or letters of administration, or a High Court administration order in favour of the Curator, proves authority to transact. In a Muslim estate a Cadi Court distribution order proves who the heirs are and in what shares. A valuation by a qualified estate valuer is a precondition of distribution. Where the property is a State lease, the Minister's written consent is needed for the assignment, and the transfer is then registered at the Deeds Registry.

Can a widow be required to leave the family home?

Section 44 of the Women's Act 2010 gives a widow the right to continue to live in the matrimonial home and to an equitable share in her husband's property — but both rights are expressly "subject to personal law", and the Constitution exempts devolution of property on death from the non-discrimination guarantee. In practice the personal law governs the shares. A buyer should establish who is in occupation before agreeing anything.

Does a minor heir stop a sale?

In substance, yes, until it is dealt with properly. The Cadi Appeals Panel has held that estate property cannot be disposed of by sale, gift or mortgage without the Panel's approval, or that of the heir once of age. Expect the Panel's approval to be a precondition and expect delay.

Can my children abroad inherit and hold my Gambian house?

We could not resolve this. Inheriting is not the same as being allocated land, and the rule that "a non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land" governs government allocation, not succession. But the Lands (Regions) Act caps a non-indigene at 50 years and any assignment of a State lease needs the Minister's written consent. Get a Gambian lawyer to answer it in writing for your family in advance.

Do appeals from a District Tribunal go to the regional governor?

No. Appeals from District Tribunals in customary land matters go to the High Court, and to the Cadi Appeals Panel where Sharia law is involved. A Governor's role under the Lands (Regions) Act is limited to disputes about the value of fixtures and economic trees, with appeal to the Minister.
