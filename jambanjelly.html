The vibe

Jambanjelly is where the Sukuta–Jambanjelly road stops, meeting the Brikama–Sanyang connector. That makes it the far end of a tarred corridor from the Kombo coast, and it has drawn developers: TAF Africa Global's Tulip Gardens, a gated estate of serviced plots and built houses, is here, and other companies are selling plots in phases around the village. The 2013 census counted 5,285 people, which makes it one of the larger villages in Kombo South.

It is inland — the nearest Atlantic coastline, the Sanyang beach stretch, is just under seven kilometres west — and it is still a farming settlement at heart, on what local reporting describes as a peri-urban corridor where estates and light industry are going up along the road. Prices reflect that: this is not coastal land, and it is not priced like coastal land.

Jambanjelly has only 1 priced plot listing with a known size — below our bar of 5 for an area-specific rate. The rate above is the band rate for the inland Kombo villages between the Brikama highway and the Sukuta–Jambanjelly road: 13 priced plot listings pooled across Farato, Latriya, Mamuda and Jambanjelly. It tells you what the band asks, not what a specific street asks. The one listing we can price in Jambanjelly itself asks D1,202 per m² — one seller’s opinion of one plot, shown here as context, not as a rate.

Every figure on this page is an asking price. No one in The Gambia publishes what property actually sells for — there is no public register of sale prices — so what a seller asks is the only thing that can be measured consistently. Sale prices are generally lower, by an amount that depends entirely on the seller. We have no rent listing we can verify here, so we quote no rental yield for this area either — a yield built on a rent we had to guess would only look like knowledge.

These figures are as honest as we can make them — and still young. Reliable Gambian market data barely exists yet, for anyone; building it is one of the reasons MyKunda exists at all. So we publish what we can measure, print the evidence count next to every figure, and leave a blank rather than defend a number we cannot. A market where buyers and sellers can trust what they read has to start somewhere. Every listing and every verified sale sharpens these figures — and if you know a figure on this page to be wrong, tell us.

What to know before buying at Jambanjelly

We searched Gambian press, court reporting and land-commission material and found no documented land dispute at Jambanjelly. That is worth stating precisely: it means we found none, not that there are none. Every inland Kombo village where farmland is being converted into plots carries the same structural risk — an undemarcated boundary, a family head selling what a neighbour also claims — and Jambanjelly's neighbours have live disputes even where it does not.

Two things are worth separating. TAF Tulip Gardens is a gated estate at Jambanjelly, and buying a serviced plot inside a developer's scheme is a different transaction from buying customary land from a family. TAF City is a different project entirely, between Gunjur and Sifoe some twelve kilometres south; the two are often confused. Whichever you are buying, ask the same questions: who held the land before the developer, what documentation transfers to you, and what a lawyer's search turns up. Our guide to verifying land title covers the searches, and developer financing covers the payment plans estates here offer.

Getting there and what is on the ground

The 13.5 km Sukuta–Jambanjelly road — US$23 million, funded largely by the Islamic Development Bank — was inaugurated in March 2020 and terminates here. It is the reason the village is now within easy reach of the coast, and it is also the source of the village's best-documented complaint: in July 2020 residents threatened legal action over flooding they blamed on inadequate drainage along the new road, and the contractor acknowledged in a village meeting that promised drainage, street lighting and water connections had been cut from the design after budget reductions. If you are buying here, ask where the water goes in August.

Schools and a mosque are mapped within a kilometre and a half of the village centre, and a fuel station within about 350 metres. Boreholes have served the village since the 1980s. We could not confirm mains piped water or a grid electricity connection to the village; the 23 MW Jambur solar plant is about five kilometres away but that says nothing about local distribution. Developer marketing claims power and water have been extended to individual schemes — treat those as the developer's claim.

What we have not measured here yet

Jambanjelly is one of five areas we added to MyKunda in August 2026, and its page is deliberately thinner than our older ones. Rather than fill it with plausible numbers, here is what we do not know.

  • Rents — no rental listing we can verify, so no rent and no yield are published.
  • House prices — one local house listing only, which is not enough for a figure; the house number above is land plus build cost.
  • Mains water and grid electricity at village level.
  • Whether the drainage problems reported in 2020 have been fixed.
  • Plot counts, acreage or completion status for the estates being sold here.

GeoNames files the village under the spelling Jambanjali (13.280 N 16.729 W); OpenStreetMap has it as Jambanjelly, 0.4 km away. The pin above sits between the two. Coordinates given elsewhere as 13.217 N 16.750 W are the centroid of Kombo South district, not the village, and are about 7 km out.

If you live here, sell here or have bought here, you can correct any of this: tell us what we have wrong. That is how the rest of these pages got better.
