The best areas to buy on the Gambian coast

MK MyKunda Updated 31 August 2026 24 min read

The Kombo coast is short — about an hour of driving from Kololi to Kartong — and the areas along it differ far more in law, tenure and risk than they do in scenery. This guide compares them on things that are actually published: where the Tourism Development Area boundary runs, which stretches of shoreline are documented as eroding, which villages have live land disputes, how far each is from the airport, and what our own listings are asking. Where a statement is our opinion rather than a sourced fact, we label it as such.

In short

  • Asking prices in MyKunda listings ranged from about US$140/m² of bare land in Kololi to about US$14/m² in Kartong in August 2026. These are asking prices. The Gambia has no public register of transacted sale prices, so nobody — including us — can publish what plots actually sold for.
  • The seaward 800 metres from Kotu to Kartong is the Tourism Development Area: leased State land in the custody of the Gambia Tourism Board. All new land applications inside it have been suspended since 23 January 2025.
  • That 800 m band is a tenure control, not an erosion setback. The World Bank reported on 28 February 2026 that "no formal setback zones or planning restrictions exist yet" anywhere on the Gambian coast.
  • Erosion on parts of the tourism corridor has been estimated at up to 4.7 metres a year. Kotu Stream to Senegambia — the most expensive stretch — is classified high economic risk from 2040.
  • Sanyang is the site of a US$1 billion deep-sea port. On 25 August 2026 the Ministry of Lands warned publicly against unauthorised development in the port area.
  • Salagi, Batokunku, Tanji, Brufut, Brusubi, Gunjur, Berending, Kartong and Farato all have documented, dated land disputes, demolitions or sand-mining conflicts. Read the area section before you pay a deposit.

How to read this guide

Area guides tend to be written in adjectives — lively, leafy, up-and-coming. Adjectives are not data, and when they are set out in a table they start to look like data. So this guide separates two kinds of statement, and marks them differently.

  • Sourced facts. Every figure, date, boundary and dispute below traces to a named source with a date, listed in Sources at the end.
  • MyKunda's view. Character, atmosphere and who an area tends to suit are our own impressions from working the market. They are opinion. We say so each time, and you should weigh them accordingly.

On prices: every price on this page is an asking price, taken from listings on MyKunda in August 2026. The Gambia has no public register of transacted prices, so an asking price is the only observable number on the coast. Asking prices are set by sellers, they are negotiated down more often than up, and they say nothing about whether a plot is buildable, assignable or free of dispute. Read them as a rough ranking of areas, not as valuations.

What we could not verify

No Gambian body publishes land values, transacted prices, rental yields, occupancy rates or bed-space counts by settlement. GBoS's own tourist-arrivals series stops at 2022. Settlement-level population tables from the 2024 census were not retrievable. There is no published facility register for schools, clinics or markets on the coast. Anyone who gives you a per-area yield, an occupancy percentage or a "10% annual capital growth" figure for Kololi or Kartong is estimating, and we have not found a source that would let us check it. We have therefore left those numbers out rather than invent them.

The coast is moving: erosion, flooding and the missing setback

This is the single largest thing missing from most Gambian area guides, including our own earlier version of this one. In Beyond the Tide: Building Flood and Erosion Resilience in The Gambia, published on 28 February 2026, the World Bank sets out the position on the tourism coast.

  • Erosion rates along parts of the tourism corridor "have been estimated at up to 4.7 meters per year".
  • More than US$2.1 billion in assets — over 90% of national GDP — sit within 800 metres of the coastline.
  • Risk is graded by horizon. Kotu Stream to Senegambia moves from low economic risk in 2020 to high in 2040 and high in 2070. Fajara to Kotu Stream moves from low to medium in 2040 and high by 2070. The Cape Point to Fajara cliffs, and the Senegambia-to-Tanji stretch, are graded low now and medium by 2070.
  • Hard defences built by hotel owners — seawalls, gabions, riprap, sandbags — are described as having "unsuccessful outcomes". A beach nourishment of one million cubic metres in 2003 disappeared "in about five years". Rock armouring and sand filling followed in 2016, and offshore breakwaters on the Senegambia shoreline are planned.
  • Cape Point is the counter-example. Revetments and six groins there "have created wide and attractive beaches for tourists". Revetments were built at the Tanji river mouth in 2017.
  • Flooding is a separate risk with a different map. Kotu Stream is graded high social risk from fluvial and pluvial flooding by both 2040 and 2070, driven by expanding impervious surfaces, limited drainage, waste blocking channels and building into wetlands and floodplains. Legislation to clear obstructions in the stream exists but, in the report's words, "its enforcement is not strict".

The part that matters most to a buyer is what the law does not do. The same report states plainly: "No formal setback zones or planning restrictions exist yet." The Integrated Coastal Zone Management Bill, which would introduce coastal setback zones, is still in legal implementation at the National Environment Agency. Establishing setback zones appears in the report's own list of priority actions still to be taken.

So there is no statutory buffer between a legal plot boundary and a retreating shoreline. A plot can be properly titled, properly consented and still sit inside a documented retreat zone. Nothing in a lease, a sketch plan or a development permit tells you where the water will be in twenty years.

One rule does bite near the water. Under the National Environment Agency's screening categories, "major structures within 150 metres of the high water mark" fall in Class A — the class that requires a full environmental impact assessment. NEA fees are D1,000 for screening, D5,000–25,000 for processing and an approval fee of 1% of development cost. If you are planning anything substantial on a beachfront plot, budget for that assessment and for the time it takes. See our guide to building a house in The Gambia for the permit path.

What we could not verify

No published dataset gives a shoreline retreat rate for an individual plot or village. The 4.7 m/year figure is described by the World Bank as applying to "parts of the tourism corridor", not to any named beach, and the risk gradings are stretch-level. We could not obtain the underlying 2021 Royal Haskoning investment options study the gradings are drawn from. Treat the gradings as a reason to commission your own coastal-engineering opinion before buying near the water, not as a measurement of your plot.

The Tourism Development Area: Kotu to Kartong, 800 metres inland

Most of the beachfront on this coast is not ordinary land. The West Coast Tourism Development Area is a defined strip running from Kotu south to Kartong, extending 800 metres inland from the high water mark. It is leased State land held in the custody of the Gambia Tourism Board under the Gambia Tourism Board Act 2011. The strip was leased to central government by the Kombo North District Authority in 1969 for 51 years and extended to 99 years under the State Lands Act, Act No. 1 of 1991.

Read that boundary against the price table further down and the point becomes obvious: the seaward edge of every area in this guide from Kotu southwards — Kololi, Bijilo, Brufut, Tanji, Batokunku, Tujereng, Sanyang, Gunjur, Kartong — falls inside it.

Four things follow, and all four are current.

  • You cannot apply for TDA land at the moment. The Ministry of Tourism and Culture issued a press release on 23 January 2025 instructing the Gambia Tourism Board to place "a temporary hold on all new applications for land within the Tourism Development Area (TDA)" pending an audit by the Board. We have found no source showing that hold lifted as at 31 August 2026. The stated appeal route runs from Board management to the Governing Body chairperson to the Minister.
  • Past allocations are under scrutiny. On 18 November 2025 a National Assembly enquiry heard the Gambia Tourism Board's Director General say that investors had received TDA land before completing environmental impact assessments, and that although allocations "should be cancelled if conditions are not met within three years, many plots have remained undeveloped for more than ten years". The committee ordered all allocation documents produced within 48 hours.
  • Land inside the TDA has been sold by people who did not hold it. The Board publicly condemned "illegal selling and clearing of landed properties" inside the TDA on 28 February 2018, naming Brufut Heights, Tanji, Batokunku, Sanyang, Medina Salam and Kartong. In May 2019 it said it had instituted legal action against estate agents, and a developer defending itself against a Board claim argued its Sanyang land was "1,400 metres from the sea" and therefore outside "the 800-metre space from the sea as specified by law". Whether a plot is in or out of the band is a live, litigated question — not a formality.
  • The 800 m band is a tenure and land-use control, not an erosion setback. It tells you who must consent to what you do. It does not tell you the sea will stop 800 metres away. The World Bank's own assessment is that development control inside the TDA suffers from "a lack of clarity and enforcement", and that the regulations under the Gambia Tourism Board Act "included no requirements for environmental impact assessments or mentions of climate-proofing".

The practical check: before you pay anything for a coastal plot, establish in writing whether it lies inside the 800 m band, and if it does, what the Gambia Tourism Board's position on it is. A vendor who cannot answer that question has not answered the most important question about the plot. Our guides to verifying land title and freehold, leasehold and customary land set out what a proper search does and does not cover.

Kololi & Senegambia

What is sourced. This is the densest hotel cluster on the coast; the World Bank names "Senegambia, Kololi, and Kotu" as the concentration. It is also the highest-risk stretch for erosion: Kotu Stream to Senegambia is graded high economic risk from 2040 and again in 2070, and it is where the failed 2003 beach nourishment and the hotel-built hard defences are. Kotu Stream carries a high social flood-risk grading by 2040. Driving distance to Banjul International Airport at Yundum is about 18.4 km, roughly 21 minutes free-flow. The seaward 800 m is inside the Tourism Development Area.

MyKunda's view (opinion, not data). If you want to walk to restaurants, bars and a hotel beach, this is the only part of the coast that delivers it. It is also the noisiest and the most seasonal, and it carries the highest asking prices we see. We have no yield, occupancy or transaction data for it, and we do not publish estimates of any.

Cape Point & Bakau

What is sourced. Cape Point is the one stretch on this coast where coastal defence has visibly worked: the World Bank records revetments and six groins there (300 m, 120 m, 100 m and others) that "have created wide and attractive beaches for tourists". Its erosion grading is the mildest on the coast — low now, low in 2040, medium by 2070 — as is the Oyster Creek to Cape Point section. Cape Point is about 23.5 km from the airport, roughly 25 minutes free-flow; Bakau about 22.2 km, roughly 22 minutes. Bakau and Cape Point sit inside Kanifing Municipality, which is State Lands territory under the State Lands Act, Act No. 1 of 1991 — so plots here are generally state leasehold rather than customary land. They lie north of Kotu and therefore outside the Tourism Development Area band.

MyKunda's view (opinion, not data). Older, quieter and closer to Banjul than the Senegambia strip, with a long-settled residential character. On the published evidence it is the most defensible beach frontage on the coast, which is the single strongest argument for the premium asking prices here.

Brufut & Bijilo

What is sourced. Brufut is about 17.6 km from the airport (roughly 22 minutes) and Bijilo about 15.6 km (roughly 19 minutes) — the closest of the named coastal areas apart from Brusubi at 14.0 km. Both have live governance issues on land. Brufut's land recovery committee told Foroyaa in May 2020 that communal land the Janneh Commission had recommended be returned was instead sold, and that "presently, we do not have a single land in Brufut". On 27 March 2026 a Brufut councillor and residents protested that land in the Brusubi Layout reserved for a market and a transport garage had reportedly been demarcated for governors, ministers and chief executives, with construction already begun on one plot. Brufut Heights was named by the Gambia Tourism Board in 2018 among the sites where TDA land was being sold illegally. The World Bank attributes the partial destruction of the Bijilo forest to weak development control inside the TDA. A 12 km bituminous road linking Brufut, Madiana, Banyaka and Kunkujang Mariama was inaugurated on 9 May 2026.

MyKunda's view (opinion, not data). This is where most of the newer walled and gated estate development is, and where buyers who want space rather than a bar strip tend to end up. The trade-off is that estate-standard infrastructure is contractual, not guaranteed: put roads, lighting and amenities in the contract with dates and remedies, as our developer financing guide explains.

Sanyang, Tanji & Kartong

This stretch can no longer honestly be described as simply quiet beach and eco-lodges. The largest infrastructure project on the Gambian coast is being built in the middle of it.

Sanyang and the deep-sea port. President Barrow laid the foundation stone for a US$1 billion deep-sea port at Sanyang on 5 July 2025. It is a public-private partnership between the Gambia Ports Authority and Türkiye's Albayrak Group, through Alport Banjul, on a 30-year concession, with a six-year build in phases costed at €253m, €145m and €207m. The concession took effect on 14 February 2025 and pre-construction was due to end on 13 February 2026; on 5 February 2026 the Ports Authority's Managing Director said the phase had been delayed by about a year by "land-access challenges, equipment problems, and incomplete technical studies", with terms being renegotiated. Then, on 25 August 2026, the Ministry of Lands issued a public warning: the public is "strongly advised to desist from undertaking any form of development, construction, occupation, or other activities within the said area without the requisite approval from the relevant authorities".

A working deep-sea port changes traffic, land use, land value and the character of a beach village, in directions nobody can currently price. It may lift values; it may also mean your beach plot ends up behind a haulage corridor. Either way, the first question about any Sanyang plot in 2026 is whether it falls inside the port footprint.

Tanji. Revetments were built at the Tanji river mouth in 2017. Tanji is about 22.1 km from the airport, roughly 30 minutes. Its Alkalo was suspended in October 2025 (see the next section). It was named by the Gambia Tourism Board in 2018 as a site of illegal TDA land sales.

Kartong. The southern end of the coast and the southern end of the TDA: about 37.3 km and roughly 49 minutes from the airport, and about 52 km and around an hour from Kololi. It is a party to the long-running Gunjur–Berending boundary dispute and has a contested sand-mining approval (both below).

MyKunda's view (opinion, not data). There is genuinely more space, more beach and lower asking prices down here, and for some buyers that is the whole point. But "long-term hold" is a strategy that assumes the thing you are holding stays what it is. On this stretch, in 2026, that assumption needs checking plot by plot.

Sand mining is a separate and specifically coastal problem, and it touches most of the southern beaches.

  • Kartong — the National Environment Agency granted environmental approval under the EIA Regulations 2014 for a sand quarry at Kartong, against village-wide opposition (Kerr Fatou, 19 September 2022).
  • Batokunku — youths protested against the mining agreement in October 2025, alleging that white sand was being taken where the memorandum permitted black sand (The Standard, 23 October 2025).
  • Gunjur — night mining on beach dunes and on public and private property, with the village development committee saying it lacks "the legal authority to intervene" (Foroyaa, 13 August 2024).
  • Sanyang — a resident account of road and electricity arriving, then rapid land buying, then "hundreds of trucks" of sand mining (Foroyaa, 25 April 2026).

The policy backdrop is not in dispute: the World Bank records that The Gambia's own Tourism Policy calls for eliminating "the haphazard planning of the coastal area, landscape erosion, indiscriminate sand mining on the beaches, and environmental pollution". Sand removal upstream of your beach is not someone else's problem — it is one of the mechanisms by which a beach disappears.

For context on how common land conflict is: the Minister for Lands said on 21 May 2026 that 67% of court cases in The Gambia are land-related; a validation workshop report of 4 May 2026 put it at about 75%. We report both, attributed, because the two official figures do not agree.

What the coast costs, area by area

The clearest way to compare areas is the asking price of bare land per square metre, because it strips out the age, size and finish of whatever is standing on the plot. The figures below are asking prices observed in MyKunda listings in August 2026. They are not valuations, not transacted prices and not an index.

There is no transacted-price register in The Gambia. Deeds are registered at the Deeds Registry of the Registrar General's Department, which records instruments and priority — not a searchable database of sale prices. No government body, and no regulated body, publishes land values or a price index by settlement. So no one on this coast, ourselves included, can tell you what plots actually sold for. Anyone who claims to is quoting asking prices too.

Kololi~US$140/m²~US$84,000
Cape Point~US$100/m²~US$60,000
Bijilo~US$80/m²~US$48,000
Brufut~US$48/m²~US$29,000
Tanji~US$25/m²~US$15,000
Sanyang~US$18/m²~US$11,000
Kartong~US$14/m²~US$8,400

The third column is arithmetic, not a market observation: it is simply the rate multiplied by 600 m². 600 m² is not a Gambian standard plot. No reliable source gives one. The only statutory framework is Schedule IV of the State Lands Regulations 1995, whose residential bands are up to 300 m², 301–500 m² and 501–750 m², with multipliers up to 1,000 m². Use your own plot's surveyed area, not ours.

The spread is the story: asking prices in Kololi are roughly ten times those in Kartong, over a drive of about 52 km and around an hour. What the top of the range buys is proximity to the tourist strip. What it also buys, on the published evidence, is the most erosion-exposed frontage on the coast. What the bottom of the range buys is space and beach — and, in Sanyang's case, a port under construction.

Before you convert any of these numbers into a budget, read the real cost of buying property in The Gambia. Stamp duty on an assignment is 2% of the amount and is paid by the buyer; the seller's capital gains tax is the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration for an individual, and it must be settled before the deed can be registered — which makes it your completion risk as well as theirs.

What we could not verify

We cannot verify our own price table against any external source, because none exists. The figures are internally consistent and drawn from our listings, but they carry no sample size, no time series and no independent check. A single unusual listing can move an area's average. Treat the ranking between areas as more reliable than any individual number.

Power, water and roads

Services are a national constraint on this coast, not an area-by-area differentiator, and they are worse in 2026 than the brochures suggest.

  • Electricity. In April 2026 NAWEC reported a 26 megawatt shortfall — capacity of about 78 MW against peak demand of about 106 MW — with 52% of available supply imported, and urged conservation to avert a total blackout. A 50-day emergency purchase of up to 70 MW from Senegal, announced on 24 June 2026, expired around 15 August 2026, with peak demand by then reported at about 140 MW. The World Bank records national access to electricity at 69% of the population in 2024. Budget for a generator or solar, and for the fuel to run it.
  • Water. NAWEC's Head of Water Planning told Foroyaa in June 2026: "The amount we have to supply and the demand we have cannot meet the needs of all," and that it had been "more than 17 years since we last commissioned the Brikama water treatment plant". A new treatment plant is being built at Tujereng to ease the shortage.
  • Connections. NAWEC's published basis is a D500 application fee, a survey in about two weeks and installation about four weeks after payment, with standard connection limits of 45 metres from a low-voltage pole for electricity and 150 metres from the mains for water. Beyond those distances the extension is at your cost — which is exactly what makes a cheap plot at the far end of a new layout expensive. A certificate of completion is required before NAWEC will give a water connection.
  • Roads. The newest link on this coast is the 12 km bituminous Brufut–Madiana–Banyaka–Kunkujang Mariama road, with an 8 metre carriageway, inaugurated on 9 May 2026.
What we could not verify

There is no published, settlement-level map of which streets on the coast have mains water, mains electricity or a tarred approach. Nor is there a published register of schools, clinics or markets by area that we could check. Individual facilities appear in press reports — a clinic at Tujereng, a mortuary at Kartong, a clinic and streetlights at Batokunku, funding for a Brufut market that is held up by a land constraint, planned health facilities at Farato — but that is a scatter of items, not a services picture. Walk the actual street, in the rainy season if you can, and ask the neighbours what the supply is like.

What is driving demand

Two things, and both should be quoted accurately.

Tourism. The Government reported 233,113 tourist arrivals in 2025, up about 3% on 2024. That figure comes from a National Economic Council report, carried by Kerr Fatou on 16 March 2026 and repeated in the State of the Nation Address of 26 March 2026 — it is a single government figure reported more than once, not two independent counts. For context, the World Bank records 235,800 arrivals in 2019, so 2025 remained marginally below the pre-pandemic level. MoFEA's target for 2026 is 298,042. GBoS's own published arrivals series stops at 2022, when it recorded 185,728.

The season matters more than the annual total for anyone buying to let. The official season opens in October and the peak runs November to April — six months, not five. We do not publish occupancy rates: the widely repeated "90% in winter, 25% off-season" comes from a US Commercial Service country guide last published on 30 August 2022, is a COVID-era figure, and measures hotel occupancy in a charter market, which is not evidence about self-catering lets. Our guide to renting out property covers the tax side: 8% of gross residential rent, 15% commercial, with no deductions allowed.

There is a counter-case, and it deserves an airing. The economist Dr Ousman Gajigo wrote in January 2026 that "the tourism sector in The Gambia today is in a moribund state" and that government focus on arrivals as a metric misses the sector's health. That is opinion, clearly labelled — but it is the argument that a guide selling coastal property should not quietly omit.

The diaspora. The Central Bank of The Gambia recorded remittance inflows of US$872.14 million in 2025, up 12.4% on 2024's US$775.6 million, in its Monetary Policy Committee statement of 26 February 2026. That is a very large number against a small economy. But it does not follow that it is going into land: the Central Bank's own Household Remittance Survey, conducted in April and May 2024 with 32,783 responses and published in its 2024 Annual Report, found that 76.8% of remittances go to household consumption and 4.0% to construction. Anyone telling you that remittances are pouring into coastal plots is arguing against the Central Bank's own survey.

Growth. The World Bank's Macro Poverty Outlook of 16 April 2026 projects 5.3% growth for The Gambia in 2026. The Central Bank projected 6.2% in February 2026 and 5.8% in August 2026; the African Development Bank projects 4.9%. The figure of 5.7% that circulates as a 2026 projection is not one: it is the Central Bank's estimate of actual 2025 growth. Construction and industry are named among the growth drivers by both the Central Bank and the World Bank, and GBoS recorded industry growth of 19.9% in the fourth quarter of 2025.

Population. The 2024 census preliminary report put the national population at 2,422,712, with Brikama Local Government Area alone accounting for 47.5% and Brikama plus Kanifing for 63.2%. The World Bank notes that coastal urban areas now hold nearly 60% of the national population. Whatever else is true of this coast, the domestic demand for housing on it is real.

If you are selling on the coast

Most area guides address only buyers. If you own on this coast and want to sell, or you are an agent or developer listing here, these are the things that actually stop transactions.

  • A plot nobody can get a permit for is not worth its asking price. In Batokunku, 40 homeowners went to court in June 2025 alleging that the Ministry of Lands had refused development permits there for nearly 20 years. If permits in your area are being refused, disclose it — a buyer will find out at the worst possible moment, and an undeveloped plot has its own separate problem below.
  • A development permit is not proof of ownership and not protection from removal. The Minister for Lands said in November 2025 that a permit "is only one process leading to the construction of a house" and that "obtaining the development permit does not stop this Government from moving people who have occupied public places". Do not market a permit as if it were title.
  • There is no compensation precedent to rely on. After the Salagi demolitions the Minister said he would "not contemplate even compensating them one dime". Compulsory acquisition under the Land Acquisition and Compensation Act, Cap. 57:06, and section 22 of the 1997 Constitution is a different route with its own compensation rules — but where the State treats occupation as unlawful, the published record shows no compensation being paid.
  • Alkalo-chain title is the weakest document you can offer. An Alkalo transfer or Alkalo certificate is evidence of a customary allocation, not registrable title. In October 2025 the Alkalolu of Batokunku and Tanji were suspended over alleged illegal sales in government layouts, and the Ministry stated that selling land in any government-designated layout is prohibited outright. A Brikama magistrate observed in August 2026 that it is "increasingly common for a single plot of land to be sold to several individuals, with each buyer subsequently presenting an Alkalo transfer document as proof of ownership". If your chain runs through an Alkalo, perfect it before you list, not during the sale.
  • Consent to assign is the step that kills coastal deals. Under regulation 22(l) of the State Lands Regulations 1995 a lessee may not "subdivide, convey, assign or otherwise alienate the premises … without the consent of the Minister in writing first obtained", and that consent "shall not be granted if the lessee has not developed the demised premises in accordance with the covenants in that behalf". In plain terms: an undeveloped state-leasehold plot may not be lawfully assignable until it is built on. The World Bank measured ministerial consent as a 60-day, GMD 750 procedure back in 2019. Regulation 22(k) requires the same consent for a mortgage. Under the Lands (Regions) Act, alienation without the required consent is null and void, not merely voidable.
  • If your plot is inside the Tourism Development Area, say so. Gambian Tourism Board consent governs what may be done with it, new applications have been suspended since 23 January 2025, and past allocations were before a National Assembly enquiry in November 2025. A buyer's lawyer will find this. Better that they find it in your listing.
  • If your plot is inside the Sanyang port footprint, that is a material fact. The Ministry of Lands warned on 25 August 2026 against any unauthorised development, construction or occupation in the port area. Selling development land there without disclosing the warning is not a defensible position.
  • Your buyer pool is not what a price table implies. A non-Gambian cannot be allocated a state plot: paragraph 2 of Schedule II to the State Lands Regulations 1995 says "A non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land." Assignment from a private holder is a different route, but it needs the Minister's consent, and consent is withheld on undeveloped land. See buying as a foreigner.
  • Budget the seller's tax before you agree a price. Capital gains tax on a disposal by an individual is the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration; for companies, partnerships and trustees it is the higher of 25% or 10%. The Gambia Revenue Authority sets a 15-day return deadline, and the tax must be paid before the deed can be registered. A D24,000 exemption and private-residence and agricultural reinvestment reliefs exist — ask GRA or your lawyer which applies.
  • Agents are not licensed here. There is no licensing regime and no professional register for estate agents in The Gambia. Agents are, however, reporting entities under the AML/CFT Act 2012 supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit, and consumers can complain to the GCCPC. There is no statutory client-account or escrow rule, so if you want purchase money held in a lawyer's client account, you must insist on it in the contract — nothing compels it. See are estate agents regulated in The Gambia?

One further piece of context for owners: the National Land Policy 2026–2035 was launched by President Barrow on 22 December 2025. It proposes Certificates of Customary Ownership, conversion with compensation, and digitalisation of land records. It is policy, not law, and none of it changes your position today — but it is the direction of travel.

Questions people actually ask

Which area on the coast is the best buy?

Nobody can answer that from published data, because none exists — there is no Gambian index of land values, yields or transacted prices by settlement. What can be answered is which areas carry which documented risks. On the published evidence, Cape Point has the best-defended shoreline, Kololi and Senegambia the most exposed, and Sanyang the largest single unknown in the shape of the port.

Is my plot inside the Tourism Development Area?

If it lies between Kotu and Kartong and within 800 metres inland of the high water mark, yes. That land is leased State land in the custody of the Gambia Tourism Board under the Gambia Tourism Board Act 2011. Whether a specific plot falls inside the band has been litigated before, so get it confirmed in writing rather than taking a vendor's word.

Can I apply for TDA land at the moment?

No. The Ministry of Tourism and Culture instructed the Gambia Tourism Board on 23 January 2025 to place a temporary hold on all new applications for land within the Tourism Development Area, pending an audit. We found no source showing that hold lifted as at 31 August 2026.

Is there a legal setback from the high water mark?

No. The World Bank stated on 28 February 2026 that no formal setback zones or planning restrictions exist yet in The Gambia. The Integrated Coastal Zone Management Bill, which would create them, is still in legal implementation at the National Environment Agency. The 800 m Tourism Development Area band is a tenure control, not an erosion setback.

Which stretch of coast is eroding fastest?

The World Bank puts erosion on parts of the tourism corridor at up to 4.7 metres a year, and grades Kotu Stream to Senegambia as high economic risk from 2040 and 2070, and Fajara to Kotu Stream as high by 2070. A one-million-cubic-metre beach nourishment in 2003 disappeared in about five years. Cape Point, with revetments and six groins, is the stretch where defences have worked.

Does a development permit protect me from demolition?

No. The Minister for Lands said in November 2025 that a development permit is only one step towards building a house, and that obtaining one does not stop the Government from moving people who have occupied public places. A permit is not title.

Will I be compensated if my building is demolished?

There is no precedent you can rely on. After the Salagi demolitions of April 2025 the Minister for Lands ruled out compensation entirely in November 2025. Compulsory acquisition under the Land Acquisition and Compensation Act, Cap. 57:06, is a separate route with its own compensation rules.

Is Sanyang still a quiet beach village?

It is the site of a US$1 billion deep-sea port, with the foundation stone laid on 5 July 2025, a 30-year concession to the Gambia Ports Authority and Türkiye's Albayrak Group, and a six-year build now running about a year late on pre-construction. On 25 August 2026 the Ministry of Lands warned against unauthorised development in the port area. Anyone describing Sanyang only as a quiet eco-lodge beach is describing 2019.

Can a non-Gambian be allocated a plot on the coast?

Not by government allocation. Paragraph 2 of Schedule II to the State Lands Regulations 1995 states that a non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land. Taking an assignment of an existing lease from a private holder is a different route, but it requires the Minister's written consent, which is withheld where the plot is undeveloped.

Are the prices in this guide what people actually paid?

No. They are asking prices from MyKunda listings in August 2026. The Gambia has no public register of transacted sale prices, so no published figure anywhere is a record of what a plot sold for.

How far is each area from the airport?

Free-flow driving distances to Banjul International Airport at Yundum, computed on 31 August 2026, run from about 14 km and 18 minutes for Brusubi to about 37 km and 49 minutes for Kartong. Kololi is about 18 km and 21 minutes, Brufut about 18 km and 22 minutes, Cape Point about 24 km and 25 minutes. Real journeys through Serrekunda take longer.

Is the electricity and water reliable?

Not currently. NAWEC reported a 26 MW shortfall in April 2026 with 52% of supply imported, and a 50-day emergency import deal expired around 15 August 2026 with peak demand at about 140 MW. National electricity access was 69% in 2024. On water, NAWEC said in June 2026 that supply cannot meet demand and that no major treatment plant had been commissioned in over 17 years; a new plant is being built at Tujereng.

Is sand mining a problem where I am buying?

It is documented at Kartong, where the National Environment Agency approved a quarry in 2022 against village opposition; at Batokunku, where youths protested against a mining agreement in October 2025; at Gunjur, where night mining on beach dunes was reported in August 2024; and at Sanyang, reported in April 2026. Sand removal near a beach is one of the mechanisms by which the beach goes.

Do I need an environmental impact assessment to build near the beach?

Possibly. A residential layout under two hectares normally falls in the National Environment Agency's Class C, which does not require a full assessment — but Class A expressly includes major structures within 150 metres of the high water mark, which does. NEA fees are D1,000 for screening, D5,000 to D25,000 for processing, and an approval fee of 1% of development cost.
