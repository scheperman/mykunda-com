Inheritance and wills for Gambian property

AC Awa Camara 15 August 2026 10 min read

The hardest property problems in The Gambia are not sales — they are estates. A house bought in one country and inherited under the law of another, with heirs on three continents and a deed in a drawer, is how a family asset becomes a family dispute. This guide explains which law applies to your property and what to do now.

Three systems, one country

The Gambian legal system is genuinely tripartite: English common law and equity together with statute, customary law applied by tribunals, and Sharia administered by the Cadi Court system. The Constitution is explicit that the laws of The Gambia include "the Sharia as regards matters of marriage, divorce and inheritance among members of the communities to which it applies".

Which one governs your estate is not something you choose casually at the end. It follows from who you are, what the property is, and — crucially — what documents exist.

The Cadi Court route

Section 137 of the Constitution establishes Cadi Courts, composed at first instance of a panel of the Cadi and two other scholars of the Sharia qualified to be a Cadi or Ulama, and gives them jurisdiction to apply the Sharia in matters of marriage, divorce and inheritance where the parties or other persons interested are Muslims. A party dissatisfied with a decision may appeal to the Cadi Appeals Panel.

Two points that surprise people. First, that jurisdiction is not exclusive — the constitutional intention is that it may be exercised concurrently by other courts, and the Mohammedan Law Recognition Act (Cap 6:04), which has never been expressly repealed, allows those courts to hear matters relating to civil status, marriage, succession, donations, testaments and guardianship. Taking a case to a Sharia court is a matter of choice, not compulsion. Second, the Cadi system is not new: the court was constituted in 1905 under British rule and is one of only a handful of Muslim court systems of its kind in the world.

Under Sharia, shares are fixed rather than discretionary. In one reported Gambian case, the Cadi Appeals Panel upheld a distribution in which the wife took one-eighth and a son and daughter shared the residue, with the male taking double the share of the female. A related principle matters for houses specifically: property capable of being partitioned without harm must be partitioned among the heirs, rather than settled by paying one heir off in money. A single villa with five heirs is a hard thing to partition, and that is precisely where estates stall.

The statutory route

Under the general law, a person may make a will directing how their property is to be distributed — but freedom of testation is not absolute. Forced-heirship principles protect the entitlements of spouses and children, so a will that disinherits them is exposed to challenge. Where there is no will, the court appoints an administrator, and the estate is distributed under the rules of intestate succession once debts, taxes and other obligations are settled.

Either way, the practical mechanism is a grant of probate (with a will) or letters of administration (without one) through the Probate Registry. Until that grant issues, nobody can lawfully sell, transfer or mortgage the house. That is why estates freeze.

Customary land is the hardest case

Land held under customary tenure is the most exposed. Customary inheritance practice in rural areas has tended to favour male heirs, particularly in the distribution of land, and disputes go first to the district tribunal with review by the regional governor — a slow route. Where customary and statutory claims overlap on the same plot, the estate can be contested for years.

The single most effective protection is to have converted the holding into a properly registered title in the owner's own name long before it is ever an estate. See freehold, leasehold and customary land explained.

What to do now — a checklist

  1. Register the property in your own name. An estate cannot pass what the register does not say you own. A deed in a cousin's name "for convenience" is the most common and most expensive mistake in Gambian diaspora property.
  2. Make a Gambian will for your Gambian property, drafted by a Gambian lawyer, even if you already have a will at home. A foreign will can often be recognised, but it has to be proved and interpreted here, which adds cost, delay and room for argument.
  3. Say explicitly which system you intend to apply, and take advice on whether that intention can be given effect given who you and your heirs are. Do not leave it to be argued after the funeral.
  4. Name an executor who is actually in The Gambia, or who can practically act here.
  5. Think about partition before it is forced. If the estate is one house and there are several heirs, decide in advance whether it is to be sold and divided, held jointly under a written agreement, or left to one heir with others compensated elsewhere.
  6. Keep the file complete and known. Deed, survey plan, receipts, rates payments, transfer-tax evidence, and the lawyer's name, in one place your family can find. Half of all estate disputes are really document disputes.
  7. Review after every life event — marriage, divorce, a child, a new property, a move between countries.

If you are the one inheriting

  • Do not pay anyone, agree anything, or accept a share until you have seen the deed and know which system governs.
  • Instruct a Gambian lawyer on behalf of the heirs jointly if you can — separate lawyers for each heir is how estates become litigation.
  • Establish whether any Area Council rates, transfer taxes or utility arrears attach to the property. They follow the property, not the deceased.
  • Get the grant of probate or letters of administration before anything else. Nothing else is possible without it.
  • Expect it to take longer than you are told.

Frequently asked questions

Which law governs inheritance of property in The Gambia?

One of three: statutory law through the High Court and Probate Registry, Sharia through the Cadi Courts where the parties or persons interested are Muslims, or customary law through district tribunals — particularly for land held under customary tenure. The Constitution recognises all three.

Do I need a Gambian will for my Gambian property?

Strongly advisable. A foreign will can often be recognised but must be proved and interpreted in The Gambia, which adds cost, delay and room for dispute. A Gambian will drafted by a Gambian lawyer, naming an executor who can act locally, is far cleaner for your heirs.

How are shares divided under Sharia inheritance in The Gambia?

Shares are fixed rather than discretionary. In one reported Gambian case the Cadi Appeals Panel upheld a distribution giving the wife one-eighth with a son and daughter sharing the residue, the male taking double the female share. Property capable of partition without harm must be partitioned among heirs rather than settled in money.

What happens to a Gambian house if the owner dies without a will?

The court appoints an administrator and the estate passes under intestate succession once debts and taxes are settled. Nothing can be sold, transferred or mortgaged until a grant of probate or letters of administration issues — which is why estates freeze, sometimes for years.
