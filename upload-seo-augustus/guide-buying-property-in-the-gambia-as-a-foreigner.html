Buying property in The Gambia as a foreigner

AC Awa Camara 15 August 2026 9 min read

The Gambia is one of West Africa's most welcoming places to buy property — but the process looks different from Europe or North America. Here's how it actually works for a foreign or diaspora buyer.

Can foreigners own property in The Gambia?

Yes. Non-citizens can own property in The Gambia, and many of the coast's homes and plots are bought by diaspora Gambians and international investors. There is no citizenship test of the kind you find in some neighbouring countries. The key distinction is not who you are but what kind of title the property carries — freehold, leasehold or customary land — which we cover in detail in our title guide.

Two pieces of legislation shape nearly every purchase. The State Lands Act 1990 vests land in Banjul and Kombo Saint Mary — the capital and the built-up coast — in the State, other than land already held in fee simple, and provides for it to be granted on leases with an initial term of 99 years. That is why a large share of what is sold as ownership along the Kombo coast is legally a long State lease rather than freehold. Outside those designated areas the Lands (Regions) Act applies: land is vested in the regional authorities, customary law governs occupation by indigenes, and the Act sets explicit conditions on when a non-indigene may occupy land, caps the interest a non-indigene may acquire, and requires approval for tenancies longer than three years. A deed that cuts across those rules can be voidable.

In practice: buying a villa or a serviced plot in Kololi, Bijilo or Brufut is legally straightforward for a foreigner. Buying farmland or a family plot upriver under customary tenure is a different transaction with different rules, and needs a lawyer who has done it before.

The single most important rule: never buy on a handshake. Always confirm ownership and title in writing, through a lawyer, before any money changes hands.

The step-by-step process

  1. Find the property — search listings, ideally with a verified title, and shortlist a few.
  2. View it — in person or by video if you're abroad. A good agent will walk the property on a live call for you.
  3. Agree a price — most prices on the coast are quoted in US dollars and are negotiable.
  4. Instruct a lawyer — they run a title search at the Lands Office and confirm there are no disputes or competing claims.
  5. Sign a sales agreement — with a deposit, usually held by the lawyer.
  6. Transfer & registration — the balance is paid, the deed is transferred and registered in your name.
For diaspora buyers, the whole process can be done remotely with a trusted local lawyer and agent — but the title search is the one step you should never skip or rush.

Doing it from abroad

Most international buyers never need to fly in until they choose to. Between video viewings, remote title verification and a partner agent who handles documents and visits on the ground, you can get a long way before you travel. What you should insist on:

  • An independent lawyer — yours, not the seller's;
  • Written confirmation of title type and a clean title search;
  • Clear, itemised costs before you commit (see our costs guide).

MyKunda tip: Listings with a Verified badge have already had their title deed and ownership documents reviewed by a qualified specialist — a strong first filter when you're buying from overseas.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying customary land without understanding it — family or community land can have multiple claimants.
  • Paying in full before registration — stage payments through a lawyer protect you.
  • Skipping the survey — make sure the boundaries on the ground match the documents.

There is no general annual property tax in The Gambia, but Area Councils levy rates on developed property and arrears attach to the property, not the seller. The Gambia Revenue Authority rolled out the first phase of a Rental Compliance System in October 2025, so letting income is tracked far more closely than it used to be. The market you are buying into

  • Remittances are the backbone. The Central Bank of The Gambia recorded US$775.6 million of remittance inflows in 2024 — on its own figures around a third of GDP, and on World Bank measure a fifth to a quarter. A large share of that money ends up in land and building.
  • The economy is growing. Growth of roughly 5.3 – 5.7% is projected for 2026, led by tourism, construction, trade and financial services.
  • Inflation is back in single digits — 7.9% across 2025 and 7.0% in April 2026, after several hard years.
  • Tourism keeps recovering — 233,113 arrivals in 2025, which is what underpins holiday-let demand on the Kombo strip.
  • Borrowing locally is expensive. The Central Bank's policy rate stands at 14%, with the standing lending facility at 15% — which is why almost everyone buys cash or on a developer instalment plan.

Where these figures come from: Central Bank of The Gambia (policy rate, inflation, remittances), the World Bank's Gambia country overview (growth), the Gambia Revenue Authority (tax rates) and government tourism figures for 2025. Last checked August 2026. Rates and tax rules change — treat them as the starting point for your lawyer's advice, not as a quote.
