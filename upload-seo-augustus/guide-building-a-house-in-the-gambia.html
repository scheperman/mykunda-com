Building your own house in The Gambia

AC Awa Camara 15 August 2026 11 min read

Most diaspora buyers do not buy a finished house in The Gambia. They buy a plot and build — usually in stages, usually from abroad, usually over several years. This guide covers the permit, the money, the contractor and the ways self-builds go wrong.

The legal starting point

Development in The Gambia is controlled by the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990 (Act No. 1 of 1991). It establishes a Planning Board and a Planning Authority for each planning district, provides for national, divisional, development and local plans, and devotes an entire part of the Act to development control — including enforcement notices, stop notices, revocation of a development permit, appeals to the Minister, and offences and penalties.

The practical meaning is short: building without a permit is not a paperwork oversight, it is an offence, and the Authority has the statutory power to stop your site and revoke your permit. Applications for the Greater Banjul area go through the Department of Physical Planning, which sits under the Ministry of Lands, Regional Government and Religious Affairs at The Quadrangle in Banjul.

The build sequence that works

  1. Verify the title first. Everything below assumes the plot is genuinely yours. If you have not done the searches, stop here and read how to verify land title in The Gambia.
  2. Get drawings from an architect. You can use an architect outside The Gambia, but building in a tropical climate has its own techniques, regulations and specifications, and the materials differ from what a European practice will specify by habit.
  3. Get the plan approved by Physical Planning. Take at least three copies of your plans and the area of your land. The fee is assessed on the square metres of the house. Note that the law requires a permit for your fence and the boys' quarters as well, not only for the main house.
  4. Build the fence before anything else. It secures the site and it fixes your boundary on the ground before a single block of the house is laid — the alternative is discovering half way up that a corner of your bungalow sits on the neighbour's plot. Build it exactly to the approved plan, and check the minimum legal setback where a road separates two plots with the Lands Office.
  5. Build the boys' quarters next. On a self-build it gives you lockable rooms for materials and tools from day one. A shipping container on site does the same job and can be sold on afterwards.
  6. Get a bill of quantities. A quantity surveyor working from your drawings tells you what quantity of material the structure needs. It usually stops short of finishing items — fans, sanitaryware, fittings — which you price yourself at the suppliers.
  7. Build, then apply for completion. Keep the approved drawings on site; inspections are against them, not against what you decided to do later.

What the whole thing costs

General building construction in The Gambia runs at roughly 300 to 500+ per square metre in hard currency — call it D26,000 to D48,000 per m² — depending on design and the quality of materials. On that basis:

100 m² two-bedroom bungalow≈ €30,000≈ €50,000+
150 m² three-bedroom≈ €45,000≈ €75,000+
220 m² four-bedroom villa≈ €66,000≈ €110,000+

Add to that: the plot, the architect, Physical Planning fees, the fence, the boys' quarters, a borehole and tank if there is no mains water, a generator or solar with battery if you want power that does not blink, and the connection charges. A fair planning rule is that the house itself is roughly two-thirds of what you will actually spend to be able to live in it.

Tax point: withholding tax on contracts is 10% for a resident contractor and 15% for a non-resident. It is your obligation as the payer, not the builder's, and it should be written into the contract rather than discovered at the end.

Choosing between the two contracts

You have two realistic routes, and they carry very different risks.

Whichever you pick, have the contract drawn up by a Gambian conveyancing solicitor and signed by both parties, and tender it to several contractors before you sign. Do not accept a verbal price.

The front-loading trap

Gambian builders will often try to load most of the cost of the construction into the early stages of the bill of quantities. Resist it. Finishing is the most expensive phase, and if a contractor walks away mid-build you want the unspent money still to be yours.

Insist on a payment schedule tied to completed, inspected stages — foundation, ring beam, roof on, plastered, finished — with the largest tranches at the end, and hold a retention of five to ten per cent for a defects period after handover. Rain finds every mistake in the first wet season; you want money still owing when it does.

Controlling a build you cannot see

  • Employ a store keeper. Materials in and materials out, recorded daily. Every delivery signed for. This single measure removes the most common source of loss on a Gambian site.
  • Employ a watchman on a recommendation only. Photocopy his ID card, know where he lives, confirm he is resident in The Gambia, do not give him room access, and count the steel rods at the start and end of each day with him.
  • Buy in bulk, collect in batches. Large suppliers will often hold paid-for cement until you need it — keep the receipt. Caution: only do this with a large, reputable company, and understand that if the company fails, your prepayment goes with it.
  • Ask for dated photographs at every stage, including the reinforcement before it is poured. What is buried cannot be inspected later.
  • Appoint someone independent of the contractor to sign off each stage. A relative with a stake in keeping the peace is not an inspector.

Five ways self-builds go wrong

  1. Starting before the permit. A stop notice mid-build costs more than the permit ever would.
  2. Sending money in lumps against nothing. Pay for completed work, not for promises and not for goodwill.
  3. Fence last instead of first. Boundaries are cheap to fix on paper and expensive to fix in concrete.
  4. Budgeting the structure and forgetting the services. Water, power, gate, drive and drainage are not extras.
  5. No written contract. Nothing else on this list is recoverable without one.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a building permit to build a house in The Gambia?

Yes. Development is controlled by the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990, and plans must be approved by the Department of Physical Planning. The law also requires a permit for your fence and boys’ quarters, not only the main house. Building without one is an offence and the Planning Authority can issue a stop notice or revoke the permit.

How much does it cost to build a house in The Gambia?

General building construction runs at roughly 300 to 500+ per square metre in hard currency — about D26,000 to D48,000 per m² — depending on design and materials. A 150 m² three-bedroom house is therefore around €45,000 for a standard finish and €75,000 or more for a higher one, excluding the plot, fees, fence, water and power.

How much is a bag of cement in The Gambia?

A 50 kg bag of 42.5R Portland cement was priced at around D525 to D575 in early 2026, about €6 to €7. Cement is the most volatile material price on a Gambian site, with a long-term upward trend, so re-price it the week you buy.

Should I use a turnkey contractor or a labour-only contractor?

A labour-and-materials (turnkey) contract suits owners who cannot be present; a labour-only contract gives you control over materials but needs someone you trust on the ground. Either way, have the contract drawn by a Gambian conveyancing solicitor, tender it to several contractors, and tie payments to completed, inspected stages with the largest tranches at the end.
