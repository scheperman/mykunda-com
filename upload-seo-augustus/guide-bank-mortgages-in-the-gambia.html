Bank mortgages in The Gambia — rates, conditions and eligibility

AC Awa Camara 15 August 2026 7 min read

If you're used to buying property with a mortgage in Europe or North America, The Gambia will feel very different. Bank mortgages exist — but they're rare, expensive, and come with conditions that rule them out for most foreign buyers. Here's an honest look at what's available.

Does mortgage finance exist in The Gambia?

Yes, but only just. The Gambian mortgage market is extremely small by international standards. The institutions that offer housing finance are:

  • Home Finance Company of The Gambia (HFC) — the country's dedicated mortgage lender, incorporated in 2001, licensed by the Central Bank as a non-bank financial institution and a subsidiary of Trust Bank since 2015. It specialises in home purchase loans and equity release against an existing property.
  • Social Security and Housing Finance Corporation (SSHFC) — a government body that provides housing loans, mainly aimed at Gambian nationals and contributors to the national social security fund.
  • Commercial banks — a small number among the country's dozen or so licensed banks, including Trust Bank, GTBank and Ecobank, occasionally offer property-backed lending, but dedicated mortgage products are uncommon and terms are restrictive.

In practice, the total number of active mortgage loans across the entire country is very small — numbering in the low hundreds. The vast majority of property transactions in The Gambia are cash purchases.

Typical terms and interest rates

Start with the price of money. The Central Bank of The Gambia's Monetary Policy Rate is 14% — cut from 16% in February 2026 and held there since, with the standing lending facility at 15% and the required reserve ratio at 13%. Inflation was 7.0% in April 2026, having averaged 7.9% across 2025. Commercial lending sits well above the policy rate, so retail housing loans price in the high teens to low twenties. This is not a distortion; it is simply what credit costs in a small, import-dependent economy.

When a mortgage or housing loan is available, the terms are dramatically different from what a European or American buyer might expect:

TermTypical range
Interest rate15 – 22% per year (variable, and moves with the policy rate)
Maximum term10 – 15 years
Loan-to-value (LTV)Maximum 60 – 70%
CurrencyGambian Dalasi (GMD)
Down payment30 – 40% minimum

To put this in perspective: a loan of €100,000 at 18% over 15 years costs roughly three times the original amount in total repayments. At typical European rates (3 – 5%), the same loan would cost roughly 1.3 to 1.5 times the original. The difference is enormous.

A Gambian mortgage is not comparable to a European mortgage. The interest rates alone mean that what feels like an affordable monthly payment can quickly become an extremely expensive way to buy property.

Rates are drifting down rather than up — the policy rate has come off its peak and forecasters expect further easing over the next two years — but nobody should plan a purchase around a rate cut that has not happened.

Can foreigners get a bank mortgage in The Gambia?

In theory, yes. In practice, it is very difficult and almost never happens. Here's why:

  • Income verification — banks require proof of a stable, verifiable local income in Dalasi. Foreign income is hard for them to assess and creates currency risk.
  • Residency — most lenders strongly prefer or require Gambian residency, a valid work permit, or long-term ties to the country.
  • Collateral — the property itself is usually the main collateral, but banks often ask for additional security (e.g. a second property, deposits, guarantors).
  • Title requirements — the property must have a clean, registered title — freehold or state leasehold. Customary land is almost never accepted as mortgage collateral.
  • Currency mismatch — if you earn in euros or pounds but borrow in Dalasi, you carry the full exchange-rate risk. The Dalasi has depreciated significantly over recent decades, which makes this unpredictable.

Bottom line: If you're a foreign buyer counting on a local Gambian bank mortgage to finance your purchase, you will almost certainly be disappointed. The products are designed for salaried Gambian workers, not international buyers.

What about SSHFC housing schemes?

The Social Security and Housing Finance Corporation builds and sells houses in planned estates, sometimes with instalment plans for buyers. These are primarily intended for Gambian nationals who are active contributors to the SSHFC pension scheme. Eligibility criteria typically include:

  • Gambian citizenship or long-term residency;
  • Active SSHFC membership with a contribution history;
  • A demonstrated ability to make regular payments;
  • A clean credit record (where available).

Foreign nationals are generally not eligible for SSHFC housing schemes. These are social housing instruments, not investment-property financing tools.

So how do foreign buyers actually finance property?

If a local bank mortgage is off the table, you still have good options:

1. Cash purchase

The most common approach by far. Save up, negotiate hard, and buy outright. This avoids all interest costs and keeps things simple. Most property prices along the Gambian coast — especially land and compounds — are within reach of a lump-sum purchase for many diaspora and international buyers.

2. Mortgage on a property in your home country

Many diaspora buyers remortgage or take equity release on a property they already own in Europe or the UK, then use the freed-up capital to buy cash in The Gambia. You get the benefit of low European interest rates (3 – 5%) and repay in a currency you earn in.

3. Personal or consumer loan abroad

For smaller purchases — a plot of land, a small compound — a personal loan from your European or UK bank may be enough. Interest rates are higher than a mortgage but far lower than anything available locally in The Gambia.

4. Developer financing

This is the most popular route for foreign and diaspora buyers — and it deserves its own guide. Read our in-depth article: Developer financing in The Gambia.

5. Family pooling

Common in the diaspora community: several family members pool funds to buy a shared property or plot. This works — but document the ownership shares clearly and legally from day one to avoid disputes later.

Key conditions for any route

Whichever way you finance your purchase, some conditions apply no matter what:

  • Clean title is non-negotiable — no lender, and no sensible buyer, should proceed without a confirmed freehold or registered leasehold title. Read our title guide.
  • Independent legal advice — always hire your own lawyer, separate from the seller's.
  • Budget for all costs — transfer duty, legal fees, survey, and registration. See our costs guide.
  • Understand currency risk — the Dalasi-to-euro rate can shift significantly. If you commit to payments in Dalasi over time, you're exposed.

MyKunda tip: Don't let the lack of a local bank mortgage stop you. Most successful foreign buyers in The Gambia finance from their home country, use developer financing, or save and buy cash — and end up better off for it.
