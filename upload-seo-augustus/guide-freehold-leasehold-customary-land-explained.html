Land & title

Freehold, leasehold & customary land explained

AC Awa Camara 15 August 2026 8 min read

Before you fall in love with a plot or a villa, understand one thing above all else: the kind of title it carries. In The Gambia this is where most problems — and most peace of mind — come from.

The three main types

TypeWhat it meansBest for
FreeholdYou own the land outright, with a registered title deed. The strongest form of ownership.Homes & long-term investment
State leaseholdYou hold the land under a lease from the State, typically 99 years. On the built-up coast this — not freehold — is the normal title for registered land.Most coastal plots & homes
Customary / family landLand held under traditional or family ownership, often without a formal individual deed.Lower price — but higher care needed

Those three words carry a fair amount of law behind them. The State Lands Act 1990 vested land in Banjul and Kombo Saint Mary — the capital and the built-up coast — in the State, other than land already held in fee simple, and provided for the State to grant it on leases with an initial term of 99 years, signed on the State's behalf by the responsible minister. Those leases carry covenants and conditions, and non-observance can in principle expose the lease to forfeiture by re-entry. So a great deal of what is sold as ownership on the Kombo coast is legally a long State lease. That is not a problem in itself — 99 years is longer than most buyers' horizons — but you should know which of the two you are buying, and how many years of the term remain.

Outside those designated areas the Lands (Regions) Act governs. Land is vested in the regional authorities, customary law governs occupation by indigenes, and the Act sets conditions under which a non-indigene may occupy land at all, limits the interest a non-indigene can acquire, and requires approval for tenancies of more than three years. Deeds that ignore those provisions can be voidable — the sort of defect that surfaces years later, when it is expensive.

Why it matters so much

The price difference between a clean freehold plot and customary land can be large — and so can the risk. Customary land may have several family members who must all consent to a sale, and disputes can surface years later. Freehold with a registered deed gives you the clearest, most defensible ownership.

This is not a theoretical worry. Research on Gambian land rights finds that around a quarter of the adult population feels tenure-insecure, in both rural and urban areas, and that a recurring source of conflict is exactly the overlap this guide is about: customary land that has also been granted to a third party as a State leasehold. Where customary land is disputed, the first forum is the district tribunal, with regional governors holding power to review its decisions — a slow route you do not want to be on.

A cheap plot with an unclear title is not a bargain. The cost of resolving a dispute — or losing the land — dwarfs any saving.

Buying land that is already on a lease

Because so much coastal land is held on a state lease, many sales are not a sale of the land itself but a transfer — an assignment — of the remaining lease. That is entirely normal, and it is what most "title deed" purchases in Kombo actually are. Three things to check before you commit:

  • How many years are left. A 99-year lease granted in 1998 is a different asset from one granted last year — the remaining term is part of what you are paying for.
  • Consent to transfer. An assignment or sublease of a state lease needs the consent of the Ministry of Lands. A seller who cannot obtain it cannot pass the land to you.
  • The lease conditions. State leases carry obligations — ground rent and, on many plots, a duty to develop within a set period. Unmet conditions travel with the land to the new holder.

How to check what you're getting

  • Ask for the title document and have a lawyer confirm what type it is;
  • Run a title search at the Lands Office to confirm the seller is the registered owner;
  • For customary land, establish exactly who the owners are and get written, witnessed consent from all of them;
  • Commission a survey so the boundaries are documented and pegged.

Converting customary land

In some cases customary land can be formalised into a registered title. It's a process worth exploring with a lawyer if you're buying family land you intend to hold long-term — it turns a higher-risk asset into a much stronger one.

What is changing

The Gambia's Land Policy 2026 – 2035 sets out reforms aimed squarely at the weaknesses above. The headline proposals are formal recognition of customary tenure through Certificates of Customary Ownership, a transparent and participatory process (with fair compensation) for converting customary land into state land, and the registration and digitalisation of all land in the country.

For a buyer today, two practical implications. First, customary land with clear, documented community consent stands to become considerably more secure — and more valuable — if certification arrives. Second, nothing has changed yet: a policy is not a register.
