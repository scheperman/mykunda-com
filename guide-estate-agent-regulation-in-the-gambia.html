Are estate agents regulated in The Gambia?

MK MyKunda Published 25 August 2026 Updated 31 August 2026 22 min read

No. Estate agency in The Gambia is not a licensed profession: there is no licence, no professional register, no minimum qualification and no authority designated to license or regulate agents. That is not the same as no rules at all. Agents carry anti-money-laundering duties supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit, tenancies are governed by the Rent Act 2014 as amended in 2024, and there are places to take a complaint. A Real Estate Agents Bill has been drafted and validated, but as at 31 August 2026 it had not been introduced in the National Assembly. This guide sets out what applies today, what is proposed, and what both sides of a deal should do in the meantime — whether you are dealing with an agent or working as one.

In short

  • There is no licensing regime, no professional register and no designated licensing regulator. The GIABA/FATF Mutual Evaluation of The Gambia (2022) puts it plainly: "No competent authority has been designated for licensing or registering and regulating the real estate sector."
  • But "nobody to complain to" is wrong. Estate agents are reporting entities under the AML/CFT Act 2012, supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit, and consumers can complain to the Gambia Competition and Consumer Protection Commission — though the GCCPC's reach over land transactions is contested.
  • The law tenants and landlords actually need is the Rent Act 2014 as amended by the Rent (Amendment) Act 2024: rent increases limited to 5% every three years, eviction notice of 90 days, and a monthly-payment threshold of D7,000. Enforcement is acknowledged to be weak.
  • The Real Estate Agents Bill was validated at a stakeholder workshop on 29 April 2026. It does not appear among the 15 bills on the National Assembly's bill tracker as at 31 August 2026, and its text has not been published. A presidential election is set for 5 December 2026.
  • No Gambian rule requires an agent to hold your money in a separate client account. Purchase money should go to a Gambian lawyer's client account, and you should insist on it in writing, because nothing compels it.
  • There is no published commission benchmark and no statutory cap, and the number of agents operating is unknown — the FATF evaluation treats that unknown number as a risk factor in itself.

The short answer: no licence exists today

Estate agency in The Gambia is not a regulated profession. Setting up as an agent requires an ordinary business registration under the Companies Act 2013 and the Single Window Business Registry Act 2013, administered by the Companies Department of the Registrar General at the Attorney General's Chambers and Ministry of Justice. Beyond that there is no examination, no professional indemnity requirement, no client-account rule and no register in which you can look a name up.

This is not a contested reading. The 2022 GIABA/FATF Mutual Evaluation Report of The Gambia records that "no competent authority has been designated for licensing or registering and regulating the real estate sector". The same report treats "the unknown number of operators" in the sector as a risk factor — so nobody, including the government, knows how many agents are trading. No published count of Gambian estate agents exists, so we do not give one.

The core land statutes long pre-date the present market. What is new is that a draft law aimed squarely at agents now exists.

What does apply to agents today

Read the sentence above carefully: there is no licensing regulator, and no conduct regulator for estate agency as a profession. There are still legal duties, and there are still places to go.

  • Anti-money-laundering law binds agents. Section 41(b) of the AML/CFT Act 2012 makes estate agents reporting entities — designated non-financial businesses and professions, in the international vocabulary — supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit. That means customer due diligence, record-keeping and suspicious transaction reporting. The 2022 evaluation notes that of the DNFBP sectors, real estate agents were the only ones that had received any supervision at all.
  • Consumer law offers a route, with a real caveat. The GCCPC administers the Competition Act 2007, the Consumer Protection Act 2014 and the Essential Commodities Act 2015, and takes consumer complaints. But the Consumer Protection Act 2014 contains no definition of "goods" and no reference to land or immovable property; Foroyaa reported in December 2023 that the Act does not cover victims of real estate transactions, and a consolidating bill validated in May 2025 concedes gaps. Use the route — but do not plan a purchase on the assumption that consumer law will get your money back.
  • The criminal and civil courts are open. Fraud, forged documents and obtaining by false pretences are police matters. Breach of contract and recovery of a deposit are civil claims. Gambian property cases have been reported to run over three years.
  • Tenancy law is enacted and in force — the Rent Act 2014 as amended, covered below.

What none of that gives you is a register to check, a licence to withdraw, a conduct code to enforce, or a compensation fund if an agent takes your deposit and disappears. That gap is real, and it is what the draft bill is aimed at.

Why the government is moving now

Two official assertions are doing most of the work in the public argument for reform. Both are statements by officials rather than audited or published statistics, and both should be read that way.

  • The D500 million figure. The Minister responsible for lands, Hamat N. K. Bah — styled "Minister of Local Government and Lands" on his own ministry's site, and "Minister for Lands, Regional Government and Religious Affairs" elsewhere — told The Point on 14 July 2025 that over D500 million had been "stolen from innocent Gambians by unscrupulous real estate agents", and described the sector as a "controlled mess". This is a ministerial estimate. No audit, court record or published dataset behind it has been located.
  • The share of court cases that involve land. Two different figures are in official circulation. Ebrima Sissawo, Permanent Secretary (Technical) at the lands ministry, said at the validation workshop that approximately 75 per cent of court cases in The Gambia are land-related (reported by The Point on 4 May 2026 and repeated in The Standard and Gunjur Online on 5 May 2026). The Minister put the figure at 67 per cent on 21 May 2026. We report both rather than choosing between them. No published judicial statistics corroborate either.

The frauds officials describe are not exotic: fake or forged leases, the same plot sold to two or three buyers, plots that do not exist on any survey, and deposits taken for land the seller has no right to transfer. Note that this list is the Minister's July 2025 description of the problem — it is not a list of published provisions of any bill.

Because no intermediary is licensed, the remedies available to a buyer are the ordinary ones: a criminal complaint, or a civil suit. Both are slow, both are expensive, and neither reliably recovers money that has already moved. That is why verifying the title before money moves matters more here than in a market with a licensing regime.

What the Real Estate Agents Bill would introduce

The text of the bill has not been published anywhere we could find — not in the Government's policy repository, not on the National Assembly's site, not at the Ministry of Justice or the lands ministry. So what follows is limited to what officials have said publicly, and it is thin.

On the record, the bill is described as providing for mandatory registration and licensing of estate agencies and agents, professional standards attached to holding a licence, and transparency and accountability rules covering how transactions are conducted. It was drafted at the Attorney General's Chambers and presented at the validation workshop by Kissima Bittaye, Principal Legislative Draftsperson. That account rests on a single reliable report — The Point, 4 May 2026 — and nothing contradicts it, but it is one source, not three.

You will find the bill's purpose quoted elsewhere. We do not quote it here: the text is unpublished, so a quotation from it is not something you could check.

What we could not verify

The licensing categories, the fees, the penalties for unlicensed practice, which body would hold the register and supervise it, and what happens to agents already trading when a law commences are all unknown. The full text is unpublished. Anyone quoting figures, fee scales or commencement dates for this bill is guessing. We will not publish them until the text is.

Where the bill stands

Status as at 31 August 2026: draft bill, validated at stakeholder level on 29 April 2026. Not introduced in the National Assembly. Not passed. Not assented. Not law. Text unpublished.

The validation workshop was held on Wednesday 29 April 2026 at the NaNA Conference Hall in the Kanifing area — not in Banjul city, as it is sometimes described. The draft was presented alongside the communication strategy for the National Land Policy. Government agencies, regional and district authorities, traditional leaders, a real estate association, civil society organisations and development partners took part, with support from the World Bank-funded WACA project.

The basis for saying the bill is not before parliament is direct: the National Assembly's bill tracker, retrieved on 31 August 2026, lists 15 bills for the current session, and no Real Estate Agents Bill is among them. The order papers for the Second Ordinary Session of 2026 show no such item, and there is no press record of an introduction.

The calendar matters. The Independent Electoral Commission has set the presidential election for 5 December 2026. A bill that has not been introduced by the end of August faces a compressed legislative timetable before that date, and legislative programmes routinely slip across an election. We are not predicting an outcome — but a reader who assumes licensing is imminent should know how little of the parliamentary process has happened.

The process was publicly criticised, and the criticism is stronger than "under-representation". Dave Manneh, writing for Securing Futures: Land Rights Action Collaborative in The Standard and Gunjur Online on 5 May 2026, alleged total exclusion — "the communities that the policy concerns received no invitation", and "communities in active land dispute: absent" — and further alleged that Cabinet had removed the restitution provision from the validated National Land Policy. Those are contested allegations, not findings, and we report them as such. They matter here because objections of that kind are the sort that slow a bill down.

The law that is actually in force: the Rent Act 2014, as amended

Almost all coverage of Gambian rent reform is about bills. The more useful fact is that the Rent Act 2014 has already been amended, and unlike the draft bills the amendment is law. It is the part of this page most likely to be useful to you today.

The Rent (Amendment) Bill, 2023, sponsored by Hon. Madi M. K. Ceesay, passed its consideration stage on 15 March 2024, was passed by the National Assembly on 21 March 2024, and is recorded as "Assented" on the National Assembly's bill tracker. The Gambian press generally calls the result the Rent Act 2024. The operative law is the Rent Act 2014 as amended by the Rent (Amendment) Act 2024.

What the amendment doesThe position in force
Rent increasesLimited to 5%, and no more often than once every three years — a minimum 36-month interval between increases.
Eviction noticeExtended to 90 days.
Monthly rather than block paymentThe threshold below which rent is payable monthly rather than in six-month blocks was raised from D3,000 to D7,000 a month.
EnforcementAcknowledged as weak. Hon. Fatoumatta Jawara, in August 2024: "We have approved the Rent Act; however, enforcement is still a challenge."

Two practical consequences. If you are renting, an agent who tells you the rent is going up 20% this year, or who gives you a month to leave, is describing something the Act does not permit — the remedy is the rent tribunal, and you will need your written tenancy agreement to use it. If you are letting property out, these are the rules your tenancy agreement and your agent's practice have to fit, and you should read them alongside the tax position in our guide to renting out property in The Gambia.

The Rent and Tenancy Bill 2025

A bill of this name exists. The Attorney General's Chambers and Ministry of Justice published a page titled "Validation Workshop on the Rent and Tenancy Bill 2025", which establishes the name, the year and that a validation workshop took place.

That page is the only source we could find, and it is blocked to automated retrieval, so we could not read its contents. No independent reliable reporting of the bill was located. Descriptions of its contents circulate, and so do direct quotations from it. We could not verify any of them against a second source, so this guide does not repeat them. We are not going to tell you what is in a bill we have not seen described by more than one reliable source.

It is also not before parliament: like the Real Estate Agents Bill, it does not appear on the National Assembly's bill tracker as at 31 August 2026. Anyone tracking agent licensing should watch both, because it is not settled which instrument would carry it — but that is as far as the evidence goes.

The wider reform package

The agents bill sits inside a broader programme, and one piece of it is already done rather than pending:

  • The National Land Policy 2026–2035 was launched on 22 December 2025 by President Adama Barrow at the Sir Dawda Kairaba Jawara International Conference Centre, and is published in the Government's policy repository. It is still often described as forthcoming; it is not. The policy proposes Certificates of Customary Ownership, conversion with compensation, and digitalisation of land records. A policy is not a statute — it binds nobody by itself.
  • A specialised land court is proposed, not established. The Justice Minister, Dawda A. A. Jallow, has said government is reviewing land legislation and may consider establishing a special court for land issues.
  • Land information systems. Work on a National Land Information System has been reviewed by stakeholders, with WACA support covering land information systems, development control legislation and institutional capacity.
  • Development control legislation — the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990 — falls inside the WACA workstream, though no source we found confirms a formal revision process has begun.

A new national housing policy is sometimes listed alongside these. We could find no reliable source for it, so we leave it out.

What none of this changes for you today

Until a bill is passed and commenced, the practical position for a buyer, a tenant or a seller is unchanged:

  • A registration certificate proves that a company exists. It proves nothing about the land, the seller's right to sell it, or the agent's honesty.
  • There is no register in which you can look up whether an agent is licensed, because there are no licences.
  • No professional body will compensate you if an agent misappropriates a deposit, and none is proposed in anything published.
  • A trade association exists — the FATF evaluation identifies a real-estate self-regulatory body, referred to as AREC, which it says "does not have any legal backing and its membership is voluntary". We could not establish its full name from a reliable source. Membership of it tells you nothing about a firm's competence and creates no recourse for you.
  • Independent verification of the title before money moves remains the only protection that does not depend on the other side's good faith.

If a bill passes, expect a transition period rather than an overnight change, and expect the first licences to take time. Nothing about a bill in draft justifies relaxing a check you would do anyway.

Where your money sits: there is no client-account rule

This is the single most important gap for a buyer, and it is worth stating without hedging: no Gambian statute or regulation requires an estate agent to hold your money in a separate client account, and there is no escrow requirement, no bonding requirement and no deposit protection scheme. If an agent puts your deposit in the company's ordinary trading account and spends it, no regulator will stop them, because none supervises agents' conduct or handling of client money.

What you can do about it:

  • Pay into a Gambian lawyer's client account, and put that in the contract. This is a private arrangement between you, the lawyer and the seller — not a legal protection that anyone will enforce on your behalf. Insist on it precisely because no rule compels it.
  • Never pay an agent in cash for anything you cannot afford to lose, and never pay to a personal account.
  • Get a receipt that names the property, the payment's purpose, and what happens to the money if the sale does not complete. Silence on that last point is the problem, not an oversight.
  • Register the sale contract. The Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01, defines an instrument widely enough to cover a contract, and registration at the Deeds Registry of the Registrar General's Department gives priority from the date of registration. For an instalment purchase this is a real, available protection. See our guide to developer financing for how this plays out on off-plan purchases.

The same applies to a seller instructing an agent: if the buyer's money passes through the agent, you carry the same risk from the other direction.

Where to take a complaint about an agent

"There is nobody to complain to" is a common thing to read about The Gambia, and it is wrong. None of these routes is a licensing regulator, and none of them will withdraw an agent's right to trade — but they exist and they are used.

RouteWhat it coversHow to reach it
Financial Intelligence UnitThe AML/CFT supervisor for estate agents as reporting entities under the AML/CFT Act 2012. Handles suspicious transactions and money-laundering compliance. It does not adjudicate your dispute or recover your money.Via the Government of The Gambia's Financial Intelligence Unit page.
Gambia Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (GCCPC)Consumer complaints under the Competition Act 2007, the Consumer Protection Act 2014 and the Essential Commodities Act 2015. Its jurisdiction over land and immovable property is contested — see the caveat above.info@gcc.gm · +220 446 6940 · 36 Kotu East, KMC, off the Bertil Harding Highway. Practical sequencing: if money has gone and you think it was taken dishonestly, the police complaint and a lawyer come first, in that order, and both should happen in days rather than weeks. A consumer complaint runs alongside; it does not replace them.

Questions worth asking any agent today

Since no licence exists to ask for, these are the questions that actually separate a serious operator from a card and a phone number:

  • Who holds the title to this plot, and may I see the document itself rather than a photograph of it?
  • Is it a state lease, a deemed leasehold with a certificate of occupancy, customary land, or one of the pre-1945 freeholds — and if it is a lease, how many years remain? Our guide to tenure explains why the answer changes everything.
  • Are you acting for the registered owner, or for someone who says he is the owner? Can I see your written instruction?
  • Has the Minister's consent to the transfer been applied for, and is the plot developed? On an undeveloped state lease, consent to assign is withheld until the building covenants are met.
  • Is your fee a fixed amount or a percentage, who pays it, and does it carry VAT?
  • Where does my money sit until completion, in whose name is that account, and what happens to it if the sale fails?
  • Who is your AML compliance officer? An agency that has never heard of the obligation is telling you something.
  • Will you put every one of these answers in writing?

An agent who cannot answer these in writing today will not be improved by a licence tomorrow.

If you are the agent: setting up and running an agency

There is no licence to apply for and no professional examination to sit. There are still obligations, and they are the ones that catch new agencies out.

ObligationThe position as at August 2026
Business registrationRegister under the Companies Act 2013 and the Single Window Business Registry Act 2013, through the Companies Department of the Registrar General at the Attorney General's Chambers and Ministry of Justice. This is an ordinary company or business registration — there is no estate agency licence, and no source supports any particular timescale for completing it.
Corporation taxThe Gambia Revenue Authority levies the higher of 27% of net profit or 1% of turnover — 2% of turnover where the accounts are unaudited. The turnover floor matters for an agency with thin margins in a slow year.
VAT15%, with compulsory registration once taxable supplies reach D2,000,000 in a tax year. Agency commission from a VAT-registered agency carries VAT; residential rent itself is exempt. Quote fees inclusive or exclusive, but say which.
Withholding tax on contractsGRA publishes two different sets of figures. The GRA withholding tax brochure and the 2025 Budget Speech give 10% for non-resident contractors, 5% for resident contractors on government public works, and 8% for other resident suppliers, consultants and contractors. GRA's own domestic FAQ page still shows 10% resident / 15% non-resident. Confirm the rate with GRA in writing before you contract.
AML/CFT dutiesYou are a reporting entity under s.41(b) of the AML/CFT Act 2012, supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit: customer due diligence, record-keeping and suspicious transaction reporting. Occasional-customer identification applies above D200,000; records are kept for five years above D5,000. Appoint a compliance officer and be able to name them.
Client moneyNo statutory client-account rule, no escrow requirement, no bonding. If you want clients to trust you with money, route it through a lawyer's client account and say so in your terms — the market will not compel it, so it is a differentiator.
CommissionNo published benchmark, no statutory cap and no default rule on who pays. It is a matter of negotiation, in writing, before you start work.

GRA can be reached on +220 422 3641 or at info@gra.gm. On the transaction taxes your clients will meet — stamp duty at 2% on an assignment, paid by the buyer, and the seller's capital gains tax — see our guide to the real cost of buying property.

What we could not verify

No reliable Gambian source publishes a commission benchmark for estate agency, and no statutory scale or cap exists. We are not going to invent a range. If you see "typically 3–5%" quoted anywhere for The Gambia, ask the publisher for their source. We also could not establish the full name or legal standing of the trade association referred to as AREC beyond the FATF evaluation's statement that it has no legal backing and voluntary membership; and no source records what would happen to trading agencies if a licensing law commenced.

How The Gambia compares: Ghana's Real Estate Agency Act 2020

The nearest published model for what a Gambian licensing law might look like is Ghana's Real Estate Agency Act, 2020 (Act 1047), assented on 29 December 2020. It is worth knowing because it shows what the Gambian gaps look like when they are filled:

  • It establishes a Real Estate Agency Council — a named body with a register, which The Gambia does not have.
  • Licensing is mandatory: a person may not provide real estate agency services unless licensed as a broker or an agent under the Act.
  • A licensee must open and operate a separate client's account and is barred from commingling client funds with the firm's own. This is precisely the protection absent in The Gambia.
  • Unlicensed practice carries 5,000 to 10,000 penalty units and/or five to ten years' imprisonment.
  • The Council may prescribe a scale of fees, but the Act sets no percentage cap on commission.

Nothing in Ghana's Act applies in The Gambia. It is a comparator, not a source of rights. We have deliberately published no comparison with Senegal: the professional-card regime commonly cited for Senegalese agents could not be traced to any official or primary source, only to property portals, so we are leaving it out rather than repeating it.

How to follow this

The checkable primary source for whether either bill has moved is the National Assembly's bill tracker, which lists the bills of the current session and their stage — that is how we established that neither bill is before parliament, and how the Rent (Amendment) Bill's "Assented" status is recorded. Ministry announcements come from the lands ministry and from the Attorney General's Chambers and Ministry of Justice; draft legislation and policy appear in the Government's policy repository. Reliable Gambian coverage appears in The Point, The Standard, Foroyaa and Kerr Fatou. We update the status line on this page as the position changes; it was last checked on 31 August 2026.

Questions people actually ask

Are estate agents licensed in The Gambia?

No. There is no licence, no professional register, no minimum qualification and no authority designated to license or regulate the sector. The GIABA/FATF Mutual Evaluation of The Gambia (2022) states that no competent authority has been designated for licensing or registering and regulating the real estate sector.

So there is genuinely nobody to complain to?

No — that is too strong. There is no licensing or conduct regulator, but estate agents are reporting entities under the AML/CFT Act 2012 supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit, consumers can complain to the GCCPC, fraud is a police matter, contract disputes go to the civil courts, and tenancy disputes go to a rent tribunal.

Has the Real Estate Agents Bill become law?

No. It was validated at a stakeholder workshop on 29 April 2026, but it does not appear among the 15 bills on the National Assembly's bill tracker as at 31 August 2026. It has not been introduced, passed or assented, and its text has not been published.

When will it pass?

Nobody has published a timetable, and we will not guess at one. A presidential election is set for 5 December 2026, which compresses the parliamentary calendar for a bill that has not yet been introduced.

Does an agent have to keep my deposit in a separate client account?

No. There is no statutory client-account, escrow or bonding rule for estate agents in The Gambia. Pay purchase money into a Gambian lawyer's client account and put it in the contract — but understand that this is a private arrangement you have negotiated, not a protection the law compels.

What commission should I expect to pay, and who pays it?

There is no published Gambian benchmark and no statutory cap, and no default rule on whether the buyer or the seller pays. It is entirely a matter of negotiation. Agree the amount, the trigger and who pays in writing before the agent starts work, and ask whether VAT at 15% is on top.

What are my rights as a tenant right now?

The Rent Act 2014 as amended by the Rent (Amendment) Act 2024 limits rent increases to 5% and to once every three years, requires 90 days' eviction notice, and sets a D7,000 monthly threshold below which rent is payable monthly rather than in six-month blocks. A serving member of the National Assembly said in August 2024 that enforcement remains a challenge, so expect to have to assert these rights rather than rely on them.

How do I check that an agency is a real company?

Through the Companies Department of the Registrar General at the Attorney General's Chambers and Ministry of Justice, which administers registration under the Companies Act 2013 and the Single Window Business Registry Act 2013. Remember what that check does: it confirms a company exists. It says nothing about the land or the agent's right to sell it.

Is an agent under any legal duty at all?

Yes. Under s.41(b) of the AML/CFT Act 2012 an estate agent is a reporting entity supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit, with customer due diligence, record-keeping and suspicious transaction reporting obligations. It is a fair question, and a revealing one, to ask who an agency's AML compliance officer is.

I want to set up an agency. What do I actually need?

An ordinary business registration under the Companies Act 2013 and the Single Window Business Registry Act 2013 — there is no licence to obtain. Then corporation tax at the higher of 27% of net profit or 1% of turnover (2% if unaudited), VAT registration once taxable supplies reach D2,000,000, withholding tax on contracts, and AML obligations to the Financial Intelligence Unit.

Is there a professional body worth joining?

The FATF evaluation identifies a real-estate self-regulatory body referred to as AREC, which it says has no legal backing and voluntary membership. We could not establish its full name from a reliable source. Membership creates no recourse for a client and no compensation scheme.

How many estate agents are there in The Gambia?

Nobody knows. The FATF evaluation expressly cites the unknown number of operators as a risk factor. Figures in the hundreds circulate, but no source supports a count.

Does the Rent and Tenancy Bill 2025 regulate agents?

A bill of that name exists and was the subject of a Ministry of Justice validation workshop, but that single government page is the only source we could find and it could not be read. We are not describing its contents. It is not on the National Assembly's bill tracker.

Would a licensing law have protected my deposit?

Not on its own. Nothing published about the Gambian draft mentions a compensation fund. Ghana's Act 1047 shows the shape of a stronger regime — a council, mandatory licensing and a compulsory separate client account — but that is Ghanaian law, and it gives you no rights in The Gambia.
