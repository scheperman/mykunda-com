Sending money to The Gambia — what it really costs

MK MyKunda Updated 31 August 2026 18 min read

On a property purchase the transfer is not an afterthought — it is a line item worth thousands, and most of what it costs is not the fee. This guide uses the World Bank's own measurements of the one Gambian corridor it prices, the Central Bank's foreign exchange policy, and the AML/CFT Act 2012, to set out what a transfer actually costs, what you have to declare, what your bank is required to ask you, and what happens when a seller wants the money out again.

In short

  • The Gambia has exactly one corridor in the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide database: the United Kingdom to The Gambia. In the latest published quarter — prices collected 7–22 August 2025, published as RPW Issue 54 in September 2025 and still the most recent as at 31 August 2026 — the 22 quotes for a £120 transfer ranged from 8.05% to 19.92% of the amount sent, averaging 12.06%. At £300 the average was 10.90%.
  • That is well above the same quarter's global average of 6.36% and Sub-Saharan African average of 8.46%.
  • Most of the cost sits in the exchange-rate margin rather than the fee — but the fee is only part of the cost, and you should compare the rate as well. In both quarters measured in 2025 the zero-fee provider was the cheapest or second-cheapest quote on the page.
  • No bank is surveyed on this corridor, so there is no published bank benchmark to compare against. Ask your own bank, in writing, for the all-in figure including intermediary deductions.
  • More than US$7,500 in cash or bearer instruments must be declared to Gambian customs, on arrival and on departure, under s.48 of the AML/CFT Act 2012.
  • The dalasi has floated freely since 1986, but the Central Bank keeps a power to restrict foreign exchange transactions temporarily, and no published rule establishes a right to take sale proceeds out. Money transfer operators may not deal in foreign exchange inside The Gambia, so an outward transfer has to go through a licensed commercial bank.

The fee is not the cost

The World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide database records, for each provider, the advertised fee, the exchange rate given, the interbank reference rate on the day, and therefore the exchange-rate margin — the gap between the real market rate and the rate you are offered. It is the only independent, repeated measurement of what it costs to send money to The Gambia.

Two things about that dataset matter before you read any number from it. First, The Gambia has exactly one corridor in it: United Kingdom to The Gambia. There is no measured France–Gambia, Spain–Gambia or United States–Gambia price. Second, prices are collected at two fixed sizes — the USD 200 and USD 500 denominations, which in the United Kingdom are surveyed as £120 and £300. They are not £200, and nothing in the database measures a purchase-sized sum.

The most recent published collection is Q3 2025, priced between 7 and 22 August 2025 and published as RPW Issue 54 in September 2025. It is still the latest published quarter as at 31 August 2026 — the World Bank did not collect this corridor in Q2 2025.

Notice the shape of the table: the fee ranges from nothing to £7.90, but the margin ranges from 7.22% to nearly 17%. On this corridor the margin is the bigger number almost every time.

The fee is only part of the cost; compare the rate as well. What matters is the number of dalasi that arrive, not the number of pounds that leave.

Neither statement survives the data. The 8.3–9.9% band described 5 of the 23 quotes collected in the first quarter of 2025 and 3 of the 22 collected in the third — it is the cheap tail, not the typical case. And in both of those quarters the zero-fee provider was the cheapest or second-cheapest quote measured. A zero fee is not a trap in itself; an unexamined rate is.

What that costs on a property purchase

The percentages look small until you apply them to a purchase price. The table below applies the measured corridor costs to purchase-sized sums. Read it as a warning about scale, not as a quote: Remittance Prices Worldwide measures £120 and £300 transfers only, and nobody publishes what a €80,000 transfer to The Gambia costs.

Amount sentAt 8.05% (cheapest measured)At 12.06% (corridor average)At 19.92% (most expensive measured)
€10,000€805€1,206€1,992

Costs do fall as the amount rises. On the same August 2025 collection the corridor averaged 12.06% at £120 but 10.90% at £300, and the cheapest quote fell from 8.05% to 7.55%. The direction is clear and the lesson holds: on a purchase-sized transfer, negotiate the rate, and ask what lands in the recipient account rather than what leaves yours.

A second correction. The previous version of this page compared those costs with "1.5% (bank transfer, keenly priced)", and built its headline saving on it. That figure had no source, and we have deleted it. Remittance Prices Worldwide surveys no banks at all on this corridor — all 22 quotes in August 2025, and all 23 in February–March 2025, are money transfer operators. There is no published bank benchmark for sending money to The Gambia.

What we could not verify

What a bank transfer to The Gambia actually costs. No Gambian bank publishes an inward or outward SWIFT tariff or an all-in foreign exchange spread, and the World Bank's corridor survey contains no banks. Correspondent-bank deductions — charges taken by intermediary banks in the payment chain — are real, but we could find no published figure or typical range for this corridor from any reliable source. The honest instruction is the one to give your own bank and the receiving Gambian bank in writing: what is the all-in cost, including the exchange rate you will apply and any intermediary deductions, and how many dalasi will be credited?

Reference rates to check against

The Central Bank of The Gambia publishes daily Exchange Rates for Valuation Purposes. The live rate is in the header of this page, taken from that feed; we deliberately do not print a rate into the text of an article, because a typed rate goes stale within days and then contradicts the header.

Three things to understand about that reference rate before you use it as a stick to beat a quote with:

  • It is a mid-market benchmark, not a dealable price. Under the Central Bank's Foreign Exchange Policy of December 2023 the official rate is a volume-weighted US dollar mid-rate (§4.4), and it is used "for Government accounting and customs purposes" (§4.5).
  • The euro and pound figures are derived, not traded. The same policy provides that cross rates for other currencies are "calculated based on independent sources such as Bloomberg/Reuters". The dollar is the measured leg; everything else is arithmetic.
  • Every retail customer pays a spread. No bank and no money transfer operator gives anyone the mid-rate. A spread is not by itself evidence of overcharging — the question is how big it is. On the measured corridor it ran from 7.22% to nearly 17% in a single fortnight.

Rates also move enough within a month to matter on a purchase. Across August 2026 the Central Bank's valuation rate for sterling moved by about 2.7% between its low and its high in that one month. On €80,000 that swing is larger than most people's negotiated saving on the margin, which is an argument for agreeing the rate and the date, in writing, rather than the percentage alone.

Rules for property money specifically

  • Pay from your own account into the named seller's or lawyer's account. Never a third party, never a different name, never "my brother will collect it". A payment that does not match the parties on the deed is the single hardest thing to explain afterwards.
  • Keep every transfer receipt. They are your proof of payment if the deed is ever disputed, and your evidence of funds if you are asked — and under the AML/CFT Act 2012 the receiving bank must keep its own records for five years on any new business transaction above D5,000.
  • Match the transfer to the contract. The sums and dates on your bank statements should reconcile line by line with the payment schedule in the sale agreement.
  • Fix the currency of the deal. An instalment plan quoted in dalasi is a multi-year bet on the exchange rate. In the year to February 2026 the Central Bank reported the dalasi down 11.53% against the euro and 4.36% against the dollar. Ask for the total to be stated in hard currency in the contract.
  • Send in fewer, larger transfers where the contract allows. Every transfer pays a margin, and on this corridor the margin is the whole game.
  • Never send money before the title checks are complete. A recovered transfer is a rare event. Start with how to verify land title, and check what consents the deal needs before any deposit moves.

If you are buying: getting the money in

How purchase money should reach a lawyer's client account

Money for a purchase should go by bank transfer into a client account held in the law firm's own name at a bank licensed by the Central Bank — the Central Bank lists 11 licensed banks — and not into a personal account, an agent's account, or an account in a name that differs from the firm or from the seller on the deed. Before you send anything:

  • Ask the firm for written confirmation of the account name, bank and number on its own letterhead, and confirm the details by a second channel — a phone call to a number you already had, not one in the email.
  • Ask expressly whether it is a designated client account and whether your money will be held separately from the firm's own money.
  • Agree in writing what releases the money: which document, signed by whom, at what stage.

You should insist on this, because no Gambian rule compels it. We could not locate any published rule of a Gambian professional body requiring a lawyer to hold client money in a separate account, and the full text of the Legal Practitioners Act 2024 is not retrievable, so we will not tell you what it says. Nor is there any statutory escrow rule for estate agents. Where a firm agrees to hold your money in a client account, that is a private contractual arrangement you have negotiated — a good one, and worth negotiating — not a legal protection the state provides. The same caution applies to any developer or agent offering to "hold" a deposit: see developer financing and off-plan and estate agent regulation.

What the receiving bank will want

A large inward transfer from abroad, from someone who is not a customer, into an account that is about to buy land, is exactly the pattern Gambian anti-money-laundering law is written around. Expect to be asked for your passport, proof of address, the signed sale agreement, and a documented trail showing where the money came from in the originating account. Expect delay if any of it is missing: under s.26(1) of the AML/CFT Act 2012 a reporting entity that cannot obtain satisfactory evidence of identity must refuse the relationship outright. The thresholds are set out in the next section but one.

The rest of the completion budget

Transfer cost is not the only percentage on a purchase. Stamp duty on an assignment or conveyance is 2% of the amount and is the buyer's, and the seller's capital gains tax has to be paid before the deed can be registered, which makes it your completion risk too. Those are set out in full in the cost of buying property in The Gambia. If you are not Gambian, read buying as a foreigner before you commit money: the constraints there are on the transaction, not on the transfer.

Carrying cash in or out — and the two official figures

Two Gambian official sources give different thresholds for declaring currency, and both are current.

SourceThresholdDirectionConsequence

Treat US$7,500 as the safe threshold, in both directions. It is the figure in the Act, it is the lower of the two, and it is the one carrying a criminal penalty. The Act also allows the Central Bank to prescribe a different amount, which may be how the two figures came apart — but we could not find any prescribed amount published, so we cannot tell you the higher figure has replaced the lower one.

None of this makes cash a sensible way to pay for property. It is undeclarable in the sums involved, it leaves no evidence that you paid, and a bank will still want to know where it came from when it is deposited.

Estate agents are inside this regime. Under s.41(b) a real estate agent is a reporting entity and must report to the Financial Intelligence Unit where a transaction relates to the buying or selling of real estate for a client "under suspicious circumstances". So while The Gambia has no licensing regulator for agents, it is wrong to say agents are unregulated altogether — they carry a statutory reporting duty, and your transaction sits within it. What that does and does not give you is a separate question.

What we could not verify

Whether the AML/CFT Act 2012 has been amended since enactment. The Central Bank's own banking supervision page publishes only the 2012 Act, and its copy is a scanned document; we could not obtain a certified consolidated text, the Gambia Financial Intelligence Unit's website did not resolve, and the current GIABA mutual evaluation for The Gambia could not be retrieved. One clause in the scan reads oddly enough to look like a scanning artefact. Treat every threshold above as "as enacted in 2012, and still the only version the Central Bank publishes as at 31 August 2026", and have a Gambian lawyer confirm anything you are relying on.

If you are selling or receiving: getting the money out

The exchange regime, and its qualifier

The Central Bank's Foreign Exchange Policy of December 2023, §3, states that the exchange rate regime "shall be free floating in accordance with Section 64 of the CBG Act 2018", and that the free-floating regime "has been in existence since 1986". Section 64 provides that where the Government does not declare an external value for the dalasi, exchange rates are market-determined.

The qualifier matters as much as the rule, and it is usually left out. The same policy provides that the Central Bank may temporarily restrict the purchase, sale, holding or transfer of foreign exchange in order to achieve its policy objectives or to avert a foreign exchange crisis. Any such restriction runs for an initial period of not more than twelve months and may be extended for a further period not exceeding twelve months with the approval of the Bank's Board. That is a standing statutory power, not a hypothetical: it is a fact to weigh before you park capital in Gambian property.

Can sale proceeds be taken out?

What we could not verify

Whether you have a right to repatriate the proceeds of a property sale. This is not established either way on the public record. No published Central Bank instrument states a right to repatriate, and none states a restriction. The Foreign Exchange Policy contains no surrender requirement and no capital-account restriction — but it also contains the temporary-restriction power described above, and we could not obtain a machine-readable text of the CBG Act 2018 (the copy on cbg.gm is a scan) or any published investment guarantee. Nor could we establish what a Gambian bank will require in practice before releasing a large outward transfer, or whether a foreign-currency account is available to a non-resident. Do not read "free float" as an assurance that money can always leave. Put the question to your Gambian bank and to the Central Bank in writing before you buy, not after you have sold.

The route out is a bank, not an operator

Whatever the answer on repatriation, the route is fixed. Foreign Exchange Policy §5.2 provides that international money transfer operators and fintech companies "are just conduit of remittance transfers and shall not engage in the buying and selling of foreign exchange currencies in the domestic market". Sale proceeds therefore have to be converted and sent by a licensed commercial bank — not a wallet, not a transfer app, not a bureau arrangement. Open the bank relationship, and satisfy its identity and source-of-funds requirements, long before completion.

What a seller must settle first

  • Capital gains tax. The seller pays it. The Gambia Revenue Authority charges an individual the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration, and a company, partnership or trustee the higher of 25% of the gain or 10% of the consideration. The return is due within 15 days of the transaction, and the tax has to be paid before the deed can be registered — which is why an unpaid seller's tax stalls the buyer's completion as well. A D24,000 exemption and reinvestment reliefs exist; take advice on whether you qualify.
  • Stamp duty and registration. Duty is 2% on an assignment or conveyance and falls on the buyer, but no deed can be registered unless it has been duly stamped, so it is in the seller's interest that it is done.
  • Ground rent and Area Council rates. Annual ground rent on a state lease is payable to the Department of Lands before 31 January, and Area Councils levy an annual rate on developed property. Get your position in writing before you market.

What will block your sale

  • Missing ministerial consent. On a state lease, regulation 22(l) of the State Lands Regulations 1995 bars a lessee from assigning or otherwise alienating without the Minister's written consent first obtained — and consent is refused where the plot has not been developed in accordance with the covenants. If you are selling an empty plot, establish that position before you accept a deposit.
  • An unregistered or unstamped chain of deeds. Perfect the paperwork before you list, not during the sale.
  • An inherited property without authority to sell. Heirs need a grant of probate or letters of administration, and for a Muslim estate a Cadi Court distribution order, before a sale can complete: see inheritance and wills.
  • A buyer who cannot evidence their funds. If the money cannot be explained to the receiving bank, it will not arrive. Ask early where the buyer's money is coming from and through which bank.

If you are being paid in instalments

A multi-year instalment price stated in dalasi transfers the currency risk to you if you think in euros or pounds, and to the buyer if you do not. Fix the currency in the contract, state the conversion rate and the date it is taken from, and say who bears the transfer cost. The same applies to rent collected on a let property and remitted abroad — see renting out property in The Gambia for the tax on that income.

Why this matters to the market

Diaspora money is not a side story in Gambian property. The Central Bank's Monetary Policy Report of February 2026 put remittance inflows at US$872.14 million for 2025, 12.4% up on 2024, in an economy of about 2.4 million people. The practical conclusion is unchanged, and better founded: on a corridor that averages 12% and reaches 20%, every point you take off the cost of moving money is a point that arrives as blockwork instead of as somebody's margin — and on a purchase-sized sum, no one is measuring the price for you.

Questions people actually ask

How much does it cost to send money to The Gambia?

On the only corridor the World Bank prices — the United Kingdom to The Gambia — the 22 quotes collected between 7 and 22 August 2025 ranged from 8.05% to 19.92% of a £120 transfer and averaged 12.06%. At £300 the average was 10.90%. In the same quarter the global average was 6.36% and the Sub-Saharan African average 8.46%. Those figures were published as RPW Issue 54 in September 2025 and are still the latest as at 31 August 2026.

What is the cheapest way to send money to The Gambia?

On the measured data, an operator with a small fee and a tight exchange rate: the cheapest August 2025 quote charged £1.00 with a margin of 7.22%, for a total of 8.05%. But nothing in that dataset measures a purchase-sized transfer, and no bank is surveyed at all. For a purchase, ask two or three banks and operators in writing what the all-in cost is and how many dalasi will be credited, and compare those answers rather than the advertised fees.

Is a "no fee" transfer a trick?

Not necessarily. The fee is only part of the cost and you should compare the rate as well — but in both quarters measured in 2025 the zero-fee provider was the cheapest or second-cheapest quote on the corridor. A zero fee is a reason to check the rate, not a reason to walk away.

Does it get cheaper if I send more?

Yes, a little, on the measured sizes. The corridor averaged 12.06% at £120 and 10.90% at £300 in August 2025, and the cheapest quote fell from 8.05% to 7.55%. Remittance Prices Worldwide does not measure anything larger, so what happens at €50,000 is a matter for negotiation with your bank, not for published data.

What is the exchange rate for the Gambian dalasi?

The Central Bank of The Gambia publishes daily Exchange Rates for Valuation Purposes, and the live rate appears in the header of this page. We do not print a rate into the article text because it would be stale within days. Remember that the official rate is a volume-weighted US dollar mid-rate used for government accounting and customs, with euro and sterling calculated as cross rates — it is a benchmark, not a price anyone will deal at.

How much cash can I bring into The Gambia?

There is no cap, but there is a declaration duty. Section 48 of the AML/CFT Act 2012 requires anyone arriving in or leaving The Gambia with more than US$7,500, or the dalasi equivalent, in cash or bearer negotiable instruments to disclose it to a customs officer at the airport, sea port or land border post, on penalty of a fine of not less than D10,000. The Gambia Revenue Authority's customs brochure gives US$10,000 on importation. Treat US$7,500 as the safe figure, in both directions.

Can I pay for a property by mobile money?

No. The Central Bank's Regulation for the Provision of Mobile Money Services of October 2011 caps a retail transfer at D10,000. Mobile money is for small local payments once you are on the ground.

How should the purchase money reach my lawyer?

By bank transfer into a client account held in the firm's own name at a bank licensed by the Central Bank, with the account details confirmed on the firm's letterhead and verified by a second channel before you send. Insist on it: we could not find any published Gambian rule that compels a lawyer or an agent to hold client money separately, so a client account is an arrangement you negotiate, not a protection the state supplies.

What will the Gambian bank ask me for?

Identity and source of funds. Under the AML/CFT Act 2012 a bank must identify an occasional customer above D200,000, must establish the purpose, origin and ultimate destination of a cash transaction above US$10,000, must identify the beneficial owner where it believes a third party is behind the transaction, must apply enhanced checks and establish source of wealth for a politically exposed person, and must refuse the relationship outright if it cannot obtain satisfactory identity evidence. In practice: passport, proof of address, the sale agreement, and a clear trail from the originating account.

Does the estate agent have to report my purchase?

Under s.41(b) of the AML/CFT Act 2012 an estate agent is a reporting entity and must report to the Financial Intelligence Unit where a transaction relates to buying or selling real estate for a client under suspicious circumstances. So there is no licensing regulator for agents, but there is a statutory reporting duty. A straightforward, documented purchase is not what that provision is aimed at.

Can I take the proceeds of a sale out of the country?

That is not established either way on the public record, and we will not tell you it is guaranteed. The dalasi has floated freely since 1986 under s.64 of the CBG Act 2018, and the Central Bank's Foreign Exchange Policy of December 2023 contains no surrender requirement and no capital-account restriction — but neither does it state a right to repatriate, and the Bank keeps a power to restrict foreign exchange transactions temporarily for up to twelve months, renewable once. Whatever the answer, the route is fixed: only a licensed commercial bank may buy and sell foreign exchange in The Gambia, not a transfer operator or a wallet. Ask your Gambian bank and the Central Bank in writing before you buy.

Are there capital controls in The Gambia?

There is no published exchange-control approval requirement for an outward transfer that we could find, and the regime is a free float. That is not the same as saying nothing can restrict you: the Central Bank holds a standing statutory power to restrict the purchase, sale, holding or transfer of foreign exchange to avert a foreign exchange crisis, for an initial twelve months, extendable once. Plan on the basis of the power existing.

What tax must I settle when I sell?

Capital gains tax, and it falls on you as seller: the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration for an individual, or 25% and 10% respectively for a company, partnership or trustee. The return is due within 15 days of the transaction and the tax must be paid before the deed can be registered. Whether a tax clearance certificate is required before an outward transfer of the proceeds is not established — ask the Gambia Revenue Authority and your bank.

Which remittance figure should I believe?

Say which one you are using. The Central Bank's gross inflow measure was US$872.14 million for 2025 and US$775.6 million for 2024; its own balance of payments table records workers' remittances of US$507.8 million for 2024; and the World Bank records personal remittances received of US$529.05 million for 2024. They measure different things and are roughly 50% apart.
