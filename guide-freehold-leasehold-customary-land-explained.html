Land & title

Freehold, leasehold & customary land explained

MK MyKunda Updated 31 August 2026 18 min read

Before you fall in love with a plot or a villa, understand one thing above all else: the kind of title it carries. In The Gambia there are four answers to that question, not three — and the one a great many Kombo sellers actually hold is the one least often named.

In short

  • The operative statute is the State Lands Act, Act No. 1 of 1991 (Cap. 57:02). It vested Banjul and Kanifing Municipality in the State. The coast most buyers are looking at — Kombo North, Kombo South and Kombo Central — became State land later, with effect from 1 March 1994.
  • Freehold is not a choice you can make. New freeholds have not been granted since 1945 and cannot be created without the approval of the legislature. Surviving grants are essentially Banjul only. A document offered to you as a new coastal freehold is a warning sign, not a prize.
  • There is a fourth category the usual three-box explanation leaves out: deemed leasehold. Under s.7(1) of the Act, a customary occupier in an area when it was designated is deemed to be a 99-year lessee of the State — usually evidenced by a certificate of occupancy rather than an executed lease. This is the real status of much Kombo land.
  • Selling, subdividing, subletting or mortgaging a state lease needs the Minister's written consent, and reg. 22(l) of the State Lands Regulations 1995 says that consent "shall not be granted if the lessee has not developed the demised premises". An undeveloped plot can be legally unsellable.
  • At the end of the 99 years, renewal is discretionary, not a right: Schedule III clause 3 says the Minister may grant a further term, provided there is no subsisting breach.
  • The National Land Policy 2026–2035, launched on 22 December 2025, proposes Certificates of Customary Ownership and a digital register. Nothing has changed yet — a policy is not a register.

Four kinds of title, not three

Most explanations of Gambian land give you three boxes: freehold, leasehold, customary. That is the wrong shape. It presents a closed historical class as a live option, and it leaves nowhere to put the status a large share of Kombo sellers actually hold.

One correction worth making before you read anything else on this subject. The statute is very widely cited as the "State Lands Act, 1990" — including, until this revision, by us. The bill was titled 1990 and the catalogue record carries that year, but the Act itself is Act No. 1 of 1991, and that is how the State Lands Regulations 1995 cite their own parent Act. The World Bank, CIFOR-ICRAF and the Government's own 2024 systems assessment all date it 1991. If a document or a lawyer's letter uses the 1990 name, it is not necessarily wrong-headed, but it is imprecise.

Freehold: a closed class, not an option

This is the single most misleading thing published about Gambian land, and we published a version of it ourselves. Freehold is routinely presented as the strongest and most desirable form of ownership, and therefore as something a buyer should look for. It is not available to look for.

Freehold "was discontinued in 1945 and can no longer be granted without approval from the National Assembly" (CIFOR-ICRAF, 2024). The World Bank's land governance assessment records the same thing from the other end: "In 1945, the Lands Act converted colonial land into state land and prohibited the creation of freeholds without the consent of the House of Representatives", and that prohibition remains under current law. Surviving freeholds are concentrated in Banjul and account for roughly a tenth of the country's land.

The State Lands Act preserved them rather than abolishing them — s.5(2)(a) provides that on designation "all land in the area shall, excluding such land as is held in fee simple, vest in and be administered by the State". That is why freehold still exists at all. It is also why it cannot be created afresh in Kombo: the land there is already vested in the State.

Practical consequence: if a seller or an agent offers you "freehold" land at Brufut, Sanyang, Gunjur, Kartong or anywhere else on the coast, treat that word as a red flag and ask to see the instrument. What you are being shown is a state lease, a deemed lease, a certificate of occupancy, an Alkalo document — or a forgery. Have a Gambian lawyer read it before any money moves.

Which land is State land, and since when

The old version of this guide said the Act vested "Banjul and Kombo Saint Mary — the capital and the built-up coast". That was wrong in a way that matters, so here is the corrected position.

Kombo Saint Mary is not the coast. It corresponds broadly to what is now Kanifing Municipality — Serrekunda, Bakau, Fajara, Kanifing. The Act vested Banjul and that area directly.

Everything further out came later. Section 5(1) of the Act gives the Minister power to designate further areas by notice, and that power was exercised over the Kombos: Kombo North, Kombo South and Kombo Central were designated as State lands by Legal Notice No. 2 of 1994, with effect from 1 March 1994. The World Bank records the same event ("In 1994, Kombo North, South, and Central were designated as state lands"); the Legal Notice number and the commencement date come from a 2026 legal commentary published by Gunjur Online, which quotes the instrument. That covers Brufut, Tanji, Sanyang, Gunjur, Kartong, Sukuta, Brikama, Bijilo and the rest of the strip MyKunda's listings sit in.

Three consequences follow, and each of them changes how you read a seller's paperwork:

  • Before 1 March 1994, that land was Regions land. Whatever the seller's predecessor held then, they held it under the Lands (Regions) Act and customary law — not as a state lessee.
  • The Lands (Regions) Act stopped applying there on designation. Section 5(2)(b) disapplies it inside a designated area. So it is simply wrong to reason about a Sanyang or Gunjur plot using the Regions rules. That Act still matters for the history of the plot, and for land further inland and upcountry.
  • Leases already running at designation were converted. Section 6 converted a subsisting lease into a term of "ninety-nine years from its original date". A lease document reading "99 years" may therefore be a re-badged older grant with far less than 99 years left. Check the date the term began, not the number.

Deemed leasehold — the status of much Kombo land

When an area is designated, the people already living on and farming it do not become trespassers. Section 7(1) of the State Lands Act makes them lessees by operation of law: a person occupying land under customary tenure or from year to year at the date of designation is deemed to be a lessee of the State, holding for 99 years, and may apply for a formal lease.

That is the fourth box, and in the Kombos it is a very full one. A deemed lessee has a real statutory interest — but often no executed lease to show for it. What they typically hold is a certificate of occupancy, issued by the District Authority and countersigned by the Alkalo, and perhaps a sketch or survey plan.

Two things follow that a buyer needs to know:

  • A deemed lease is not registrable. Only land that is surveyed, mapped and given a unique identity can be registered at the Deeds Registry. CIFOR-ICRAF states the exclusion plainly: that "excludes all customary land and deemed leasehold land". For exactly the plots where a buyer most wants a central check, the central check returns nothing.
  • Deemed status is provable but contestable. Because it depends on who was in occupation at designation, disputes about it are disputes about facts and family history, not about a filed document. That is one reason Gambian land conflicts run so long.

If you are buying deemed-leasehold land, the sensible route is to have the seller perfect it into an executed lease before completion, or to price and structure the deal on the basis that you will have to do it yourself. See how to verify land title in The Gambia for the document-by-document checks.

Why it matters so much

The price gap between a clean, executed state lease and undocumented family land can be large — and so can the risk behind it. Customary and family land may have several people whose consent is needed, and a claim can surface years after you have built.

This is not a theoretical worry. Prindex's survey wave of 2018–19 found that 24% of adults in The Gambia — roughly a quarter, in both rural and urban areas — report feeling insecure in their tenure; Prindex's 2024 round records a further nine-point increase. The recurring pattern behind those numbers is the overlap this guide is about: land held under customary tenure that has also been granted to somebody else as a state leasehold.

The scale shows up in the courts, though the two official figures do not agree. The Minister for Lands put land-related matters at 67% of court cases (21 May 2026); a stakeholder validation workshop report of 4 May 2026 put it at around 75%. Take either and the conclusion is the same.

Where customary land is disputed, the first forum is the District Tribunal — there is one in each of the country's 41 districts, presided over by the Seyfo, and its jurisdiction covers both ownership and possession of land. What happens above that level is less clear than it is usually stated. CIFOR-ICRAF says regional governors hold power to review tribunal decisions. ActionAid's study of land grievance mechanisms describes something looser: governors convening ad hoc mediation panels, with matters that cannot be settled "referred to the Courts". We have only one source for the formal review power, so we do not state it as settled.

A cheap plot with an unclear title is not a bargain. The cost of resolving a dispute — or losing the land — dwarfs any saving. Gambian property cases have been reported running over three years.

Buying land that is already on a lease

Because so much coastal land is held on a state lease, many sales are not a sale of the land itself but an assignment of the remaining lease. That is entirely normal, and it is what most "title deed" purchases in Kombo actually are. What to establish before you commit:

  • The original date and the remaining term. A 99-year lease granted in 1998 is a different asset from one granted last year — and, because of s.6, a document that says 99 years may run from a much older date. Ask for the date the term began, not the length of it.
  • What use the lease was granted for. Terms vary. Residential leases run 99 years; other uses run roughly 21 to 50 years depending on sector policy and ministerial discretion; leases in the Regions are capped at 50 years. Note that the specimen lease in the 1995 Regulations is a flat 99 years with no differentiation — the instrument and the practice diverge, and CIFOR-ICRAF and the Government's 2024 systems assessment both report the shorter terms. If you are buying anything that is not plain residential, read the term on the actual document.
  • Consent to assign. It is required, and it can be refused. See the next section but one — this is where deals die.
  • The state of the covenants. Ground rent arrears and unmet build obligations attach to the lease, and a lease in breach is exposed to forfeiture by re-entry.

What happens when the 99 years run out

The old version of this guide implied that 99 years is effectively the end of the story because it is longer than most buyers' horizons. That skips the question, and the answer is in the primary text.

Clause 3 of the specimen lease at Schedule III of the State Lands Regulations 1995 reads:

"If the Lessee is desirous of taking a new lease of the demised premises after the expiration of the term hereby granted then the Minister may grant to the Lessee a new lease … for a further term of NINETY-NINE years provided there shall be no subsisting breach of any of the lessee's obligations under these presents."

Read that carefully. The word is may, not shall. Renewal is a discretion exercised by the Minister, not a right held by the lessee — and the discretion is expressly conditioned on the lessee having no subsisting breach. CIFOR-ICRAF reports that in practice renewal "is almost automatic". That is a description of practice, not of entitlement, and practice is not a thing you can enforce.

What that means for a buyer today is less dramatic than it sounds and more important than it looks. Very few buyers will personally reach the end of a term. But it matters for three reasons: an heir or a future buyer will; a lease bought as a re-badged older grant may have far less time left than the document's headline suggests; and a lease sitting in breach — unpaid ground rent, an unbuilt plot, a use it was not granted for — is a lease whose renewal the Minister may lawfully decline. Keeping the covenants clean is not housekeeping. It is what protects the asset's second century.

Consent to sell, sublet, subdivide or mortgage

This is the part of Gambian land law that decides real transactions, and the part most guides mention in a single line. The covenants set out in regulation 22 of the State Lands Regulations 1995 bind every state lessee. Two of them govern what you can do with the land:

  • Reg. 22(l) — dealings. The lessee "shall not subdivide, convey, assign or otherwise alienate the premises or any part thereof by sale, transfer of possession, lease or sublease without the consent of the Minister in writing first obtained and such consent shall not be granted if the lessee has not developed the demised premises in accordance with the covenants in that behalf."
  • Reg. 22(k) — security. The lessee shall not mortgage or create any charge or encumbrance on the premises without the Minister's written consent, first obtained.

The second half of reg. 22(l) is the trap that actually bites, and it is the sentence to take away from this guide. An undeveloped state-leasehold plot may not be lawfully assignable, and may not be mortgageable, until it has been built on. Somebody who bought a bare plot as an investment, intending to sell it on, may find that the law will not let them — and a lender asked to take it as security is being offered collateral it cannot easily realise. That, rather than any story about foreign income being hard to assess, is the best-evidenced reason Gambian lenders are wary of plot lending; see bank mortgages in The Gambia.

Consent is also slow. The World Bank's Doing Business 2020 exercise, on data collected in May 2019, recorded "Application for Ministerial Consent to transfer" as a discrete procedure taking 60 days at a cost of GMD 750 — the longest single step in a Gambian conveyance. Treat that as a 2019 measurement, not a current fee and not a promise about timing.

Two further covenants that come as a surprise to buyers: reg. 22(b) forbids using the land for a purpose other than the one the lease was granted for, so turning a residential plot into a guesthouse or a shop is a change that needs the lessor's permission; and reg. 22(g) forbids development without a development permit under the Physical Planning and Development Control Act.

Ground rent, rates and the duty to build

A state lease is not a one-off purchase. It carries continuing obligations, and default on them exposes the lease to forfeiture.

Ground rent

Regulation 37: "All properties within a designated area shall be charged land rent in accordance with Schedule V." Regulation 40(2) makes it payable in advance before 31 January each year, to the Department of Lands and Surveys. Regulation 40(3) provides that a defaulter is "liable to forfeit the grant of land and the Minister may re-enter".

The last published basis for the amount is Schedule V of the 1995 Regulations: area × a slab rate of D10.00 to D25.00 per 100 m² banded by plot size, multiplied by a total index. That index combines a location component (central 4, tourism development area 5, sub-urban and fringe lower, +1 for a major road, +0.25 for public facilities) and an intensity component (1 for a ground floor, +0.5 for each further storey). The Regulations' own worked example is a 500 m² central residential plot of three storeys at D362.50 a year — in 1995 dalasi.

What we could not verify

What ground rent actually costs today. Regulation 43 allows the schedule to be revised by notice in the Gazette, and thirty-one years of inflation make it near-certain that it has been. We could not locate any published post-1995 revision, and no Gambian body publishes a current ground rent schedule. Treat the 1995 figures as the last published basis and nothing more. Before you buy, ask the seller for the current rent demand and the receipts for the last several years, and have your lawyer confirm the assessed rent with the Department of Lands.

Local rates

There is also an annual local rate on developed property, levied by the Area Council under the General Rate Act 1992 with valuations under the Rating Valuation Act 1987 — this is according to the Local Government Revenue Initiative; we could not obtain the text of the Act itself. "There is no property tax in The Gambia" is not true. Whether unpaid rates follow the property to a new owner is something we could not establish from any published source; ask the Council directly rather than assuming either way.

The duty to build

The covenants include real deadlines. Regulation 22(a) requires a boundary fence within six months of the lease. Regulation 23(a) requires an agricultural lessee to plant within two years. Development itself requires a permit, and under the Physical Planning and Development Control Act a development permit is valid for two years unless renewed. These deadlines are what reg. 22(l) means by "developed in accordance with the covenants" — miss them and you are not merely in breach, you are unable to sell. See building a house in The Gambia.

Customary land in the Regions

Outside the designated areas — so upcountry and inland, not the Kombo coast — the Lands (Regions) Act, Cap. 57:03 applies. It is an old statute (Act No. 16 of 1945, commenced 1 January 1946, amended several times since) and its provisions are frequently misquoted, including by us. Here is what it says, section by section.

ProvisionWhat it actually provides
s.4 — vesting"All Regions' lands are hereby declared to be vested in the Authorities for the Districts in which the lands are situated" — held in trust for the communities. That is the District Authority, the Seyfo's office, not a regional body. Regional Governors have consent, registration and appeal roles; they are not where the land vests. Turning customary or deemed land into a registered lease

Formalisation is possible, and it is the single thing that most improves the value and the safety of family land. There are two distinct routes and they are not interchangeable.

Inside a designated area (Banjul, KMC, the Kombos). A deemed lessee applies under Part III of the State Lands Regulations 1995. In outline: a certificate of occupancy issued by the District Authority and countersigned by the Alkalo or District Authority, clearance from Physical Planning, an application to the Land Administration Board, and then the Minister's approval — resulting in a 99-year lease. (Note that the Lands Commission Act 2007 dissolved the Land Administration Boards established under the State Lands Act and gave the Lands Commission functions including assessing land rent and premium; if you are told the Board still sits, ask which body is actually deciding your application.)

Outside the designated areas. A lease from the District Authority with the Minister's consent, under the Lands (Regions) Act. The statutory term limit here is 50 years, not 99. The World Bank's assessment records that "the process for transferring customary land to leaseholds lacks transparency" — plan for it to take time, and start it before you need it rather than during a sale.

Either way this is lawyer's work, and it is worth doing before you market the land rather than in the middle of a transaction.

How to check what you are getting

The checks differ by tenure, which is why identifying the tenure comes first.

  • Name the document. Gambian sellers hold a recognisable set: Letter of Allocation, Alkalo Transfer, Alkalo Certificate of Ownership, Area Council Transfer, Certificate of Occupancy, Lease, Deed of Conveyance or Assignment, sketch plan approved by Physical Planning (not a hand drawing), survey plan. Each says something different about what is being sold. Have a lawyer say which one you are looking at before you discuss price.
  • Search the Deeds Registry — and know its limits. Deeds affecting land are registered at the Deeds Registry of the Registrar General's Department, in the Ministry of Justice, under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01. It is a deeds register, not a title register: registration gives priority, not title, and it is indexed by the names of the parties, not by parcel. So a search is a name search — and it will return nothing at all for customary or deemed-leasehold land, which cannot be registered.
  • Treat Alkalo paperwork with care. Alkalo documents are evidence of a customary allocation, not registrable title, and they are routinely forged. Magistrate Kanjura B. Sambou of Brikama said on 27 August 2026 that it is "increasingly common for a single plot of land to be sold to several individuals, with each buyer subsequently presenting an Alkalo transfer document as proof of ownership."
  • For family land, identify every person whose consent is needed and get it in writing and witnessed. In the Regions, s.18(1) makes alienation without the required consent null and void — a consent you failed to obtain is not a technicality you can fix later.
  • Commission a survey so the boundaries are documented and pegged, and so the land is capable of being registered at all.
  • Ask for the ground rent receipts and evidence that the build covenants have been met — because without them, consent to assign to you may not be granted.

If you are not Gambian

A portal aimed partly at the diaspora and at foreign buyers should say this plainly, and the old version of this guide said nothing at all.

Government allocation is closed to non-Gambians. Schedule II of the State Lands Regulations 1995 sets the eligibility criteria for the grant of residential land in approved layout areas, and paragraph 2 reads: "A non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land." The same Schedule bars anyone under 21, sets one plot per person as the general rule, and disqualifies anyone already holding more than one plot.

What that Schedule does not do, on its face, is prohibit a non-Gambian from taking an assignment of an existing lease from a private holder. That is a different route, and it turns on the Minister's consent under reg. 22(l) — with the development condition attached, as for everyone. CIFOR-ICRAF records that the Act "limits the acquisition of land by foreigners" and that leases extended to 99 years are "subject to provisions limiting ownership rights of non-Gambians". Separately, in the Regions, the 50-year cap on non-indigenes applies.

What we could not verify

Some sources, including the US State Department's 2018 Investment Climate Statement, report that non-Gambians may hold residential land only for up to 21 years, only outside 1.5 km of the high water mark, and only up to 2,500 m². We have not been able to trace that rule to any published Gambian statute or regulation, and it does not appear in the State Lands Regulations 1995. We therefore do not state it as law — but because it would matter enormously on the coast, any non-Gambian buyer should have a Gambian lawyer confirm the position in writing before committing.

One thing that is settled: buying property gives you no right to live in The Gambia. There is no golden visa and no retirement visa. See buying property in The Gambia as a foreigner and residency and retiring in The Gambia.

If you are the one selling

Most guides on this subject are written entirely for buyers. If you hold Gambian land and want to sell it — as a private owner, an heir, or a developer disposing of plots — the tenure question decides what you can do, and in what order.

  • Get the Minister's consent, and find out early whether you will get it. Reg. 22(l) requires written consent for a sale, and it is refused where the plot has not been developed in accordance with the covenants. If your plot is bare, that is the first thing to fix — not the listing photographs. The World Bank measured the consent step at 60 days in 2019; do not assume it is quick.
  • Clear the ground rent and the rates. Arrears sit against the lease, and non-payment is a forfeiture trigger under reg. 40(3). A buyer's lawyer will ask for the receipts.
  • Perfect the title before you market it. If what you hold is a certificate of occupancy or an Alkalo document, converting it into an executed lease is worth more to your price than anything else you can do — and it is much harder to do while a buyer is waiting.
  • Know what tax you pay. Capital gains tax falls on the seller: for an individual, the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration; for a company, partnership or trustee, the higher of 25% or 10% on the same basis. It must be paid before the deed can be registered, and GRA gives a 15-day return deadline. The buyer pays stamp duty on the assignment at 2% of the amount, under the Stamp Act (Amendment of Schedule) Order 2019. Confirm the current rates with GRA before you commit to a net figure — see the cost of buying property in The Gambia.
  • If it is family or customary land, you cannot sell it on your own. In the Regions, s.18(1) makes alienation without the District Authority's written consent and the Minister's approval null and void, and s.18(2) requires the assignment, once consented, to be by written agreement. A sale that skips this does not merely risk challenge; it has no legal effect. If you are selling as an heir, see inheritance and wills for Gambian property.
  • If you sell an allocated state plot, you cannot be allocated another for five years (Schedule II, para. 4). The same five-year bar applies to a person whose plot was re-entered for breach (para. 8).
  • Subdividing or renting out is the same problem as selling. Sublease and subdivision fall under reg. 22(l), with the same consent requirement and the same development pre-condition. Changing the use needs permission under reg. 22(b). See renting out property in The Gambia.
  • If you are a developer selling off-plan, note that there is no Gambian statutory regulation of off-plan sales, no developer licensing, no mandatory escrow and no buyer-deposit protection — which cuts both ways. Buyers who know that will want the schedule, the infrastructure and the remedies written into the contract. See developer financing in The Gambia.

If the State wants the land

Compulsory acquisition is governed by the Land Acquisition and Compensation Act, Cap. 57:06 (Act No. 5 of 1991), sitting under section 22 of the 1997 Constitution, which protects against compulsory deprivation of property without compensation.

The important distinction for this guide is what gets compensated. For land held under customary tenure, the Act provides compensation "for the loss of usufructuary rights over the land" plus improvements — that is, for the value of the use and of what you have built, framed differently from compensation for a registered interest. A state lessee's position and a customary occupier's position are not the same when a road, a port or a public project arrives.

That is not hypothetical on this coast. The Ministry of Lands publicly warned on 25 August 2026 against unauthorised development in the area of the Sanyang deep-sea port project, whose construction had already been delayed over land access. If you are buying near a large announced project, ask what the acquisition position is before you build.

What is changing

The National Land Policy 2026–2035 was launched by President Barrow on 22 December 2025. Its headline proposals speak directly to the weaknesses above: formal recognition of customary tenure through Certificates of Customary Ownership, a participatory and transparent process for converting customary land to state land including fair compensation, and the registration and digitalisation of all land in the country.

Two practical implications for anyone buying or selling today. First, customary land with clear, documented community consent stands to become considerably more secure — and more valuable — if certification arrives. Second, nothing has changed yet: a policy is not a register. The policy itself lists reform of the State Lands Act and the Land Acquisition Act as still to come, and we could find no amending Act. Until certificates and a digital register exist, verify title the traditional way, set out step by step in how to verify land title in The Gambia.

Questions people actually ask

Can I buy freehold land on the Gambian coast?

No. New freeholds have not been granted since 1945 and cannot be created without the approval of the legislature. Surviving freeholds are essentially confined to Banjul and amount to around a tenth of the country's land. If a coastal plot is offered to you as freehold, ask to see the instrument and have a lawyer read it — the word is a warning sign, not a selling point.

What is a deemed lease?

Section 7(1) of the State Lands Act, Act No. 1 of 1991, provides that a person occupying land under customary tenure or from year to year when the area was designated as State land is deemed to be a lessee of the State for 99 years, and may apply for a formal lease. Many Kombo holders are deemed lessees. They usually hold a certificate of occupancy rather than an executed lease, and deemed leasehold land cannot be registered at the Deeds Registry.

When did the Kombos become State land?

Banjul and Kombo Saint Mary, which corresponds broadly to today's Kanifing Municipality, were vested by the Act itself. Kombo North, Kombo South and Kombo Central were designated later, by Legal Notice No. 2 of 1994, with effect from 1 March 1994. Before that date those areas were governed by the Lands (Regions) Act and customary law.

What happens when my 99-year lease expires?

Clause 3 of the specimen lease at Schedule III of the State Lands Regulations 1995 says the Minister may grant a new 99-year term provided there is no subsisting breach. Renewal is a discretion, not a right. CIFOR-ICRAF reports that in practice renewal is close to automatic, but that describes practice, not entitlement, and the discretion is expressly conditioned on the covenants being clean.

Do I need consent to sell my state-leasehold plot?

Yes. Regulation 22(l) of the State Lands Regulations 1995 requires the Minister's written consent, first obtained, for any subdivision, conveyance, assignment, sale, transfer of possession, lease or sublease. The World Bank measured the consent application at 60 days and GMD 750 on 2019 data — treat that as a measurement of the time it took then, not a current fee or a promise.

Can I sell a plot I have not built on?

Probably not lawfully. Regulation 22(l) says the Minister's consent shall not be granted if the lessee has not developed the demised premises in accordance with the covenants. An undeveloped state-leasehold plot can be legally unsellable until it is built on. The covenants include a boundary fence within six months of the lease, and development itself requires a permit.

Can I mortgage a state lease?

Only with the Minister's written consent, first obtained, under regulation 22(k). A lender's security over an undeveloped plot is weak in any case, because the consent it would need in order to sell on enforcement is withheld until the build covenants are met.

How much is ground rent?

Nobody publishes a current figure. Regulation 37 charges land rent in accordance with Schedule V, and Schedule V of the 1995 Regulations sets a slab rate of D10 to D25 per 100 square metres multiplied by location and intensity indices, with a worked example of D362.50 a year for a 500 square metre central residential plot of three storeys. Those are 1995 amounts, revisable by Gazette notice under regulation 43, and no post-1995 revision is public. Ask the seller for the current rent demand and receipts.

Can a non-Gambian be allocated a state plot?

No. Schedule II, paragraph 2 of the State Lands Regulations 1995 states that a non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land. That governs government allocation in approved layout areas. Taking an assignment of an existing lease from a private holder is a different route, and it turns on the Minister's consent under regulation 22(l). Take Gambian legal advice before relying on that distinction.

Can I search a central register to confirm who owns a plot?

Only sometimes. Deeds affecting land are registered at the Deeds Registry of the Registrar General's Department under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01. It is a deeds register indexed by the names of the parties, not a title register indexed by parcel, and only surveyed, mapped land with a unique identity can be registered at all. Customary land and deemed leasehold land are excluded, so for many Kombo plots a search returns nothing.

Can one family member sell family land?

Not in the Regions. Section 18(1) of the Lands (Regions) Act makes alienation without the District Authority's written consent and the Minister's approval null and void — not merely voidable. Section 18(2) requires the assignment, once consented, to be in writing. Establish in advance exactly whose consent is needed and record it.

What tax is payable when land changes hands?

Capital gains tax falls on the seller: an individual pays the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration; a company, partnership or trustee pays the higher of 25% or 10%. It must be paid before the deed can be registered. The buyer pays stamp duty of 2% of the amount on the assignment, under the Stamp Act (Amendment of Schedule) Order 2019. Confirm the current rates with GRA.

Can the State take my land?

Yes, by compulsory acquisition under the Land Acquisition and Compensation Act, Cap. 57:06, subject to section 22 of the 1997 Constitution, which protects against deprivation of property without compensation. For land held under customary tenure the Act provides compensation for the loss of usufructuary rights plus improvements, which is a different basis from compensation for a registered interest.

If I sell an allocated plot, can I be allocated another?

Not for five years. Schedule II, paragraph 4 of the State Lands Regulations 1995 bars a person who has sold, assigned or transferred allocated land from being allocated again for five years. Paragraph 5 disqualifies anyone already holding more than one plot, and paragraph 8 imposes the same five-year bar where a plot was re-entered for breach.
