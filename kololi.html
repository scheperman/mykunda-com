The vibe

Kololi is the lively heart of The Gambia’s coast: long golden beaches, seafood grills and beach bars, with some of the country’s best-known residential addresses set back from the sand. It sits directly beside Senegambia and the two names are often used interchangeably — but they behave as different markets. Senegambia is the tourist strip and trades on nightly lets; Kololi proper is where the villas, gated compounds and long-term rentals sit, on quieter roads a few minutes from the noise. Prices here are among the highest in the country, and demand comes from diaspora buyers and expatriates who want the beach without living on top of the bars.

Kololi is bought for yield as often as for living, and the two lead to different properties. Read renting out property in The Gambia and the best areas to buy on the coast before you narrow it down.

Evidence behind the land rate: band rate — 32 listings across the band. Behind the rent: 3 rental listings only. Gambian tenants think in months; agents advertise by the year, and landlords normally ask six to twelve months up front, so both units are shown.

Kololi has only 4 priced plot listings with a known size — below our bar of 5 for an area-specific rate. The rate above is the band rate for the urban coastal strip (Bijilo to Bakau) and Banjul: 32 priced plot listings pooled across Bijilo, Fajara, Kololi, Kerr Serign, Brusubi, Kotu, Banjul and Cape Point. It tells you what the band asks, not what a specific street asks. The 4 listings we can price in Kololi itself ask a median of D8,800 per m² — too few to call a market, shown here as context, not as a rate.

Every figure on this page is an asking price. No one in The Gambia publishes what property actually sells for — there is no public register of sale prices — so what a seller asks is the only thing that can be measured consistently. Sale prices are generally lower, by an amount that depends entirely on the seller. Against the asking price above, the advertised rents work out at a gross yield of 2.4% to 5.3% — before agent's fees, repairs and the months a place stands empty, and below the 9.45% a one-year Gambian treasury bill paid in August 2026. Buyers here are mostly buying land, not income.

These figures are as honest as we can make them — and still young. Reliable Gambian market data barely exists yet, for anyone; building it is one of the reasons MyKunda exists at all. So we publish what we can measure, print the evidence count next to every figure, and leave a blank rather than defend a number we cannot. A market where buyers and sellers can trust what they read has to start somewhere. Every listing and every verified sale sharpens these figures — and if you know a figure on this page to be wrong, tell us.
