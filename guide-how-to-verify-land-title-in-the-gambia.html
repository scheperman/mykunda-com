Land & title

Land in The Gambia: prices, sizes and how to verify title safely

MK MyKunda Updated 31 August 2026 26 min read

Land is the most-searched category on MyKunda, and it is where the most money goes missing. This guide covers what plots are asking by area and how large those square metres really are, then sets out how ownership is actually checked here — which is not what most buyers are told. The central search is done at the Deeds Registry in the Ministry of Justice, not at the Ministry of Lands. It searches names, not plots. And for a large share of the land on sale in Kombo it returns nothing at all, because that land cannot be registered.

In short

  • Deeds affecting land are registered at the Deeds Registry of the Registrar General's Department, in the Attorney General's Chambers and Ministry of Justice, under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01. There is no land registry under the Ministry of Lands.
  • It is a deeds register indexed by the names of the parties, not a title register indexed by plot. Registration gives you priority over later instruments, not proof of title.
  • Customary land and deemed-leasehold land cannot be registered at all, so the central search returns nothing for a large share of Kombo plots. For that land the checks are the Area Council, Physical Planning, the alkalo and the Seyfo, and the site itself.
  • Most of the coast is state land: Banjul and Kanifing Municipality under the State Lands Act, Act No. 1 of 1991, plus Kombo North, Kombo South and Kombo Central, designated with effect from 1 March 1994.
  • Assigning or mortgaging a state lease needs the Minister's written consent, and the Regulations say consent is not granted where the plot has not been developed as the lease requires.
  • Stamp duty on an assignment is 2% and the buyer pays it. The seller pays capital gains tax — for an individual, the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration.
  • Every price on this page is an asking price from MyKunda listings as at August 2026. The Gambia has no public register of sale prices, so no transacted benchmark exists.
  • Prindex measured 24% of Gambian adults as feeling insecure about their tenure in its 2018–19 survey round; its 2024 round records a nine-percentage-point increase.

Why title verification matters here

Most land disputes in The Gambia trace back to the same handful of problems: a plot sold twice or three times over, a family member selling land that belongs to the whole family, a sale by someone with no authority, or a document that looks official and is not.

These are not exotic scenarios. Sitting at Brikama Magistrates' Court on 27 August 2026, Magistrate Kanjura B. Sambou said it was "increasingly common for a single plot of land to be sold to several individuals, with each buyer subsequently presenting an Alkalo transfer document as proof of ownership", and warned alkalolu that they risked imprisonment. Six years earlier The Standard reported that an alkalo transfer certificate could be obtained "in five minutes" for D5,000 to D10,000.

On scale, two Gambian officials have given different figures in the same year. The Minister for Lands, Hamat N. K. Bah, told the World Urban Forum on 21 May 2026 that "in The Gambia, 67% of all court disputes are related to land conflicts". A report of the national validation workshop of 4 May 2026 puts the figure at roughly 75%. We publish both rather than choosing between them. Separately, the Minister for Lands told The Point on 14 July 2025 that more than D500 million has been lost to land fraud — that is his assertion, not an audited figure, and we treat it as such.

Time matters too. Foroyaa has reported Gambian land cases running "over 3 years"; The Standard put appeals at five to seven years or more. A dispute you inherit with a plot is not a paperwork problem, it is a decade.

The cost of a proper title investigation is a rounding error next to the purchase price. The cost of skipping it can be the entire purchase price, plus years in court, plus — as the cases below show — a bulldozer.

First, establish what kind of title you are buying

You cannot verify a title until you know which kind it is, because each is recorded — or not recorded — in a different place. Most guides offer three categories. There are four, and the one usually left out is the one that describes most Kombo land.

TenureWhat it isUsual evidenceRegistrable at the Deeds Registry?
Freehold (fee simple)Ownership without a time limit. A closed, pre-1945 class — new freeholds have been prohibited since 1945 without the consent of the legislature, and surviving grants are essentially confined to Banjul.A deed of conveyance, historically issued by the Governor GeneralYes — the conveyance is a registrable instrument

Freehold is not a prize to look for. Because it cannot be newly created, a document offered as a new coastal freehold is a red flag, not an upgrade. If someone tells you a plot in Brufut or Sanyang is freehold, ask to see the pre-1945 chain.

A 99-year lease is not automatically renewable. Clause 3 of the specimen lease provides that at expiry the Minister may grant a further term of 99 years, "provided there shall be no subsisting breach" of the lessee's covenants. Renewal is discretionary. CIFOR-ICRAF's 2024 guide adds that in practice renewal is close to automatic — that is practice, not a right. Residential leases run 99 years; other uses run 21 to 50 years depending on sector policy and ministerial discretion, and leases in the Regions are capped at 50 years.

Registration is the dividing line that decides how you check a plot. Only land that has been surveyed, mapped and given a unique identity can be registered, and that "excludes all customary land and deemed leasehold land". If you are buying an unregistrable plot — and on much of the coast you are — a deeds search will come back empty, and an empty search proves nothing either way. If the distinctions are new to you, read freehold, leasehold and customary land explained first.

Which law applies where — and why the coast is state land

This is where a lot of published advice goes wrong, including our own earlier version of this page. The two Acts do not divide the country into "Greater Banjul" and "everywhere else".

The State Lands Act, Act No. 1 of 1991 (Cap. 57:02) vests all land in the City of Banjul and Kanifing Municipality in the State, "excluding such land as is held in fee simple and subject to any grant which has been or may hereafter be made". It also lets the Minister designate land in the Regions as State Lands. Kombo North, Kombo South and Kombo Central were designated with effect from 1 March 1994 (Legal Notice No. 2 of 1994). That is the whole coastal and near-coastal strip — Brusubi, Brufut, Tanji, Batokunku, Tujereng, Sanyang, Gunjur, Kartong, and the inland Kombo belt around Sukuta, Salagi, Nema Kunku and Farato.

So on the coast: the Lands (Regions) Act no longer governs, the land vests in the State, occupiers who were there at designation are deemed lessees for 99 years, and administrative authority sits with the Minister rather than with a District Authority. A "customary sale" in Sanyang is, in substance, the transfer of a possessory claim over designated state land. It is perfected — if it is perfected at all — through Physical Planning clearance, an Area Council transfer and a certificate of occupancy, not by an alkalo's signature on its own.

The Lands (Regions) Act, Cap. 57:03, still governs the rest of the country. Read carefully, it says these things, which are commonly run together into one wrong rule:

  • s.4 declares Regions land vested in the Authorities for the Districts in which it is situated — District Authorities, presided over by the Seyfo. Not "regional authorities", and not the alkalo, who administers rather than holds.
  • s.5 applies customary law to occupation by indigenes.
  • s.7(1) requires the consent of the District Authority before a non-indigene may occupy; s.7(2) provides that without the Regional Governor's approval a non-indigene is deemed a tenant at will; s.7(3)(c) caps occupation recorded by memorandum at three years where no lease has been executed.
  • s.8 requires a tenancy of more than three years to be created by an agreement in writing. That is a formality requirement, not an approval requirement.
  • s.9 caps a non-indigene at a tenancy of 50 years, renewable for a further fifty. "Non-indigene" is defined by descent, so many Gambian citizens are non-indigenes in a given district. This is not a nationality rule.
  • s.13 lists conditions — execution before two witnesses, endorsed certificates, no subletting without consent, registration within sixty days at the Registry Office, a copy deposited with the Governor — failing which the agreement is voidable by either party. s.14 provides that without the Minister's endorsed approval, a tenancy required to be in writing creates no interest greater than a tenancy from year to year. s.18(1) goes further and makes alienation without the required consent null and void — void, not merely voidable.

Where customary land is in dispute, the first forum in practice is the District Tribunal — the Kombo South District Tribunal, for example, gave the 2018 judgment described further down this page. That jurisdiction comes from the District Tribunals legislation, not from the Lands (Regions) Act, whose only governor-review provision concerns the value of fixtures and buildings, with appeal to the Minister.

What we could not verify

We were unable to obtain the consolidated Gazette text of the State Lands Act itself. The section numbers and wording above are taken at second hand from CIFOR-ICRAF's 2024 guide to land rights in The Gambia and from a dated Gambian op-ed reproducing the Act. Note also that the Act is dated 1990 in some catalogues and 1991 in others — the bill was 1990, and the State Lands Regulations 1995 cite "the State Lands Act, 1991 (Act No. 1 of 1991)". Ask your lawyer to work from the Gazette, not from a website, before you rely on a section number.

What land is asking, by area

Before the paperwork, the practical question. The figures below are asking prices taken from MyKunda listings as at August 2026 — what sellers are advertising, not what plots have sold for. The Gambia has no public register of sale prices, and neither the Gambia Bureau of Statistics nor any other body publishes a land price index, so there is no transacted benchmark to compare them against. Three things specific to coastal land are worth knowing before you fall in love with a beach plot:

  • The West Coast Tourism Development Area runs from Kotu to Kartong, 800 m inland from the high water mark, and is leased State land under the custody of the Gambia Tourism Board. That 800 m band is a tenure control, not an erosion setback. All new applications for TDA land have been suspended since 23 January 2025 pending an audit.
  • Erosion is measured and it is fast. The World Bank's Beyond the Tide study, published 28 February 2026, records erosion of up to 4.7 m a year on the tourism corridor and states that "no formal setback zones or planning restrictions exist yet". There is no statutory erosion buffer to protect you; you have to judge the distance yourself.
  • Sanyang is a construction site in waiting. A deep-sea port is planned there, and on 25 August 2026 the Ministry of Lands publicly warned against unauthorised development in the port area. Ask specifically whether a plot sits inside it.

How big is a plot, really?

There is no published Gambian standard residential plot size, and no source we could find supports any "typical compound" convention. Anyone who tells you a Gambian plot is 20 m by 30 m is describing a Nigerian or Zambian convention, not a Gambian one. We have removed that claim from this page.

What does exist is a statutory framework. Schedule IV of the State Lands Regulations 1995 bands residential plots for the purpose of calculating the premium payable on a state grant:

Band (purely residential)Premium basis under Schedule IVWhat that area looks like
Up to 300 m²No premiumHouse, small yard, parking. For illustration only, 300 m² is 15 m × 20 m.
301–500 m²D10 per m² on the excessThe band a documented real example falls in: three Salagi plots in evidence before a Gambian court in 2026 each measured 20 m × 25 m = 500 m².
501–750 m²D20 per m² on the excessHouse plus separate quarters and a walled compound.
Above 750 m², with multipliers to 1,000 m²Higher multipliers applyBeyond 1,000 m² you are outside the residential bands entirely and into a different kind of grant.

Read that as the regulatory centre of gravity for a residential plot: roughly 300 to 750 m². Plots of 1,200 m² and 2,500 m² are sold here, and there is nothing wrong with buying one, but do not assume they will be treated as ordinary residential plots for premium, planning or subdivision purposes. Subdividing is itself development requiring a permit.

Two practical notes. Comparisons to a football pitch, which we used to publish, are worthless: the Laws of the Game permit anything from 90 × 45 m to 120 × 90 m, so the "percentage of a pitch" changes by a factor of nearly three depending on the pitch. And the dimensions in the table above are simple arithmetic to help you picture an area — they are not shapes anyone has surveyed. The only shape that matters is the one on the approved plan.

The documents a Gambian seller may hold, and what each is worth

Buyers here are shown a specific and fairly short list of documents, several of which are not title at all. Learn the names, because a seller who says "I have the papers" may mean any of these.

That resolves a contradiction this guide used to contain. We told buyers a hand-drawn sketch was not a survey plan, and then told sellers to upload a hand-drawn sketch. Both statements are half right. The distinction is between a sketch plan approved by Physical Planning, which is a statutory document, and an informal drawing, which is a marketing aid at best. A listing photograph of an informal drawing is fine as an illustration; it is not evidence of anything.

A hard rule worth adopting: never pay a deposit, not even a small one to "hold" the plot, before you have seen the seller's documents yourself and had someone independent read them. Deposits are extremely difficult to recover once paid, and no Gambian rule compels an agent to hold your money in a client account.

The checks that actually verify ownership

Documents can be forged. What separates real verification from a document review is that you check the seller's papers against records held by somebody else.

1. A search at the Deeds Registry — and what it can and cannot tell you

Deeds affecting land are registered at the Deeds Registry of the Registrar General's Department, which sits in the Attorney General's Chambers and Ministry of Justice, under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01. Section 3 of that Act establishes "an office at Banjul … called the Registry Office". There is no land registry under the Ministry of Lands and no government body of that name. The Ministry of Lands issues leases and consents and collects ground rent; the Registrar General registers deeds. A buyer sent to the Ministry of Lands for a title search has been sent to the wrong building.

Four features of that register change how you use it:

  • It is a deeds register, not a title register. It records that an instrument exists and was lodged. It does not adjudicate who owns anything.
  • Registration gives priority, not title. Section 7 provides that an instrument takes effect against other instruments affecting the same land from the date of registration — related back to execution if it is registered within 7 days in Banjul, 60 days elsewhere in The Gambia, and 12 months if it was executed outside The Gambia. That last window matters to a diaspora buyer signing abroad: a deed you signed in London a year ago can still leapfrog one registered here in the meantime.
  • It is indexed by the names of the parties, not by parcel. You cannot search "against the plot". You search against a named person and a known instrument, which is why you need the seller's full legal name exactly as it appears on the deed, and the names of everyone in the chain before them.
  • It shows mortgages and caveats. It does not show litigation. Leases, assignments, conveyances, mortgages, caveats and memoranda of deposit are registrable. A pending court case is a separate enquiry, and a District Tribunal judgment from years ago will not appear at all.

The World Bank measured deed registration at the Registrar General's Office at 5 days and GMD 1,500 in its Doing Business 2020 data, collected in May 2019. Treat that as a 2019 measurement, not a current fee.

2. When the search comes back empty — the checks for unregistrable land

If the plot is customary or deemed leasehold, there is nothing at the Deeds Registry to find, and an empty result is not reassurance. For that land the real checks are:

  • The Area Council — the register of yard owners, and whether rates have been paid and by whom.
  • The Department of Physical Planning — the approved layout, the land use report and the planning clearance.
  • The Department of Lands and Surveys — whether a certificate of occupancy or a lease file exists, and whether ground rent is up to date.
  • The alkalo and the Seyfo — and, crucially, not only the alkalo the seller introduces you to.
  • The site — see check 4.

3. A check with Physical Planning

The Department of Physical Planning, under the Ministry of Lands and Regional Government, holds approved layouts and zoning and issues the land use report, planning clearance and approved sketch plan. This tells you whether the plot exists as an approved plot in an approved layout, what it may be used for, and whether it sits on land reserved for a road, a school or a drainage channel. Regulation 3 of the State Lands Regulations 1995 provides that all grants of State land shall be in planned areas only. A plot that appears on a seller's drawing but not on the official layout is a serious warning sign.

Two things to keep in mind. A development permit is valid for two years unless renewed, under section 30(2) of the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990 — a staged self-builder can let one lapse without noticing. And the Minister for Lands said publicly in November 2025 that a development permit does not confer ownership. It is not a substitute for title, and it is sometimes presented as one.

4. A boundary check on the ground

Instruct your own surveyor — not the seller's — to visit the plot and confirm that what is on the ground matches what is on the plan. This is how overlapping plots, double allocations and encroachment come to light, and it is the step buyers most often skip because it costs money and takes time. The World Bank measured a survey plan at GMD 2,750 in 2019.

5. Local enquiries at the site

Free, and repeatedly the thing that saves people. Speak to the neighbours, to anyone farming or living on the land, and to more than one person in the village. Ask who owns it, whether anyone else claims it, and whether there has ever been a dispute or a tribunal case. Communities know their own land.

There is no online register you can check yourself. Digitalisation is a policy proposal, not a service. If you search for a Gambian land registry online you will find a site at the domain landregistry.gm — as at 31 August 2026 it has an invalid security certificate and serves an unrelated commercial shop. It is not an official Gambian land registry and we do not link to it.

The Minister's consent — the step that decides whether a sale works

This is the single most consequential omission from most Gambian buying advice, including our own earlier version of this page.

The covenants set out in regulation 22 of the State Lands Regulations 1995, and reproduced in the specimen lease, say in terms:

  • reg. 22(l) — the lessee "shall not subdivide, convey, assign or otherwise alienate the premises or any part thereof by sale, transfer of possession, lease or sublease without the consent of the Minister in writing first obtained and such consent shall not be granted if the lessee has not developed the demised premises in accordance with the covenants in that behalf";
  • reg. 22(k) — no mortgage, charge or encumbrance without the Minister's written consent first obtained;
  • reg. 22(g) — no development without a development permit under the Physical Planning and Development Control Act.

Read those together and the consequence is uncomfortable but important: an undeveloped state-leasehold plot may not be lawfully assignable, and may not be mortgageable, until it has been built on. That is the best-evidenced explanation of why Gambian banks are cautious about lending against bare land — better than the usual story about foreign income being hard to assess. See bank mortgages in The Gambia for what lenders do publish.

In practice the seller applies for consent, with fees, and the Minister may approve or refuse. The World Bank listed "Application for Ministerial Consent to transfer" as a formal procedure taking 60 days and GMD 750 in its 2019 data — the longest single step in a Gambian conveyance. Build it into your timetable and into the contract, and make completion conditional on it.

In the Regions, the equivalent trap is sharper. Section 18(1) of the Lands (Regions) Act makes alienation without the required consent null and void, and section 13(c) bars a non-indigene tenant from subletting or assigning without the Authority's consent and the Minister's approval.

If you are not a Gambian citizen

Paragraph 2 of Schedule II to the State Lands Regulations 1995 — the general eligibility criteria for the grant of residential land in approved layout areas — says plainly: "A non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land." The same Schedule bars anyone under 21, sets one plot per person as a general rule, and bars for five years a person who has sold or transferred land previously allocated to them.

That governs government allocation. It does not, on its face, prohibit a non-Gambian from taking an assignment of an existing lease from a private holder — but every such assignment needs the Minister's written consent under reg. 22(l), and consent is withheld on an undeveloped plot. In the Regions, a non-indigene cannot take more than a 50-year tenancy. Never accept the claim that foreign buyers face no legal limits, or that a passport is all you need; both are wrong. Buying property in The Gambia as a foreigner covers this in full.

What we could not verify

Some sources, including the US State Department's 2018 Investment Climate Statement, report that non-Gambians may hold residential land only for up to 21 years, only outside 1.5 km of the high water mark, and only up to 2,500 m². We have not been able to trace that rule to any published Gambian statute or regulation, and it does not appear in the State Lands Regulations 1995. We therefore do not state it as law — but because it would matter enormously on the coast, any non-Gambian buyer should have a Gambian lawyer confirm the position in writing before committing.

The two rules that catch people out. Section 29 of the Stamp Act provides that no deed may be registered unless it is duly stamped, and section 17 makes an unstamped instrument inadmissible in evidence. And capital gains tax is payable before the deed can be registered. So although the tax is legally the seller's, an unpaid CGT bill stops your registration — which makes it a buyer's completion risk. GRA gives a return deadline of 15 days from the transaction. Deal with it in the contract: agree who pays, agree that it is paid on or before completion, and see the money go.

Why you will hear "5% transfer tax". You will, and it is worth understanding. There is no Gambian transfer tax. The 5% figure comes from two places. First, the World Bank's Doing Business 2020 recorded a single combined procedure — "Payment of Capital Gains Tax and Stamp Duty … 5% of the purchase price (Stamp Duty)" — on data collected in May 2019, four months before the September 2019 Order that set the rate at 2%, and it lumped the seller's capital gains tax in with the duty. Second, and more often, the "5%" a Gambian buyer is quoted is simply the seller's capital gains tax floor — 5% of the consideration — being passed across the table. Ask which one is meant, and ask GRA or your lawyer to confirm the rate applied to your particular deed.

What we could not verify

Two things. Current ground rent amounts are not published. The last public basis is Schedule V of the 1995 Regulations, which computes rent from plot area at a slab rate of D10 to D25 per 100 m², adjusted by location and intensity indices, with a worked example of D362.50 a year for a 500 m² central residential plot. No post-1995 revision is public, so we do not quote 1995 figures as if they were current — ask the Department of Lands what is actually owing on the specific plot. Non-payment matters: the Regulations make a defaulting lease liable to forfeiture and re-entry by the Minister. Second, whether unpaid Area Council rates follow the property to a new owner is not something we could establish from any Gambian source. Do not assume either way; ask the Council in writing before completion, and get the answer on paper.

Demolition, eviction and what actually happens when a title fails

The consequence of a bad title in The Gambia is not usually a refund. It is losing the land, and sometimes the building on it. This guide did not previously say so.

The clearest documented example was reported by Foroyaa on 31 March 2026. A Kombo South District Tribunal judgment given on 29 August 2018 — "merely three paragraphs with no detailed boundary definitions", resting on conflicting sketch-plan measurements — was executed in 2025 by the Sheriff Division of the High Court together with the Department of Physical Planning, with a police escort, against more than 70 families, roughly 40 hours before Eid al-Fitr. Most of those families had bought in good faith years after a judgment they had never heard of.

It is not an isolated case. Recent, dated, attributable examples:

  • Salagi, April 2025 — demolitions in two phases, discussed publicly by the Minister for Lands in November 2025. In August 2026 Foroyaa reported a prosecution at Brusubi Magistrates' Court over three Salagi plots and D1.51 million, involving false alkalo stamps, brought under section 265 of the Criminal Offences Act 2025.
  • Batokunku and Tanji, October 2025 — the alkalolu of both villages were suspended over the sale of layout land, following a High Court suit brought in June 2025 by 40 homeowners.
  • Gunjur and Berending — a boundary dispute running 38 years, including a fatal shooting in 2019, with a new government taskforce appointed in August 2025.
  • Brufut and Brusubi, March 2026 — protests over the reallocation of plots.
  • Farato — criminal proceedings arising from land sales.

Separately, the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990 backs development control with an order for removal of the development plus a fine of up to D2,000 and/or a year's imprisonment. The fine is trivial; the demolition is not, and it is enforced. Enforcement notices, stop notices and revocation are all in the Act, and an appeal to the Minister must be lodged within 15 days with work stopped.

What this means for you, practically: ask specifically whether any tribunal or court judgment has ever been given over the land or the layout it sits in. That will not show up in a deeds search, and it is exactly the thing that arrives with a bulldozer eight years later.

Warning signs that should stop a transaction

  • The only document is an alkalo transfer, particularly on the Kombo coast, where the land is designated state land and an alkalo's signature is evidence of local recognition rather than of a legal estate.
  • The seller will not let you copy the documents, or will only show photographs of them.
  • The name on the deed does not match the seller's identification, and the explanation involves an uncle, a nickname or a spelling error.
  • Someone is selling for an absent owner without a power of attorney that is properly executed and registered against the property.
  • Nobody will apply for the Minister's consent, or the seller says it is not needed on state land. It is.
  • The plot is undeveloped and the seller is a leaseholder — check whether the development covenants have been met, because consent to assign may not be available.
  • Heavy pressure to pay quickly because another buyer is supposedly ready. Urgency is the oldest tool in the box.
  • A price well below the going rate for the area with no plausible explanation.
  • A deceased owner and no grant — the estate must be properly administered before anyone can sell.
  • Family land with one signature — where the beneficial interest is held by a family, one member alone usually cannot sell.
  • A new "freehold" on the coast. New freeholds cannot be created.
  • A request for cash with no receipt, or payment to a personal account that is not the seller's.

Who should do the checking, and how long it takes

Two professionals, both instructed by you and paid by you:

  • An independent lawyer. Not the seller's lawyer, not the agent's recommended lawyer, and not a lawyer the seller is paying. Your lawyer runs the deeds search, reads the chain, checks the consent position, drafts the agreement and handles stamping and registration.
  • A surveyor. To confirm boundaries on the ground and to produce or verify the survey plan.

On timing, we have deleted a figure we could not support. This page used to say the legal and search work takes two to six weeks. No Gambian source publishes any such figure, and the evidence points the other way: the Minister's consent alone was measured at 60 days in 2019, and the World Bank measured the whole Banjul registration process at 6 procedures, about 73 to 74 days and 7.8% of property value on May 2019 data. Customary and deemed-leasehold land, where the route runs through the Area Council and Physical Planning rather than the Deeds Registry, takes longer again. Plan in months, not weeks, and do not let a deadline push you into paying before the checks are done.

On agents: there is no licensing regime and no professional register for estate agents in The Gambia. Agents are, however, reporting entities under the AML/CFT Act 2012 supervised by the Financial Intelligence Unit, and consumers can complain to the Gambia Competition and Consumer Protection Commission. See are estate agents regulated in The Gambia?

Paying safely once the title checks out

Verification and payment structure are two halves of the same protection. Even with a clean title:

  • Pay in stages — a deposit on signing, the balance on consent, stamping and registration;
  • Insist that money goes into a lawyer's client account rather than to the seller or agent in cash. Be clear-eyed about what that is: it is a private arrangement you have negotiated, not a legal protection, because no Gambian rule compels an agent or a lawyer to operate an escrow or client account for property money;
  • Keep a written record of every payment, however small;
  • Register the sale contract. This is a real and underused protection. "Instrument" under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act extends to "any State grant, deed, contract", and "land" to "any estate or interest whatever in real property" — so a contract of sale, and an instalment contract in particular, can be registered and take priority from that date;
  • Do not treat the transaction as complete until the deed is stamped and registered in your name. Handing over money is not the same as owning land.

Buying from abroad? All of this can be done remotely, but only with someone you trust and an independent lawyer. Insist on photographs and video of the site visit and boundary check. Grant powers of attorney narrowly, to someone accountable, and have the power registered against the property. Remember the 12-month relation-back window for instruments executed outside The Gambia — it protects you, and it can also mean a deed signed abroad a year ago outranks yours. For moving the purchase money, see sending money to The Gambia.

Selling land: what to perfect before you list

A seller who understands the buyer's side sells faster and for more. Presentation matters, but paperwork decides. Here is what actually blocks a Gambian land sale, and what to do about it.

Perfect the title: from an alkalo document to a certificate of occupancy

If what you hold is customary or deemed-leasehold evidence, the most valuable thing you can do — before you advertise — is take it up the chain. The route documented by CIFOR-ICRAF runs:

  1. Alkalo confirmation or transfer for the plot.
  2. Land Use Report, Physical Planning Clearance and an approved sketch plan from the Department of Physical Planning.
  3. The Area Council transfer form, endorsed and stamped by the alkalo, the Seyfo and the council's chief executive, with the register of yard owners updated.
  4. Apply to the Department of Lands and Surveys for a certificate of occupancy, enclosing the alkalo certificate, the land use report, the planning clearance and the stamped Area Council form.
  5. Physical Planning survey and clearance for occupancy, then the certificate is issued and endorsed.

From a certificate of occupancy, the next step on designated state land is to apply for a formal lease — the State Lands Act expressly allows a deemed lessee to do so. A registered lease is what makes a plot mortgageable, cleanly assignable and worth more to a serious buyer. It is also, bluntly, the difference between a plot a bank will look at and one it will not: banks generally do not accept a certificate of occupancy as security, though some microfinance and credit institutions do.

Get consent, and get it early

If you hold a state lease, you apply for the Minister's written consent to transfer, with the fees. Start it before you have a buyer under pressure — it was measured at 60 days in 2019. And know the trap: if the plot is undeveloped and you have not met the development covenants, the Regulations say consent shall not be granted. If that is your position, either build enough to satisfy the covenants or be honest with buyers about it, because a sale that cannot receive consent is a sale that will unravel.

Know your tax before you agree a price

Capital gains tax is yours: the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration for an individual, or the higher of 25% of the gain or 10% of the consideration for a company, partnership or trustee, with a 15-day return deadline. The buyer may agree to bear it, but that is a negotiation, not the default. Budget for it in the price you set. There is a D24,000 exemption and reliefs exist for a private residence and for agricultural reinvestment; ask GRA or your accountant what applies to you, because the conditions are not something we can state generally. Stamp duty of 2% falls on the buyer, and no deed can be registered until it is stamped.

Clear the arrears and the endorsements

Ground rent on a state lease is payable in advance before 31 January each year and non-payment makes the lease liable to forfeiture and re-entry. Area Council rates should be up to date and the register of yard owners updated into the buyer's name after the sale. A buyer's lawyer will ask for the receipts.

Family land and inherited land

Where the beneficial interest is held by a family or community, control rests with the head of family and the land is not transferable without a partition. Where land was distributed or allocated, an affidavit from the head of family or the alkalo is normally required. For inherited property you will need a deed of assent or an order of the court — and in a Muslim estate, the High Court administers the estate while the Cadi Court fixes entitlement, so a Cadi distribution order may be needed as well as a grant. "Probate" on its own is not the whole answer in a majority-Muslim jurisdiction. See inheritance and wills for Gambian property.

Then present it well

  • Pin the exact plot on the map, on the boundary rather than the nearest village.
  • Upload the approved sketch plan or the survey plan if you have one. An informal drawing can illustrate shape, but label it as an illustration — do not let a buyer mistake it for a planning document.
  • Photograph the boundaries from all four corners, including the road or access point.
  • State what is missing. "No road access yet", "electricity 200 m away", "certificate of occupancy applied for, not yet issued" reads as honest and lets buyers budget. It also protects you: a buyer who was told cannot claim later that they were not.

What to do if something does not add up

Stop. Do not pay anything further, do not sign, and do not accept a promise that the paperwork will follow. If you have already paid, instruct a lawyer immediately. The practical options are lodging a caveat at the Deeds Registry, which is a registrable instrument carrying D500 stamp duty, to warn off further dealings in registered land, and pursuing recovery. Both get harder with time, and Gambian property litigation is measured in years.

It is worth saying plainly: walking away from a plot you liked is not a failure. There is a great deal of land for sale along the Gambian coast, and there will be another one with clean papers.

Reform is coming — but it has not arrived

The National Land Policy 2026–2035 was launched by President Barrow on 22 December 2025. It proposes the things that would make this guide shorter: formal recognition of customary tenure through Certificates of Customary Ownership, a participatory and transparent process for converting customary land to state land with fair compensation, and the registration and digitalisation of all land in the country through a national land administration system.

On insecurity, the number usually quoted with the policy is not the policy's own. It is Prindex, which measured 24% of adults in The Gambia as feeling insecure about their property rights in its 2018–19 survey round, published in 2020. Prindex's 2024 round records a nine-percentage-point increase for The Gambia — closer to a third than a quarter, and moving the wrong way.

None of it changes what you should do this year. Until a digital register exists and certificates are actually being issued, verification is a lawyer, a surveyor, a deeds search where one is possible, and a conversation at the Area Council and on the site. Treat the announcements as a reason to keep your paperwork immaculate: when registration does come, the holder with a registered lease, a survey plan and a clean chain will convert without difficulty. Everyone else will be arguing.

A draft Real Estate Agents Bill would introduce licensing of agents for the first time. It was validated at a national stakeholder workshop on 29 April 2026, but as at 31 August 2026 it does not appear among the bills before the National Assembly, its text is unpublished, and it is neither passed nor assented. A separate Rent and Tenancy Bill 2025 has been through a Ministry of Justice validation workshop; its contents are not published and we do not describe them.

Questions people actually ask

Where is the land registry in The Gambia?

There is no land registry under the Ministry of Lands. Deeds affecting land are registered at the Deeds Registry of the Registrar General's Department, in the Attorney General's Chambers and Ministry of Justice in Banjul, under the Land (Registration of Deeds) Act, Cap. 57:01. The Ministry of Lands issues leases and consents and collects ground rent; it does not register deeds.

Can I search the register against a plot number?

No. The register is indexed by the names of the parties to each instrument, so a search is a name search. You need the seller's full legal name exactly as it appears on the deed, and the names of everyone earlier in the chain. There is no parcel index and no online search.

If a deeds search finds nothing, is the land unencumbered?

No. Customary land and deemed-leasehold land cannot be registered at all, so an empty result may simply mean the plot was never registrable. For that land you check the Area Council register of yard owners, the Department of Physical Planning, the Department of Lands and Surveys, the alkalo and the Seyfo, and the site itself.

Does registration prove I own the land?

No. It is a deeds register, not a title register. Registration gives your instrument priority over other instruments affecting the same land from the date of registration, related back to execution if registered within 7 days in Banjul, 60 days elsewhere in The Gambia, or 12 months if the instrument was executed outside The Gambia.

Do I need the Minister's consent to buy leasehold land?

Yes, for state land. Regulation 22(l) of the State Lands Regulations 1995 provides that a lessee shall not subdivide, convey, assign or otherwise alienate without the Minister's written consent first obtained, and that consent shall not be granted where the lessee has not developed the plot in accordance with the covenants. A mortgage needs consent too, under regulation 22(k). That is also why an undeveloped plot is hard to buy or to lend against: consent to assign is withheld until the covenants are met.

What tax do I pay when I buy land?

Stamp duty of 2% of the amount on the assignment or conveyance, payable by the buyer, under the Stamp Act (Amendment of Schedule) Order 2019. No deed can be registered until it is stamped, and an unstamped instrument is inadmissible in evidence.

What tax does the seller pay?

Capital gains tax: for an individual, the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration; for a company, partnership or trustee, the higher of 25% of the gain or 10% of the consideration. GRA gives a return deadline of 15 days. It must be paid before the deed can be registered, so an unpaid seller's bill blocks the buyer's registration.

Is the 5% transfer tax I keep hearing about real?

There is no Gambian transfer tax. The 5% figure is usually the seller's capital gains tax floor being passed to the buyer. It also appears in the World Bank's Doing Business 2020, which recorded 5% on data collected in May 2019 — before the September 2019 Order that set stamp duty at 2% — and which combined capital gains tax with stamp duty in one procedure.

Is an alkalo transfer document proof of ownership?

No. It is evidence that a village head recognised a customary allocation, it is not registrable title, and it is routinely forged. A Gambian magistrate said in August 2026 that it is increasingly common for one plot to be sold to several buyers, each holding an alkalo transfer. On the Kombo coast the land is designated state land in any event, so an alkalo signature is local recognition, not a legal estate.

What is a standard plot size in The Gambia?

There is no published standard. The only statutory framework is Schedule IV of the State Lands Regulations 1995, which bands residential plots at up to 300 m², 301 to 500 m² and 501 to 750 m², with multipliers to 1,000 m². A documented real example is three Salagi plots of 20 m by 25 m, or 500 m², in evidence before a Gambian court in 2026.

Can I be demolished after buying in good faith?

Yes. Foroyaa reported in March 2026 that a three-paragraph Kombo South District Tribunal judgment from 29 August 2018 was executed in 2025 by the Sheriff Division of the High Court and the Department of Physical Planning, with a police escort, against more than 70 families. A judgment or tribunal decision does not appear in a deeds search, so ask specifically whether any case has ever been decided over the land or its layout.

Can a foreigner buy land in The Gambia?

Not by government allocation: Schedule II, paragraph 2 of the State Lands Regulations 1995 says "a non-Gambian shall not be allocated a plot of land". Taking an assignment of an existing lease from a private holder is not prohibited on the face of that Schedule, but it needs the Minister's written consent, and in the Regions a non-indigene cannot take more than a 50-year tenancy. Anyone who tells you there are no legal limits is wrong.

How do I make my customary plot sellable?

Take it up the chain: alkalo confirmation, then a land use report, planning clearance and approved sketch plan from Physical Planning, then the Area Council transfer form endorsed by the alkalo, Seyfo and council chief executive, then apply to the Department of Lands and Surveys for a certificate of occupancy — and from there apply for a formal lease. A registered lease is what makes the plot mortgageable and cleanly assignable.

How long does the legal work take?

Longer than most people are told, and no Gambian body publishes a figure. The Minister's consent alone was measured at 60 days and the whole Banjul registration process at about 73 to 74 days in World Bank data collected in May 2019. Customary and deemed-leasehold land takes longer again. We have removed the two-to-six-weeks estimate this page used to carry because nothing supports it.
