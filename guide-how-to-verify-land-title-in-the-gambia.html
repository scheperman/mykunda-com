Land & title

Land in The Gambia: prices, sizes and how to verify title safely

MK MyKunda Updated 15 August 2026 12 min read

Land is the most-searched category on MyKunda — and buying it is straightforward when the paperwork is in order, and ruinous when it isn't. This guide covers what land actually costs and how big a plot really is, then walks through how ownership is checked and verified, in the order a careful buyer would do it.

In short

  • Verification rests on four independent checks: a Land Registry search, a check with Physical Planning against the approved layout, your own licensed surveyor on the boundaries, and local enquiries with the alkalo and neighbours.
  • The legal and search work takes between two and six weeks, longer for customary land or an incomplete chain of ownership.
  • Indicative bare-land prices run from about $140/m² in Kololi to about $14/m² in Kartong, and a 600 m² plot — the typical walled family compound — is roughly 8% of a football pitch.
  • Research finds roughly a quarter of Gambian adults feel insecure about their tenure, and until the Land Policy 2026–2035 produces a digital register, verification stays manual.

Why title verification matters here

Most property disputes in The Gambia trace back to the same handful of problems: land sold twice to two different buyers, a family member selling land that belongs to the whole family, a plot sold without the consent of the people who actually hold it under customary tenure, or a "seller" who is really just a middleman with no authority at all.

These are not exotic scenarios. They are common enough that every experienced buyer, lawyer and estate agent in the country treats a title search as non-negotiable — including for land that has been in the same family for generations.

The cost of a proper title search is a rounding error next to the purchase price. The cost of skipping it can be the entire purchase price, plus years of litigation.

First, establish what kind of title you are buying

You cannot verify a title until you know which kind it is, because each type is recorded in a different place. The Gambia has three broad categories:

TypeWhat it isWhere it is recorded
FreeholdOutright ownership, no time limitLand Registry, under the Ministry of Lands
State leaseholdA long lease from the State, typically 99 years, with conditionsLand Registry plus the Ministry of Lands
Customary or tribal landLand held under traditional community tenure, allocated by the village headOften no central register at all — district and village level only

The statutes behind that table matter when something goes wrong. The State Lands Act 1990 covers Banjul and Kombo Saint Mary: land there is vested in the State except where it is held in fee simple, and the State grants it on leases with an initial term of 99 years. The Lands (Regions) Act covers the rest of the country: land is vested in the regional authorities, customary law governs occupation by indigenes, and there are statutory conditions on non-indigene occupation, a limit on the interest a non-indigene may acquire, and an approval requirement for tenancies beyond three years — with deeds that ignore them capable of being voidable. Where customary land is disputed, the first forum is the district tribunal, subject to review by the regional governor.

Customary land is where most problems arise, simply because there is frequently no central record to check against. That does not make it unbuyable — a great deal of coastal land started life as customary land — but it does mean the verification work is different and the conversion process matters. If you are unclear on the distinctions, read freehold, leasehold and customary land explained before going further.

Before the paperwork, the practical question: what does bare, unbuilt land actually cost? Prices range from a few dollars a square metre inland to well over $100 on the prime coastal strip. Listings are priced and measured in square metres, which is not always intuitive if you are used to acres or a different building tradition. Here is what the common sizes translate to on the ground.

The documents a seller should be able to produce

Ask for these before you discuss price seriously. A seller who cannot produce them, or who keeps promising them "next week", is telling you something.

  • The title deed itself — a deed of conveyance, deed of assignment, or a lease from the State. This is the core document.
  • Proof of registration — the deed should carry a registration stamp and number from the Land Registry. An unregistered deed is far weaker than a registered one.
  • A survey plan — prepared and signed by a licensed surveyor, showing the exact boundaries, dimensions and coordinates of the plot. A hand-drawn sketch is not a survey plan.
  • The chain of ownership — how did the current owner acquire it, and from whom? For land that has changed hands several times, you want to see each link.
  • Rates and tax receipts — up-to-date payments to the relevant Area Council. Arrears become your problem after transfer.
  • Identification — a valid ID for the seller, matching the name on the deed exactly. Not "close enough".
  • For customary land — written confirmation from the alkalo and, where relevant, evidence of family or community consent to the sale.

A hard rule worth adopting: never pay a deposit, not even a small one to "hold" the plot, before you have seen the title deed and survey plan with your own eyes. Deposits are extremely difficult to recover once paid.

The four checks that actually verify ownership

Documents can be forged. What separates a real verification from a document review is that you check the seller's papers against independent records held by someone else.

1. A search at the Land Registry

This is the central check for registered land. A search against the property confirms who the registered owner is, whether the deed the seller showed you matches what is on file, and — critically — whether the land is subject to any mortgage, charge, caveat or pending dispute. A caveat lodged by a third party is a clear signal to stop.

2. A check with Physical Planning

The Department of Physical Planning and Housing holds approved layouts and zoning. This tells you whether the plot exists as a legitimate, approved plot in an approved layout, what it may be used for, and whether it sits on land reserved for a road, a school, a drainage channel or another public purpose. Plots that appear on a seller's sketch but not on the official layout are a serious warning sign.

3. A physical survey and boundary check

Instruct your own licensed surveyor — not the seller's — to visit the plot and confirm that the boundaries on the ground match the survey plan. This is how overlapping plots and encroachment come to light, and it is the step buyers most often skip because it costs money and takes time. It is also the step that most reliably catches a double allocation.

4. Local enquiries at the site

Genuinely useful, and free. Speak to the alkalo, the neighbours and anyone farming or living on the land. Ask who owns it, whether anyone else claims it, and whether there has ever been a dispute. Communities know their own land. A quiet word on site has saved more buyers than any document ever has.

Warning signs that should stop a transaction

  • The seller will not let you copy the documents, or will only show photographs of them.
  • The name on the deed does not match the seller's ID, and the explanation involves an uncle, a nickname or a spelling error.
  • Someone is selling on behalf of an owner without a properly executed and registered power of attorney.
  • Heavy pressure to pay quickly because another buyer is supposedly ready. Urgency is the oldest tool in the box.
  • A price well below the going rate for the area with no plausible explanation.
  • Deceased owner, no probate — the estate must be properly administered before anyone can sell.
  • Family land with one signature — where land is held by a family, one member alone usually cannot sell it.
  • A request for cash with no receipt, or payment to a personal account that is not the seller's.

Who should do the checking

Two professionals, both instructed by you and paid by you:

  • An independent lawyer. Not the seller's lawyer, not the agent's recommended lawyer, and not a lawyer the seller is paying. Conflicts of interest are precisely the problem you are trying to avoid. Your lawyer conducts the registry search, reviews the chain of ownership, drafts the agreement and handles registration.
  • A licensed surveyor. To confirm boundaries on the ground and produce or verify the survey plan.

Expect the legal and search work to take somewhere between two and six weeks depending on the land type and how complete the seller's paperwork is, and to cost a small percentage of the purchase price. Customary land, or land with an incomplete chain of ownership, takes longer. Budget for it as a normal part of the transaction — see the real cost of buying property in The Gambia for the full picture.

Paying safely once the title checks out

Verification and payment structure are two halves of the same protection. Even with a clean title:

  • Pay in stages — a deposit on signing the agreement, the balance on transfer and registration;
  • Pay through your lawyer, against documents, rather than directly to the seller in cash;
  • Keep a written record of every payment, however small;
  • Do not treat the transaction as complete until the deed is registered in your name. Handing over money is not the same as owning land.

Buying from abroad? All of this can be done remotely, but only if you appoint someone you trust. Use an independent lawyer, insist on photographs and video of the site visit and boundary check, and be extremely careful with powers of attorney — grant them narrowly and to someone accountable.

Selling land? Make the listing work harder

Everything above protects a buyer — but a seller who understands it can present a plot far more convincingly. Buyers cannot walk through empty land the way they walk through a house, so these four things do the convincing instead.

  • Pin the exact plot. Drop the map pin precisely on the boundary, not just the nearest village — this generates a Google Plus Code for each edge that buyers can navigate to.
  • Upload a sketch plan. Even a hand-drawn plan with rough dimensions reassures buyers about shape and size before they have seen the plot.
  • Photograph the boundaries. Shoot from all four corners so the edges are obvious, and include the road or access point. Morning or late-afternoon light reads far better than midday glare — a phone on a selfie stick or a short drone clip shows the whole plot better than any single photo.
  • State what is missing, not just what is there. "No road access yet" or "electricity 200m away" reads as honest, not negative — buyers budget around it and trust the rest of your listing more.

What to do if something does not add up

Stop. Do not pay anything further, do not sign, and do not accept a promise that the paperwork will follow. If you have already paid, instruct a lawyer immediately — the practical options are lodging a caveat at the Land Registry to prevent further dealings in the land, and pursuing recovery. Both get harder with time.

It is worth saying plainly: walking away from a plot you liked is not a failure. There is a great deal of land for sale along the Gambian coast, and there will be another one with clean papers.

Reform is coming — but it has not arrived

The Gambia's Land Policy 2026 – 2035 proposes exactly the things that would make this guide shorter: formal recognition of customary tenure through Certificates of Customary Ownership, a transparent process for converting customary land to state land with fair compensation, and the registration and digitalisation of all land in the country. Research underpinning that policy found that roughly a quarter of Gambian adults, urban and rural alike, feel insecure about their tenure — which is the problem in one number.

None of it changes what you should do this year. Until a digital register exists and certificates are actually being issued, verification is still a lawyer, a surveyor, a registry search and a conversation with the alkalo. Treat announcements about digitalisation as a reason to keep your paperwork immaculate: when registration does come, the owner with a registered deed, a licensed survey plan and a clean chain of ownership will convert without difficulty. Everyone else will be arguing.

Part of that reform is aimed squarely at the people who sell you the land. A draft Real Estate Agents Bill would make registration and licensing of estate agents mandatory for the first time, and a separate Rent and Tenancy Bill would regulate agents on the rental side. Both are still drafts. We track what they propose and how far they have got in a separate guide.
