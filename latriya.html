The vibe

Latriya sits on the Sukuta–Jambanjelly road, between Jambur and Jambanjelly, in Kombo North. Since that road was tarred and opened in 2020 it has been a twenty-minute drive from Sukuta rather than an expedition, and that is what put it on buyers' lists. It is a village with a strong mosque-centred civic life — its central mosque dates from 2001 and its annual Gamo has run for two decades — and a health post, nursery and lower basic school that were, almost all of them, built with Dutch charitable funding from 1999 onwards.

It is also, like Mamuda and Jambanjelly, absent from every mapping dataset we checked: no GeoNames record, no OpenStreetMap node, nothing in the national settlement layer. A real village of several hundred compounds that the maps have simply not caught up with.

Latriya has only 4 priced plot listings with a known size — below our bar of 5 for an area-specific rate. The rate above is the band rate for the inland Kombo villages between the Brikama highway and the Sukuta–Jambanjelly road: 13 priced plot listings pooled across Farato, Latriya, Mamuda and Jambanjelly. It tells you what the band asks, not what a specific street asks. The 4 listings we can price in Latriya itself ask a median of D2,644 per m² — too few to call a market, shown here as context, not as a rate.

Every figure on this page is an asking price. No one in The Gambia publishes what property actually sells for — there is no public register of sale prices — so what a seller asks is the only thing that can be measured consistently. Sale prices are generally lower, by an amount that depends entirely on the seller. We have no rent listing we can verify here, so we quote no rental yield for this area either — a yield built on a rent we had to guess would only look like knowledge.

These figures are as honest as we can make them — and still young. Reliable Gambian market data barely exists yet, for anyone; building it is one of the reasons MyKunda exists at all. So we publish what we can measure, print the evidence count next to every figure, and leave a blank rather than defend a number we cannot. A market where buyers and sellers can trust what they read has to start somewhere. Every listing and every verified sale sharpens these figures — and if you know a figure on this page to be wrong, tell us.

What to know before buying at Latriya

Land at Latriya has been the subject of a long-running ownership dispute between a claimant family from neighbouring Jambur and long-settled residents of the village. It has been reported over a twelve-year span: more than fifteen compounds were demolished in March 2014 under a court order, with residents saying they were given no notice and that the matter was at the time still before the courts. The parcel at issue has been described as several hundred metres on a side, and the dispute was still live and unresolved as of May 2026, when the district chief publicly denied allegations about how a land document at Latriya came to be signed.

We are deliberately not naming private individuals here, and we are not taking a side: no court ruling settling ownership appears in any source we could find, and the accounts of the parties contradict each other. What we will say is the practical part. Do not treat any plot at Latriya as having clean title on the strength of a receipt or a village stamp. Establish the chain of title, ask specifically whether the parcel falls inside the disputed area, and get independent legal advice before committing money. Our guide to verifying land title sets out the searches; we can also run an ownership check.

Getting there and what is on the ground

The village is on, or immediately off, the 13.5 km Sukuta–Jambanjelly road — a US$23 million project funded largely by the Islamic Development Bank and inaugurated in March 2020, running from Sukuta through Jabang and Jambur to the Brikama–Sanyang connector at Jambanjelly. The 23 MW Jambur solar power station, in commercial operation since 2024, is about two kilometres away.

Latriya has a health post serving the village and its neighbours, a nursery and a lower basic school, a borehole and three mosques. Almost all of that infrastructure was charitably funded, largely by a Dutch foundation working here since 1999; before that, local reporting describes the village as without adequate water, healthcare, roads or schools. Individual properties advertise mains water and electricity; we have no source for village-wide coverage.

What we have not measured here yet

Latriya is one of five areas we added to MyKunda in August 2026, and its page is deliberately thinner than our older ones. Rather than fill it with plausible numbers, here is what we do not know.

  • Population — no settlement-level census figure we could retrieve.
  • Rents — no rental listing we can verify, so no rent and no yield are published.
  • Village-wide mains water and electricity coverage.
  • An exact position. Latriya is in no gazetteer; the pin is an estimate on the road between Jambur and Jambanjelly, accurate to roughly 2 km.
  • Whether the ownership dispute has been resolved since May 2026.

Latriya appears in no gazetteer we checked — not GeoNames, not OpenStreetMap, not Wikipedia, not the national settlement layer. Its position is established from press reporting that places it on the Sukuta–Jambanjelly road between Jambur and Jambanjelly; the pin is an estimate accurate to roughly 2 km. It is not the same place as Latrikunda in Serrekunda, about 13 km north.

If you live here, sell here or have bought here, you can correct any of this: tell us what we have wrong. That is how the rest of these pages got better.
