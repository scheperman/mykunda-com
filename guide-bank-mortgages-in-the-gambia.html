Bank mortgages in The Gambia — rates, conditions and eligibility

MK MyKunda Updated 31 August 2026 16 min read

Bank mortgages exist in The Gambia, but the market is very small and almost nothing about it is published. No licensed Gambian bank puts a mortgage rate, a maximum term or a loan-to-value limit on its website, and the Central Bank publishes no average commercial lending rate. This guide sets out the figures that are published, who publishes them, and the legal rule — the Minister's written consent — that decides whether a lender can take security over your property at all.

In short

  • The Central Bank of The Gambia's Monetary Policy Rate is 14%, cut from 16% at the February 2026 meeting and held on 21 May and again on 20 August 2026, with the standing lending facility at 15%.
  • No licensed Gambian bank publishes a mortgage rate sheet, and the Central Bank publishes no average lending rate. The only published figures come from the Africa Housing Finance Yearbook and international data: a prevailing range of 9–17%, an average of 19.5%, a commercial lending rate of 22%, a maximum term of 20 years, a deposit of 20–35% and a loan-to-value ceiling of 70%.
  • A state lease cannot be mortgaged without the Minister's written consent, and consent to sell on enforcement is refused where the plot has not been developed. That makes an undeveloped plot effectively unmortgageable, and it is the best-evidenced reason Gambian banks are cautious about lending against land.
  • Home Finance Company Limited, long described as the country's dedicated mortgage lender, went into voluntary liquidation in November 2021 and appears on no Central Bank register.
  • SSHFC offers a fixed rate, has no contribution-history requirement, and allows non-Gambians to buy complete housing units with an additional levy of 20% on the price.
  • Stamp duty on a mortgage is 0.5% of the amount, payable by the mortgagee, under the 2019 amendment to the Stamp Act schedule.

Does mortgage finance exist in The Gambia?

It does, but on a scale that will surprise anyone arriving from a European or North American market. The Africa Housing Finance Yearbook, compiled by the Centre for Affordable Housing Finance in Africa (CAHF), records just two residential mortgage providers in The Gambia and a ratio of mortgages to GDP of 0.04% in 2021. Nearly every property transaction in the country is a cash purchase or an instalment arrangement with a developer.

Who the lenders are

  • Commercial banks. The Central Bank of The Gambia's register listed 11 licensed commercial banks when we checked it on 31 August 2026: Access Bank (Gambia), Agib Bank Gambia, BSIC, Bloom Bank Africa (Gambia), Ecobank (Gambia), First Bank Gambia, Guaranty Trust Bank (Gambia), Mega Bank (Gambia), Trust Bank, Vista Bank (Gambia) and Zenith Bank Gambia. Standard Chartered no longer operates here: Access Bank Gambia announced completion of its acquisition of the Standard Chartered business on 30 June 2025.
  • Ecobank Gambia publishes a mortgage calculator under its personal lending pages, which is evidence that a mortgage product exists. It publishes no rate, term or loan-to-value figure alongside it.
  • Access Bank Gambia's personal banking menu lists Diaspora Banking and Diaspora Savings — deposit accounts, with no lending product attached to them.
  • AGIB Bank is a licensed commercial bank offering Sharia-compliant Real Estate Financing. See Sharia-compliant home finance below.
  • SSHFC, the Social Security and Housing Finance Corporation, established by an Act of Parliament in 1981, allocates serviced plots and sells completed housing units on a fixed-rate mortgage. See SSHFC — what it actually offers.

Home Finance Company is no longer a lender

Older guides — including an earlier version of this one — describe Home Finance Company Limited (HFC) as The Gambia's dedicated mortgage lender. That is no longer true, and has not been for some years. CAHF records that HFC, "unprofitable for a good part of its business life, went into voluntary liquidation in November 2021". It does not appear on the Central Bank's List of Licensed Banks, nor on its List of Licensed Microfinance Institutions. CAHF adds, without a date and in hedged terms, that HFC's "functions appear to have been taken over by the Trust Bank"; Trust Bank's own website serves no readable text to anything other than a browser, so we cannot confirm what, if anything, that means for a borrower today. Do not plan a purchase around HFC, and treat any agent or seller who still names it as a source of finance as out of date.

What we could not verify

How many residential mortgages are actually outstanding in The Gambia. The only published figures are CAHF's: 7,000 mortgages outstanding with a total value of US$0.76 million in 2021. Those two numbers cannot both be right — together they imply about US$108 per loan. We report them because they are the only figures anyone publishes, not because we believe the pair. No Gambian institution publishes a reliable count.

What a Gambian mortgage costs — and what is actually published

Start with the price of money. The Central Bank of The Gambia's Monetary Policy Rate is 14%. It was cut from 16% at the Monetary Policy Committee meeting of 25–26 February 2026 and has been maintained since, on 21 May 2026 and again on 20 August 2026. The standing lending facility is 15% (the policy rate plus one percentage point), the standing deposit facility is 5%, and the required reserve ratio for commercial banks is 13%.

Inflation averaged 7.6% across 2025 on the Central Bank's own measure, in its Monetary Policy Report of February 2026; the African Development Bank, updating on 16 June 2026, gives 7.5% for the same year. Through 2026 the monthly headline rate has moved 6.4% in January, 7.0% in April, 7.5% in May, 7.6% in June and 7.0% in July. The Central Bank projects around 5.8% for 2026 and a 5% medium-term target for 2027, but its August 2026 statement still cites persistent inflationary pressures — which is why the policy rate has not moved since February.

Those figures disagree with each other, and that is worth saying rather than smoothing over. A 9–17% "prevailing" range and a 19.5% average cannot both describe the same book of loans, and the 22% lending rate in international data is a whole-market average across all commercial credit, not a mortgage rate. Read the table as the outer edges of what anyone has published, not as a quotation you can hold a bank to.

What we could not verify

Any actual mortgage rate offered by a licensed Gambian bank. We checked the public websites of the banks on the Central Bank's register. None publishes a mortgage interest rate, a maximum tenor or a loan-to-value limit. The Central Bank publishes a policy rate, the standing facilities and the reserve ratio, but no average commercial lending rate. Earlier versions of this guide quoted "15–22%, 10–15 years, 60–70% loan-to-value, 30–40% deposit" — that table could not be traced to any Gambian bank or to the Central Bank, and we have removed it. Anyone quoting you a Gambian mortgage rate, including us, is either passing on CAHF's numbers or guessing. Get the rate, the term, the fees and the rate-reset mechanism in writing from the lender before you rely on any of it.

What high rates actually do to a loan

The arithmetic matters more than the headline. A loan of €100,000 at 18% over 15 years costs about €1,610 a month and roughly €290,000 in total repayments — 2.9 times the amount borrowed. The same €100,000 borrowed in Europe at 3–5% over the same 15-year term would cost between about 1.24 and 1.42 times the amount borrowed.

That like-for-like comparison flatters the European loan, because European mortgages are not usually 15-year loans. Over a standard 25 or 30-year term, €100,000 at 3–5% costs between about 1.42 and 1.93 times the amount borrowed. If you are comparing a Gambian loan with one at home, hold the term constant or the comparison is meaningless.

A Gambian mortgage is not a cheaper version of a European one. At the rates that are published, what looks like a manageable monthly payment can nearly triple the cost of the property over the life of the loan.

The Minister's consent — the rule that decides whether a bank can lend

This is the most important legal fact about borrowing against Gambian property, and it is missing from almost everything written on the subject. It is law, not practice, and it is in the primary text.

Most coastal and near-coastal land in The Gambia is state leasehold. The specimen lease at Schedule III of the State Lands Regulations 1995 (Legal Notice No. 13 of 1995, made under section 30 of the State Lands Act, Act No. 1 of 1991) demises land "for the term of NINETY-NINE years", with the Minister empowered to grant a further 99-year term at expiry "provided there shall be no subsisting breach of any of the lessee's obligations". Renewal is therefore discretionary, not automatic as of right.

Two covenants in regulation 22 govern finance:

  • Regulation 22(k) — the lessee "shall not mortgage or create any charge or encumbrance on the premises without the consent of the Minister in writing first obtained". Every state lease carries this covenant. A mortgage granted without that consent is granted in breach of the lease.
  • Regulation 22(l) — the lessee shall not "subdivide, convey, assign or otherwise alienate the premises… without the consent of the Minister in writing first obtained and such consent shall not be granted if the lessee has not developed the demised premises in accordance with the covenants in that behalf".

Read those two together and the consequence is stark. A lender's security is only worth what it can sell on default. Selling on enforcement is an assignment, and an assignment needs consent under 22(l) — consent that is withheld until the development covenants have been met. So security over an undeveloped state-leased plot is close to worthless to a bank, however good the title looks.

The practical rule: an undeveloped plot is effectively unmortgageable in The Gambia. Not because banks dislike land, but because the consent a lender would need in order to sell it after a default cannot be obtained until the plot has been built on. If you are buying land and hoping to borrow against it, plan on financing the purchase and the build another way.

Regulation 22(g) adds that no development may be carried out without a development permit under the Physical Planning and Development Control Act 1990. So the build that unlocks assignability itself needs a permit first. Our guide to building a house in The Gambia covers that sequence.

The World Bank's Doing Business 2020 exercise, on data collected in May 2019, recorded "Application for Ministerial Consent to transfer" as a formal procedure taking 60 days and GMD 750 — the longest single step in a Gambian conveyance. Treat that as a 2019 measurement rather than a current fee or timetable.

What we could not verify

How Ministerial consent works in practice today: who lodges the application when a bank is taking security, how long it currently takes, and what it costs. The State Lands Regulations 1995 require the consent but set out no timetable and no fee for it, and the Ministry of Lands publishes neither. The only figures we could source are the World Bank's 2019 measurements above. Ask your lawyer to build the consent step into the transaction timetable explicitly, with a longstop date.

How much you can borrow — the one-third-of-salary rule

The one affordability constraint anyone publishes is a deduction cap, not an income multiple. CAHF reports Gambian labour rules preventing banks from deducting more than a third of an employee's salary for a mortgage repayment. For a salaried borrower repaying by deduction at source, that is the binding limit on the loan you can carry, and it bites harder than any loan-to-value ceiling at rates near 20%.

Work it backwards before you look at properties. At a 19.5% rate over a 20-year term — CAHF's average rate and CAHF's maximum tenor — a monthly repayment of one third of your salary supports a loan of roughly five times your annual salary. At the same rate over 10 years it supports roughly three and a half times. Add the deposit of 20–35% that CAHF records mortgage providers and developers requiring, and the purchase price you can reach is set by the deposit you have saved rather than by what a bank will lend.

What we could not verify

Any lender's own affordability policy, income multiple, minimum income, maximum age at maturity or credit-scoring rule. None is published. Nor could we source an arrangement fee, valuation fee or legal fee schedule from any Gambian lender. The one-third deduction cap above is CAHF's reporting of Gambian labour rules, not a rule we have read in a statute.

Sharia-compliant home finance

The Gambia is a majority-Muslim country and Sharia-compliant home finance is a real part of the market, so a guide to housing finance that only covers interest-bearing mortgages is incomplete.

AGIB Bank (Arab Gambian Islamic Bank) is on the Central Bank's register of licensed commercial banks and describes itself as "The first Islamic Bank In the Gambia". Its published product set includes Real Estate Financing, alongside Murabaha (cost-plus sale), Ijarah (Islamic leasing) and Salam & Istisna (forward purchase and construction financing). Istisna in particular is the structure normally used to finance a building under construction rather than a completed house.

The Central Bank's List of Licensed Microfinance Institutions additionally includes Islamic finance companies — APS Islamic Microfinance Ltd, Salam Financial Services Ltd and Yonna Islamic Microfinance Ltd — licensed in the "Finance Company" category. These are smaller institutions than the banks, and their lending limits are correspondingly smaller.

Two things to hold in mind. First, no Islamic institution in The Gambia publishes a profit rate, a maximum term or a financing-to-value limit either — the disclosure gap is the same as for conventional lenders. Second, the security position is identical: a Murabaha or Ijarah over a state lease still needs the Minister's written consent under regulation 22(k), and the same development covenant still governs any onward transfer.

Can foreigners get a bank mortgage in The Gambia?

The honest answer is that nobody publishes a policy either way, and you should not accept a confident answer in either direction — including a confident "no".

What can be established:

  • No licensed Gambian bank publishes a residency requirement for a mortgage, because none publishes mortgage terms at all. There is no published rule excluding a non-resident, and no published product admitting one.
  • The historic Home Finance Company product did lend to non-residents, on a shorter term — a maximum of 20 years overall, 15 years for resident Gambians and 10 years for non-residents. HFC has been in liquidation since November 2021, so this is history, not an offer.
  • SSHFC allows non-Gambians to buy complete housing units outright, with an additional levy of 20% on the price. That is a purchase route, not a loan.
  • Access Bank Gambia markets Diaspora Banking and Diaspora Savings. Both are deposit accounts. Neither carries a lending product.
  • CAHF records that "most property purchases are made by citizens in the diaspora" — and the finance for those purchases is overwhelmingly not Gambian bank debt.

Beyond disclosure, three structural obstacles are well evidenced:

  • Security, not income, is the binding constraint. The Ministerial consent regime above explains lender caution about land better than any story about foreign income being hard to assess.
  • Title. The World Bank's Gambia Financial Sector Assessment of June 2022 found that the "poor state of land titling, interplay of various laws, and the complexity of customary ownership (especially outside the Banjul area) further complicate lending against property". Customary land and deemed leasehold land cannot be registered at the Deeds Registry at all, which makes them very difficult security. Our guide to freehold, leasehold and customary land explains which is which.
  • Currency mismatch. If you earn in euros or pounds and borrow in dalasi, you carry the exchange-rate risk in full. The Central Bank's February 2026 report records the dalasi falling 11.53% against the euro and 4.36% against the dollar over the preceding year.

Non-Gambian buyers also face a separate set of land-holding rules that have nothing to do with finance. Those are covered in our guide to buying property in The Gambia as a foreigner, and you should read them before you approach a lender.

SSHFC — what it actually offers

The Social Security and Housing Finance Corporation was established by an Act of Parliament in 1981. It allocates serviced plots and sells completed housing units, financed on its own mortgage. The World Bank's June 2022 assessment put SSHFC's assets at 6.3 billion dalasi, about 6% of GDP, which makes it a substantial institution by Gambian standards.

Two things commonly written about SSHFC are wrong, and we published both of them in an earlier version of this guide.

  • There is no contribution-history requirement. SSHFC's own Mortgage Process page lists no requirement to be an active contributor to the social security scheme. We have removed that claim.
  • Non-Gambians are not excluded. SSHFC states plainly: "Non-Gambian nationals can purchase complete housing units only with an additional levy of 20% on the price." Outright purchasers of a house or serviced plot are also exempt from the first three conditions below.

SSHFC's published conditions for an allocation are:

  • proof of Gambian nationality;
  • that you do not own another landed property in the Greater Banjul area, or in Brikama and its immediate surroundings;
  • documentary proof of your ability to meet the monthly repayment;
  • that you build on the plot a residential dwelling house fit for immediate occupation within three years of allocation;
  • that you submit architectural plans for approval.

Three further points matter to a borrower. SSHFC offers a fixed rate mortgage — it says so on its own page, which makes it the only Gambian housing lender whose rate type is published, and the only reason to be sceptical of any blanket claim that all Gambian housing finance is variable. Its historic KESS loans ran at 9% over a 25-year repayment period, which shows what the institution has done in the past but is not an offer today. And on full repayment, "the plot will be assigned to you for all residue of the term vested in the Corporation" — you receive a sub-lease, not a freehold.

Note also what the SSHFC model is: plot allocation plus a covenant to build within three years. That is closer to a self-build facility than to a purchase mortgage, and it sits alongside the outright sale of completed units.

What we could not verify

SSHFC's current fixed rate, its maximum term, its deposit requirement and the size of the levy base for non-Gambian purchasers beyond the stated 20%. None is published. SSHFC's application form asks for personal details, residential status, bank details, employment information, plot or house ownership and a declaration, plus two passport photographs and a birth certificate or certified national identity document — but the corporation publishes no decision timetable.

How buyers actually finance a Gambian purchase

With two mortgage providers and a mortgage book worth 0.04% of GDP, local debt is not how most purchases happen. The realistic routes are these.

1. Cash

By far the most common. It avoids the interest cost entirely, avoids the consent problem on the mortgage side, and gives you negotiating power. It does not avoid the need for the checks: read our title verification guide before any money moves, and move the money through a licensed commercial bank so there is a paper trail — see sending money to The Gambia.

2. Borrowing at home against a property you already own

Many diaspora buyers remortgage or release equity on a home in Europe or the UK and buy for cash here. You borrow in the currency you earn in, at whatever rate your own market offers, and you avoid the Gambian consent regime altogether because the security sits abroad. Rates in your own market change; check them where you live rather than relying on a range quoted in a Gambian guide.

3. An unsecured personal loan abroad

For a plot or a small compound, an unsecured loan from your own bank may cover it. It is dearer than a mortgage at home, but it is not secured on the Gambian property, so none of the consent problems arise.

4. Developer instalments

Widely used, and the route with the least legal protection. The Gambia has no statutory regulation of off-plan sales, no developer licensing, no mandatory escrow and no buyer-deposit protection. If you go this way, register the sale contract at the Deeds Registry and schedule every milestone with dates and remedies. Read developer financing in The Gambia in full first.

5. Sharia-compliant financing

AGIB Bank and the licensed Islamic microfinance institutions, as above. Terms are not published; ask directly.

6. Family pooling

Common and workable, and the source of a great many later disputes. Document the shares in writing at the outset, decide in advance who may sell and on what terms, and read our guide to inheritance and wills for Gambian property — co-owned family land is where inheritance rules bite hardest.

Fees, stamp duty and what a mortgage adds to your costs

A mortgage does not just add interest; it adds instruments, and instruments attract duty. The Stamp Act, Cap. 82:01, as amended by the Stamp Act (Amendment of Schedule) Order 2019 (Legal Notice No. 18 of 2019, Gazette No. 34 of 4 October 2019), sets the rates below.

InstrumentStamp dutyPrimarily liable
Mortgage0.5% of the amountThe mortgagee
Further charges0.5% of the amountThe chargee
Memorandum of deposit0.5% of the amountThe depositor
Assignment or conveyance (the purchase itself)2% of the amountThe assignee or transferee — the buyer
Guarantee0.75%—

The duty on the mortgage is nominally the mortgagee's, but expect a lender to pass it on. Section 29 of the Stamp Act means no deed may be registered unless it is duly stamped, and section 17 makes an unstamped instrument inadmissible in evidence — so this is not a fee anyone can skip.

On top of that sit the transaction costs that apply whether or not you borrow. Regulation 41 of the State Lands Regulations 1995 sets the charge for preparing a lease at one hundred dalasi, excluding survey fees, stamp duty and the registration fee; the premium, survey fee and stamp duty are payable separately, and an allocation lapses if payment is not made within 90 days. The World Bank's 2019 measurements — again, 2019, not current — put deed registration at 5 days and GMD 1,500, a survey plan at GMD 2,750, and the whole transfer at 6 procedures, about 73 to 74 days and 7.8% of property value. Our costs of buying guide sets these out in full.

Note one item that affects a mortgaged purchase in particular: capital gains tax is the seller's, but it is payable before the deed can be registered. For individuals it is the higher of 15% of the gain or 5% of the consideration; for companies, partnerships and trustees the higher of 25% of the gain or 10% of the consideration. If a seller has not budgeted for it, your registration — and therefore your lender's security — stalls.

What we could not verify

Lender-side fees. No Gambian bank publishes an arrangement fee, a valuation fee, a legal fee, an early-repayment charge or a fee for the Ministerial consent application. Nor does any lender publish a decision or drawdown timetable. Ask for a written fee schedule before you sign anything, and ask specifically who pays for the consent application and what happens to your fees if consent is refused.

Selling to a buyer who needs a mortgage

If you are the seller, a buyer's finance is your problem too — because a purchase that collapses at the lender's stage costs you months. Three questions come up repeatedly.

Does an undeveloped plot block my buyer's mortgage?

In practice, yes. Under regulation 22(l) the Minister's consent to assign is not granted where the lessee has not developed the premises in accordance with the covenants, and that is exactly the consent a lender would need in order to sell the plot after a default. A bank asked to lend against a bare plot is being asked to take security it may not be able to realise. If you are selling land rather than a house, assume your buyer is a cash buyer, price accordingly, and do not build a timetable around mortgage approval.

Who obtains the Minister's consent, and how long does it take?

The consent under regulation 22(l) is a consent to the lessee's dealing, so it is the seller's lease and the seller's covenant — in practice the application is made by or on behalf of the current lessee, usually through the lawyer handling the conveyance, and the separate consent under 22(k) is needed for the mortgage the buyer is granting. Neither the Regulations nor the Ministry of Lands publishes a timetable or a fee for either. The only measurement we can point to is the World Bank's 2019 figure of 60 days and GMD 750 for a consent to transfer. Start the application early, agree in the contract who pays for it, and set a longstop date rather than a hopeful one.

Should I accept a finance-conditional offer?

Weigh it against the evidence rather than the buyer's confidence. There are two residential mortgage providers in the country, the mortgage book is 0.04% of GDP, and no lender publishes an approval timescale or an approval rate. That is a thin basis for holding a property off the market. If you do accept a conditional offer, make the condition specific — a named lender, a defined loan amount, a dated deadline for a written offer — and keep the right to continue marketing until it is met. A cash offer at a lower price is often worth more than a mortgage-conditional offer at a higher one.

What to perfect before you market

  • Your lease and your ground rent. Renewal at the end of a 99-year term is at the Minister's discretion and depends on there being "no subsisting breach", and ground rent arrears are a breach. Annual ground rent on a state lease is payable to the Department of Lands before 31 January, with forfeiture by re-entry for non-payment.
  • Your development covenants. If the plot is undeveloped, a buyer needing finance is out of reach until it is built on.
  • Your registered chain of deeds. The Deeds Registry is indexed by the names of the parties, not by parcel, so a buyer's lawyer searches names. Gaps in the chain surface as delays. See how to verify land title.
  • Any existing mortgage or caveat. Mortgages and caveats are on the register. A subsisting charge has to be discharged, and the discharge itself is an instrument.
  • Your capital gains tax position. It is payable before the deed can be registered, and a return is due within 15 days of the transaction according to GRA.
What we could not verify

Mortgage approval rates, average approval times, or how often finance-conditional sales complete in The Gambia. No Gambian lender, and no Gambian regulator, publishes any of it. If an agent tells you a typical approval takes a given number of weeks, ask what that figure is based on.

Key conditions for any route

Whichever way you finance the purchase, these hold.

  • Registrable title comes first. No lender, and no sensible cash buyer, should proceed without a title that can actually be registered. Customary land and deemed leasehold land cannot be registered at the Deeds Registry at all, and registration there gives priority rather than title. Read our title verification guide.
  • The Minister's consent is not a formality. Build it into the timetable, in writing, with a longstop date and an agreed answer to what happens if it is refused.
  • Your own lawyer, not the seller's, and not the agent's. Estate agents in The Gambia are not licensed by any authority, and no Gambian rule compels an agent or a lawyer to hold your money in a separate client account. Insist that purchase money goes into a lawyer's client account anyway, and read our guide to estate agent regulation.
  • Budget the whole cost. Stamp duty at 2% on the purchase and 0.5% on the mortgage, the seller's capital gains tax as a completion risk, survey, registration, legal fees and VAT at 15% on services from a VAT-registered supplier.
  • Understand the currency exposure. Borrowing in dalasi while earning abroad puts the whole exchange-rate risk on you.
  • Take Gambian legal advice before you commit. This guide is a starting point, not a substitute for a lawyer who has read your documents.

Questions people actually ask

Can I get a mortgage in The Gambia as a foreign buyer?

Nobody publishes a policy either way. No licensed Gambian bank publishes mortgage terms at all, so there is no published rule excluding a non-resident and no published product admitting one. The historic Home Finance Company product did lend to non-residents on a 10-year maximum term, but that company has been in liquidation since November 2021. Ask a bank directly and get the answer in writing rather than relying on anyone's confident yes or no.

What interest rate will I pay on a Gambian mortgage?

No Gambian bank publishes one. The only published figures are CAHF's prevailing range of 9–17%, sourced to the Gambia Competition and Consumer Protection Commission, and CAHF's average of 19.5%. World Bank and IMF data record a commercial bank lending rate of 22% for 2024 and 2025, but that covers all lending, not mortgages. The Central Bank's policy rate is 14%, held on 20 August 2026.

Can a bank take security over my plot at all?

Only with the Minister's written consent. Regulation 22(k) of the State Lands Regulations 1995 is a covenant in every state lease: the lessee shall not mortgage or create any charge or encumbrance on the premises without the consent of the Minister in writing first obtained.

Is an undeveloped plot mortgageable?

Effectively no. Regulation 22(l) provides that consent to assign shall not be granted if the lessee has not developed the premises in accordance with the covenants. Since a lender needs to be able to assign in order to sell after a default, security over a bare plot is very weak, and banks price and refuse accordingly.

How long is a Gambian state lease, and can it be renewed?

The specimen lease at Schedule III of the State Lands Regulations 1995 runs for 99 years. At expiry the Minister may grant a further 99-year term, provided there is no subsisting breach of the lessee's obligations. Renewal is discretionary, not a right — so ground rent arrears and unmet development covenants matter.

How much of my salary can go on mortgage repayments?

CAHF reports Gambian labour rules preventing banks from deducting more than a third of an employee's salary for a mortgage repayment. That deduction cap, rather than any published income multiple, is the practical limit on what a salaried borrower can carry.

Is there a Sharia-compliant way to finance a home?

Yes. AGIB Bank, a licensed commercial bank, publishes Real Estate Financing alongside Murabaha, Ijarah, and Salam and Istisna. The Central Bank also licenses APS Islamic Microfinance, Salam Financial Services and Yonna Islamic Microfinance. None publishes a profit rate, and the Ministerial consent rule applies to Islamic security exactly as it does to a conventional mortgage.

Are Gambian mortgage rates fixed or variable?

SSHFC states on its own site that it offers a fixed rate mortgage. No commercial bank publishes its rate type, so any blanket claim that all Gambian housing finance is variable is unsupported. Ask the lender what the rate is, what it is linked to, and how often it can be reset.

What does a mortgage cost in stamp duty?

0.5% of the amount, under the Stamp Act as amended by the Stamp Act (Amendment of Schedule) Order 2019. The mortgagee is the person primarily liable, though expect a lender to pass it on. The purchase deed itself carries 2%, payable by the buyer.

What documents will a Gambian lender want?

No bank publishes a list. SSHFC's application asks for personal details, residential status, bank details, employment information, plot or house ownership and a declaration, plus two passport photographs and a birth certificate or certified national identity document. Expect any lender to want proof of income, the lease, the survey plan, the registered chain of deeds and evidence that ground rent is paid up.

How long does mortgage approval take?

No Gambian lender publishes a timescale. For context on the surrounding steps, the World Bank measured property registration at six procedures and about 73 to 74 days in 2019, including 60 days for a Ministerial consent to transfer. Those are 2019 measurements, not current service levels.

Can I borrow to build rather than to buy?

SSHFC's model is closest to it: an allocation of a serviced plot with a covenant to build a dwelling fit for occupation within three years. On the Islamic side, Istisna is the structure designed for construction financing and AGIB publishes it. No conventional Gambian bank publishes a self-build or construction product.

Is there anything aimed at diaspora buyers?

Access Bank Gambia markets Diaspora Banking and Diaspora Savings, but both are deposit accounts with no lending attached. CAHF records that most property purchases here are made by citizens in the diaspora — financed from abroad, not by Gambian mortgage debt.

As a seller, should I accept an offer that depends on a mortgage?

Be cautious. There are two residential mortgage providers in the country, the mortgage book is 0.04% of GDP, and no lender publishes an approval rate or timescale. If you accept, make the condition specific — a named lender, a defined amount and a dated deadline for a written offer — and keep marketing until it is met.
