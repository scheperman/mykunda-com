How we measure these prices

A price figure for a country with no public register of sale prices has to say exactly what it is made of. This is that statement: the sources, what each one is worth, what gets thrown away, how much evidence sits behind each area, and where the number stops being useful.

This page describes how the figures on the price table and the area guides are made. The headline figure is the asking price of land, per square metre, in Gambian dalasi, for each of the 52 areas we cover. Land is measured on its own, separately from buildings, because in The Gambia land and buildings are usually sold separately — and because land is what most of the market actually trades.

Last measured 25 August 2026. Rates used to bring foreign-currency listings into dalasi: D72.70 per US dollar, D85.74 per euro (Central Bank of The Gambia, 25 August 2026). Every amount on this site is stored and shown in dalasi; other currencies are converted at the live rate on the day you look.

Where the data stands today — and why we publish anyway

Plainly: for most of The Gambia, reliable market data does not yet exist — not from the state, not from the banks, and not from us. What we hold is a young evidence base of asking prices that grows month by month. We would rather say that than dress it up. Every figure on this site is our best measurement of a thin market, printed with the evidence behind it, and thin evidence can be wrong — which is why a figure here never hides its count.

We publish anyway, because the alternative is worse. An opaque market favours whoever already knows more than you, and that is precisely what has held Gambian property back. MyKunda was built to help develop a professional property market in The Gambia — one where buyers and sellers can trust what they read — and that trust has to begin with honesty about how much is known. It grows with every listing, every verified sale and every correction that reaches us: figures earn trust the way people do. If you can add to the evidence — a price actually paid, a listing, a figure here you know to be wrong — tell us.

What the figures are built from

Every figure comes from advertised asking prices that we can read a size out of. For the August 2026 measurement that means 95 priced plot listings with a stated plot dimension, 72 house listings and 45 rental listings whose period and property type we can actually read, drawn from:

Facebook Marketplace & groupsThe private local market, in dalasi — 1,102 listings screened, 918 usableBy far the densest source, and the only one that reaches ordinary Gambian sellers. Prices are often typed carelessly (D650 for a plot of D650,000), so amounts are rescaled against a plausible range and anything still ambiguous is dropped.

Where a size is given as a dimension — and in The Gambia it nearly always is, as 20 × 20 or 20 × 25 metres — the price per square metre is our own arithmetic. Almost nobody in the country advertises a price per square metre.

Every figure says how much evidence sits behind it

This is the part that matters most. An area with fourteen plot listings and an area with none cannot carry the same kind of number, so we do not pretend they do. Each area falls into one of four classes, and the class is printed next to the figure:

Measured — five or more local plot listings

The figure is the median of what is actually advertised in that area, with a range around it. Twelve of the 52 areas are in this class today, and they are almost all in the Kombos and along the coast, because that is where the advertising is. Until 27 August 2026 the bar was three; it is now five, because a median of three is still at the mercy of one unusual seller.

Band rate — fewer than five listings of its own

The area shows the rate of the surrounding band instead: the median over all plot listings pooled across a named group of neighbouring areas, with the pool size printed next to it. The area’s own listings, if any, appear in the text as individual asking prices — one plot is not a market, it is a single seller’s opinion of their own land, and since 27 August 2026 it can no longer set an area figure on this site. That rule exists because it briefly did: one advertisement put Bakoteh above Kololi.

Few observations — upcountry only

One upcountry town currently carries its own single observation, marked as such, because upcountry there is no evidenced band to fall back on. Read it as an indication, nothing more.

Derived — upcountry, no listings of our own

The town gets the provincial rate, scaled from the areas that do have evidence. It is marked as derived wherever it appears and should be read as an order of magnitude, nothing more.

Rent has no derived class at all

Land can be scaled from a neighbouring area because land prices move with location in a way we can see. Rents do not: across the areas where we hold rental listings, the local rent and the local land rate turn out to have almost no relationship, so a rent scaled from next door would be a number with nothing behind it. A rent is therefore published only where rental listings for that area exist — 13 of the 52 areas today — and the other 39 pages say plainly that we have none. Until 27 August 2026 those pages carried a rent anyway, calculated as 2% of the asking price of a house in the same area. That was wrong, and it is gone.

What gets thrown away

Anything without a usable size

Without square metres there is no price per square metre. Listings that give only a total price still count towards the plot and house figures, but never towards the rate per m².

Prices that are not prices

A striking number of listings carry a phone number in the price field, or a placeholder of D1. Anything whose price matches a phone number in its own text is dropped, and anything that lands outside a plausible range after rescaling is dropped too. Duplicate postings — the same plot offered by several people, which is common here — are excluded on the flag the source carries.

The two rental markets are never averaged together

A three-bedroom house in Fajara genuinely rents for around D175,000 a year unfurnished on the local market, and for €18,000 to €38,000 a year furnished to a foreign tenant. That is a factor of nine on the same street. A single “average rent” would be wrong for both, so the figure we publish is the local, unfurnished rent — the market most readers are in.

Two other things are separated out for the same reason. A single room or a room-and-parlour is not a small house, so the rent we publish is for a home of two or three bedrooms and nothing else is counted towards it. And an advertisement whose period we cannot read is left out rather than guessed: Facebook shows only the first hundred characters or so, and a Gambian rent quoted as “D45,000” can mean a month, six months or a year. Roughly one rental listing in six is dropped on that rule alone.

Official statistics are context, never input

Inflation, the policy rate and the dalasi exchange rate are tracked alongside the figures but never priced into them. They are what you read the figures against: with inflation at 7.0% (Gambia Bureau of Statistics, July 2026), a price rising 5% a year is falling in real terms.

How often it is updated

The listing sources are read daily; the published figures are recalculated monthly and every one of them carries the date it was measured. A recalculation is reviewed by a person before it goes live — an area figure that jumps because three plots came onto the market is worse than no change at all. Where an area has no new evidence in a month, its figure stays where it is and its evidence class is what tells you so.

What this is not

These are not transaction prices, and there is no way to make them so

The Gambia has no public register of sale prices. Deeds are registered at the Registrar General’s Department and conveyances are assessed for stamp duty, so the state sees prices — but nothing is published, and the Centre for Affordable Housing Finance in Africa states plainly that there is no centralised source for house prices or rents in the country. So every figure here is what was asked, consistently measured. What is paid is generally less, by an amount that depends entirely on the seller.

It is not a valuation

An area rate cannot value a property. Title, access, water, power, boundaries and the state of the building move a real price far more than the area does, and none of them are in this number. Use it to sanity-check an asking price and to compare areas — not to decide what a specific plot is worth. For a figure on one property, use the valuation tool, which prices land and building separately and tells you how confident it is; for the papers, Ownership Verification.

Yields here are low, and we would rather say so

Across the areas where we hold both an asking price and a rent we can verify, gross rental yields run from about 1.5% to 4.7% — below the 9.45% a one-year Gambian treasury bill paid in August 2026, and below the only published outside estimate for the country. Buying property here is mostly a bet on land, not an income strategy. Anyone quoting you a double-digit rental yield for The Gambia should be asked where the number comes from.

Until 27 August 2026 this page said the yield was “roughly 2%”. That was not a measurement. The rent published on most area pages was itself calculated as 2% of the asking price on the same page, so the yield we reported back was the assumption we had put in. The rents have since been rebuilt from rental listings only, and the yield is now what falls out of two separately measured numbers — which is why it is no longer a single figure.

It is not an official register

MyKunda is a property platform, not a statistics office. This is our own measurement, published so it can be checked and argued with. It carries no official standing, and where a figure elsewhere on this site comes from an outside body, that body is named on the page.

What we do not know

Coverage is honest but uneven, and it is worth being blunt about where it runs out. There is almost no advertising upcountry, so for Basse, Soma, Janjanbureh, Bansang, Kuntaur, Gambissara, Fatoto, Barra, Essau and Kerewan we publish land only, on a derived regional rate, and no house or rent figure at all. Built floor area is published by exactly one portal, so a reliable price per built square metre exists only for parts of the coast. Most listings carry no date, so “current” means current as at the date of measurement and nothing stronger. And the whole dataset is asking prices from a market where the same plot is sometimes sold twice — check the papers yourself, every time.

Who is responsible for it

These figures and the written guides are produced and checked by MyKunda, with Edwin Scheperman responsible for what is published. If a figure looks wrong to you, tell us — with the listing or the area, if you can. Corrections are made in the next monthly recalculation, or immediately if something is plainly broken. Who does what is set out on the team page.
